## What the survey teaches

A history of philosophy can look like a graveyard of refuted systems: Thales was wrong about water, Descartes about the pineal gland, the logical positivists about meaning. A student finishing a survey may reasonably ask what, if anything, has been learned. Here are six lessons the whole history teaches, more durable than any single doctrine.

**1. Positions come with costs.** Every serious view about the big questions buys its advantages at a price. Physicalism about the mind ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)) fits science, but has trouble with consciousness; dualism keeps consciousness, but has trouble with causation. Compatibilism about free will keeps responsibility, at the cost of a thinner notion of "could have done otherwise"; libertarianism keeps the thicker notion, at the cost of mysterious causation. Utilitarianism ([Chapter 16](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md)) gives a clear decision procedure, at the cost of permitting what feels monstrous in some cases; Kant ([Chapter 13](13-kant.md)) forbids the monstrous, at the cost of rigidity. David Lewis made the point in terms of prices: the philosopher's job is not to find the view with no costs but to measure the costs and decide which you are willing to pay. Mature philosophical belief is belief that knows its own price.

**2. Arguments outlast conclusions.** Zeno's paradoxes ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)), the Euthyphro dilemma ([Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md)), Descartes' dream argument ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)), Hume's problem of induction ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)), the Gettier cases and Mary's room: these are still taught not because anyone believes their original authors' conclusions but because the arguments set problems that every later view must answer. Learning philosophy is largely learning a repertoire of such arguments and what they demand.

**3. Problems have histories.** The mind-body problem is not timeless. In its modern form it arose when the new science of the seventeenth century ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)) described matter as mere extension in motion, leaving color, feeling and thought with nowhere to go. Knowing where a problem came from sometimes shows that it rested on an assumption you can drop. That was the pragmatists' hope ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md)), Wittgenstein's ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)) and the genealogists' ([Chapter 23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md)).

**4. Philosophy gives birth to sciences.** Physics, biology, psychology, linguistics and economics all grew out of philosophy (and logic, the foundation of computer science, was born there); each became independent once it found methods for settling its questions. This is one reason philosophy seems not to progress: whenever it does, the result gets a new name. What remains are the questions no method yet settles.

**5. Disagreement persists, and it is evidence.** The largest survey of professional philosophers, the PhilPapers Survey (2009, repeated in 2020 with 1,785 respondents), shows how much remains open. In 2020, 67 percent of respondents accepted or leaned toward atheism, 59 percent toward compatibilism about free will, 62 percent toward moral realism, and 52 percent toward physicalism about the mind. On normative ethics (where respondents could pick more than one answer) they split three ways: virtue ethics 37 percent, deontology 32, consequentialism 31. David Chalmers, one of the survey's designers, asked in a 2015 paper, "Why Isn't There More Progress in Philosophy?" His answer is that philosophy has made progress, but less convergence than the sciences. That is a reason for humility: if many careful, informed people disagree with you, you may be missing something. It is not a reason for silence, because suspending judgment on every contested question would leave you unable to live.

**6. Traditions converge from different directions.** The Stoic dichotomy of control, the Buddhist analysis of craving ([Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md)), Spinoza's freedom through understanding, Nietzsche's *amor fati* ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md)) and modern cognitive therapy all share a striking claim: much suffering comes not from events but from how we judge and grasp them, and it can be changed by training. Confucius' stress on ritual and habit, Aristotle's habituation into virtue and the pragmatists' account of belief as habit agree on something else: character is built by practice, not by theory alone. When traditions that never met arrive at the same place, it's worth paying attention.

## Four courses, four lenses

This survey has three sibling courses. Each looks at the same human situation through a different lens, and together they cover most of what a philosophy of life needs.

- **The survey** (this course) gives *breadth and history*: the full range of questions and the main answers, in the order in which they arose. Its lesson is perspective. Whatever view you hold, someone has held it before, someone has refuted it, and someone has answered the refutation.
- **[Stoicism](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md)** gives *depth in one complete way of life*: a physics, a logic and an ethics designed to fit together, and a set of practices for living them. Its lesson is that philosophy can be lived, every day, and that the test of a view is partly what it does to the person who holds it. Its last chapter, [Living a Stoic Life](../learn-stoicism/16-living-a-stoic-life.md), faces the standard objections (quietism, emotional suppression, a providential cosmos few now believe in) and shows how to build a practice anyway.
- **[Nihilism](../learn-nihilism/01-what-is-nihilism.md)** gives *the challenge*: the possibility that nothing matters, that values are fictions, that life is absurd. Its lesson is that a philosophy of life must survive its strongest objection. Its last chapter, [Beyond Nihilism](../learn-nihilism/15-beyond-nihilism.md), sets the major responses (Nietzschean, existentialist, absurdist, religious, Stoic, Buddhist) side by side and surveys the contemporary debate about meaning in life.
- **[Ethics](../learn-ethics/01-what-is-ethics.md)** gives *method for action*: the main moral theories, metaethics, applied problems, and the method of reflective equilibrium for building your own considered views. Its last chapter, [Building Your Ethics](../learn-ethics/22-building-your-ethics.md), is a workshop for doing exactly that.

The four lenses correct one another. The survey without practice becomes erudition; Stoicism without the nihilist challenge can become complacent; nihilism without ethics can become an excuse; ethics without history can mistake the prejudices of its moment for timeless truths. The rest of this chapter tries to hold them together.

## What a philosophy of life needs

The ancient schools offered complete packages. To become an Epicurean was to accept an atomist physics, an empiricist theory of knowledge, a hedonist ethics, a view of death and a community of friends with shared practices. Pierre Hadot's thesis, introduced in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-philosophy.md), is that these packages were primarily ways of life, and the theories served the life. Today almost no one joins a school. But the components of a philosophy of life are the same, and you can assemble them deliberately rather than inheriting them by accident. There are five.

### 1. A picture of the world and of your knowledge of it

You need a working view of what kind of world you live in. Is there a God? Is the mind physical? Is there free will? Is the universe indifferent to us? You don't need certainty, and you shouldn't expect it. You need a view that fits the evidence as you understand it, held with the degree of confidence that evidence warrants, and open to revision. That is fallibilism, the common lesson of Peirce, Popper and the post-Gettier epistemologists ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md), [Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

Two cautions. First, your worldview constrains your other commitments less than you might fear. The Stoics built their ethics on a providential cosmos, but modern Stoics practice the same ethics in a naturalistic one. Religious and secular people often converge on an ethics and disagree about its foundation. Second, the worldview question is not idle. Whether death is the end, whether there is a cosmic purpose, and whether other animals are conscious all affect what you should do.

### 2. An account of what is good for a person

What makes a life go well for the person living it? This is the question of **well-being** or **prudential value**, distinct from what makes a life morally good. Derek Parfit's classification of the main theories, from an appendix to *Reasons and Persons* (1984), is now standard.

- **Hedonism.** A life goes well to the extent that it contains pleasure and lacks pain. This was the view of Epicurus ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) and Bentham. The standard objection is Robert Nozick's **experience machine** (1974): if a machine could give you any experiences you liked, forever, while you floated in a tank, would you plug in? Most people say no. If so, we care about more than experiences: about actually doing things, being a certain kind of person, and being in contact with reality.
- **Desire satisfaction.** A life goes well to the extent that your desires are satisfied. This fits a modern liberal respect for individual difference, but desires can be ill-informed (you want the glass that contains poison), manipulated, or adapted to bad circumstances (the oppressed may learn not to want what they can't have). Refining the theory to count only informed or idealized desires helps, but it moves the theory toward the next one.
- **Objective list.** Some things are good for you whether or not you want them: knowledge, friendship, achievement, health, autonomy, perhaps pleasure too. This is the view of Aristotle ([Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md)) and of contemporary Aristotelians such as Martha Nussbaum, whose "capabilities" list has influenced development policy. The objection is that it seems paternalistic: who decides what goes on the list?

Many philosophers now hold **hybrid** views: a life goes best when you take pleasure in, or care about, things that are actually worthwhile. Aristotle gave this view its canonical form. He also added that a good life is an achievement of a whole life, not a moment:

> ...for as it is not one swallow or one fine day that makes a spring, so it is not one day or a short time that makes a man blessed and happy.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* I.7 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Aristotle prefaces the sentence with a Greek phrase meaning "in a complete life". Bertrand Russell, writing in 1925 for a popular audience, compressed his own hybrid view into a single italicized sentence:

> I cannot, therefore, prove that my view of the good life is right; I can only state my view, and hope that as many as possible will agree. My view is this: *The good life is one inspired by love and guided by knowledge.*
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *What I Believe*, Chapter II

Notice the honesty of the first sentence. Russell does not claim a proof. He thought ultimate values could be stated, clarified and defended against confusion, but not demonstrated. His later *The Conquest of Happiness* (1930) is a practical book, unusual for a major philosopher, and its central advice turns outward:

> The secret of happiness is this: let your interests be as wide as possible, and let your reactions to the things and persons that interest you be as far as possible friendly rather than hostile.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Conquest of Happiness*, Chapter X

This is close to what psychologists who study well-being report: engagement with the world and warm relationships predict life satisfaction better than wealth beyond a modest level. But a caution applies to the whole "science of happiness". Measures of reported life satisfaction capture one thing; whether a life is *good* for the person may be another. The philosophical question isn't settled by the survey data.

### 3. An ethics

Your well-being is not the only thing that matters. A philosophy of life also needs an account of how to treat others, and of what you owe to strangers, future generations and other animals. That is the subject of the ethics course, and its last chapter, [Building Your Ethics](../learn-ethics/22-building-your-ethics.md), offers a method. Its core is John Rawls's **reflective equilibrium** ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md); [the Ethics course, Chapter 12](../learn-ethics/12-contractualism-and-justice.md)): you move back and forth between your judgments about particular cases and your general principles, revising each in light of the other, until they fit together. No single theory has won, but most people, philosophers included, share a large core of judgments (cruelty for its own sake is wrong, promises matter, people are owed honesty, suffering is bad wherever it occurs) and the theories are best understood as different attempts to explain and extend that core.

Two lessons from the survey are worth adding. The first is Mill's ([Chapter 16](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md)): since no one has the final truth about the good life, a society should allow, and an individual should welcome, a diversity of ways of living:

> As it is useful that while mankind are imperfect there should be different opinions, so is it that there should be different experiments of living; that free scope should be given to varieties of character, short of injury to others; and that the worth of different modes of life should be proved practically, when any one thinks fit to try them.
>
> — John Stuart Mill, *On Liberty*, Chapter III

The second is the lesson of [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md) and [Chapter 23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md): a personal ethics is incomplete without a view of the institutions you live in. Private virtue inside an unjust structure is still participation in the structure. The Stoics knew this; Marcus Aurelius ran an empire. A philosophy of life must ask not only "How should I live?" but "How should we live together, and what is my part in changing it?"

### 4. A response to the question of meaning

Even with a good life and a sound ethics, you can ask: what's the point? Why does any of it matter? This is the question the nihilism course takes on, and [its final chapter](../learn-nihilism/15-beyond-nihilism.md) surveys the current debate in detail. Here is a short version.

Three broad families of answers dominate contemporary philosophy.

- **Supernaturalism.** Life has meaning only if God or a transcendent order gives it a purpose. Tolstoy's *A Confession* (1882) describes the crisis that follows when this foundation seems to give way. Critics reply that being assigned a purpose by another, even God, is not obviously what makes a life meaningful, and that the view implies that if God doesn't exist, no life has meaning.
- **Subjectivism.** A life is meaningful to the extent that the person finds it so: is engaged, fulfilled, caught up in what they care about. Richard Taylor's reading of the myth of Sisyphus is the classic example. If the gods implanted in Sisyphus a burning desire to roll stones, his life would be meaningful to him. Critics reply that this makes a life devoted to counting blades of grass as meaningful as one devoted to curing disease.
- **Objectivism and hybrids.** Susan Wolf's influential view (first stated in a 1997 essay, developed in *Meaning in Life and Why It Matters*, 2010) is that meaning arises "when subjective attraction meets objective attractiveness": when you are actively and lovingly engaged in projects that are actually worthwhile, whether in relationships, creative work, inquiry or service. Thaddeus Metz's "fundamentality" theory identifies meaning with orienting your rational nature toward the fundamental conditions of human existence: the true, the good, the beautiful.

Thomas Nagel's "The Absurd" (1971) adds a twist. The sense of absurdity comes from our ability to step back and see our lives from outside, from a viewpoint from which nothing we do seems to matter, while still being unable to stop caring from inside. Nagel's advice is neither despair nor Camus's heroic defiance ([the Nihilism course, Chapter 10](../learn-nihilism/10-camus-and-the-absurd.md)) but irony: the absurd is just a feature of being a creature who can reflect, and it's no tragedy.

William James, in a lecture to the Harvard YMCA in 1895, gave the pragmatist answer ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md)). Whether life is worth living, he argued, is not entirely a fact waiting to be discovered. It depends partly on us, on whether we bring to it the energy and trust that make it worth living:

> These, then, are my last words to you: Be not afraid of life. Believe that life *is* worth living, and your belief will help create the fact.
>
> — William James, "Is Life Worth Living?", in *The Will to Believe*

That can sound like wishful thinking. But James's point is narrower and stronger. In some domains, such as friendship, courage and commitment to a cause, belief really does help to create the fact. A person who believes their friendship can survive a quarrel acts in ways that help it survive. Meaning may be one of those domains.

### 5. Practices

The last component is the one modern philosophy most often neglects: the habits that make your views livable. Hadot showed that ancient philosophers practiced **spiritual exercises**: meditation on death, the view from above, the evening review of one's day, attention to the present moment, reading and writing as self-formation. Foucault's late work ([Chapter 23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md)) called them technologies of the self. They get a full chapter in the Stoicism course ([Chapter 12](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md)).

Every tradition has its own. Confucius began not with a theory but with a life of learning:

> The Master said, 'At fifteen, I had my mind bent on learning.
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* II.4 (tr. James Legge)

The passage continues through the decades to seventy, when he "could follow what my heart desired, without transgressing what was right": a whole life as a training whose aim is that desire and rightness coincide. Buddhist practice has meditation and the precepts, Christian practice prayer and examination of conscience, and Spinoza the slow work of understanding one's passions until they lose their grip. Spinoza ends the *Ethics* with a warning that applies to all of them:

> How would it be possible, if salvation were ready to our hand, and could without great labour be found, that it should be by almost all men neglected? But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare.
>
> — Spinoza, *Ethics*, Part V, Proposition 42, Note (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

The objection to the practices is that they can become techniques of adjustment: ways of making you content with circumstances you should change, or fit for an employer's demands (the Stoic-flavored "resilience training" that Byung-Chul Han would call self-exploitation). The answer is to keep the practices tied to the ethics and politics above. A practice that makes you calmer so that you can act better is a good practice. A practice that makes you calmer so that you stop noticing injustice is not.

## Time and death

One test of a philosophy of life is what it says about time and death, because both are unavoidable. Here the traditions converge more than anywhere else. Seneca's essay on the shortness of life opens with the complaint that life is too short and then reverses it:

> We do not have a very short time assigned to us, but we lose a great deal of it: life is long enough to carry out the most important projects: we have an ample portion, if we do but arrange the whole of it aright...
>
> — Seneca, "Of the Shortness of Life" I (tr. Aubrey Stewart)

Marcus Aurelius drew a stranger conclusion from the same premise: whether you live three thousand years or thirty, you can only ever lose the present moment, because that is all you have.

> If thou shouldst live three thousand, or as many as ten thousands of years, yet remember this, that man can part with no life properly, save with that little part of life, which he now lives: and that which he lives, is no other, than that which at every instant he parts with.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* II.14 (tr. Meric Casaubon, who numbers it II.12)

Epicurus argued that death is nothing to us, since where death is, we are not ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)); Montaigne titled an early essay "That to study philosophy is to learn to die" and, in his last essays, seemed to arrive at the opposite: that to study philosophy is to learn to live, and that dying would take care of itself. Heidegger made being-toward-death the condition of an authentic life ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)), and Parfit, having concluded that personal identity is not what matters, reported that he found death less frightening: the walls of the glass tunnel he had seemed to live in had disappeared.

The common thread: since time is limited and death certain, the question is not how to get more time but how to inhabit the time you have. That is less a doctrine than a direction of attention.

## Holding views under uncertainty

A philosophy of life built after a survey like this one will be held with less certainty than the ancient schools hoped. That raises a practical question: how do you act on views you know might be wrong?

Several tools help.

- **Proportion belief to evidence and stakes.** Hold contested views tentatively, and act more cautiously when the cost of being wrong is high and irreversible. Philosophers working on **moral uncertainty** (William MacAskill, Krister Bykvist and Toby Ord's *Moral Uncertainty*, 2020) have developed this into formal proposals for acting well when you are unsure which moral theory is correct.
- **Look for robust actions.** Some choices are good on almost every view: honesty, kindness to the vulnerable, care for the people close to you, avoiding needless suffering. Where theories converge, you can act with confidence even without settling the theory.
- **Keep a commonplace book.** The tradition of keeping notes on what you read and think, practiced by Marcus Aurelius, Montaigne, Locke and many others, turns reading into self-examination.
- **Find interlocutors.** Socrates did philosophy in conversation for a reason. Your own views will look most solid when no one questions them. A reading group, a friend who disagrees with you, or an online forum with good norms will find your weak points faster than you will.
- **Return to Montaigne.** Montaigne's motto was *Que sais-je?* ("What do I know?"). His *Essays* are the best model in literature of a mind that holds strong opinions, revises them in public, and doesn't mistake its revisions for a failure. His last essay ends with a deflationary account of the best lives:

> 'Tis to much purpose to go upon stilts, for, when upon stilts, we must yet walk with our legs; and when seated upon the most elevated throne in the world, we are but seated upon our breech. The fairest lives, in my opinion, are those which regularly accommodate themselves to the common and human model without miracle, without extravagance.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, "Of Experience", *Essays* III.13 (tr. Charles Cotton)

Russell's closing paragraph in *The Problems of Philosophy* gives the other half of the answer. Uncertainty is not just a burden but a liberation: the mind that contemplates questions it cannot yet answer is enlarged by them.

> Philosophy is to be studied, not for the sake of any definite answers to its questions, since no definite answers can, as a rule, be known to be true, but rather for the sake of the questions themselves; because these questions enlarge our conception of what is possible, enrich our intellectual imagination and diminish the dogmatic assurance which closes the mind against speculation; but above all because, through the greatness of the universe which philosophy contemplates, the mind also is rendered great, and becomes capable of that union with the universe which constitutes its highest good.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Chapter XV

## Writing your own

The best way to find out what you think is to write it down. Here is a structure, adapted from the ancient genre of the *enchiridion* (handbook), for a document of two to five pages. Write it, put it away for a month, and revise it.

1. **The world.** Three to five sentences on God, mind, freedom and the universe, with a confidence level for each and the argument that moves you most.
2. **A good life.** Your view among hedonism, desire satisfaction, objective list and hybrids, with an example from your own life that tests it.
3. **Obligations to others.** Your core ethical commitments, the theory or theories that best explain them, and the case where you are least sure.
4. **Meaning.** The projects, people and activities that matter to you, and your answer to the objection that they don't really matter.
5. **Practices.** Two or three daily or weekly practices, and what each is for.
6. **What would change your mind.** For each section, the evidence or argument that would make you revise it.

The last section is the most important. A philosophy of life that nothing could change isn't a philosophy; it's a creed.

## How to keep studying

**Read primary texts.** Secondary sources are useful, but there is no substitute for Plato, Hume, Kant or Wittgenstein in their own words. A reasonable goal is one classic a season, read slowly, with a good commentary beside it.

**Use the reference works.** The *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* (plato.stanford.edu) is free, written by leading specialists, and regularly updated; it is the single best resource in the field. The *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy* is a good complement. PhilPapers (philpapers.org) indexes almost the entire professional literature, with bibliographies by topic.

**Listen and watch.** Peter Adamson's podcast *History of Philosophy without Any Gaps* covers the whole history, including Indian, Chinese, Islamic and African traditions, in short episodes, and is accurate enough for graduate students. *Philosophy Bites* offers short interviews with leading philosophers.

**Join a conversation.** Reading groups, public-philosophy events, and philosophy cafés, open discussions held in cafés and bookshops around the world, give you interlocutors. Teaching is the best way to learn: try explaining the Euthyphro dilemma or the trolley problem to a friend, a child or a class.

**If you consider graduate study**, know the field. Admission to PhD programs is very competitive, the academic job market is difficult, and most PhDs in philosophy do not end up in permanent academic posts. Talk to current graduate students. Look at a department's placement record. A master's degree, especially a funded one, is a good way to test your interest and strengthen an application. The writing sample, a polished paper of around 15–20 pages that makes an original argument, matters more than anything else in the application. The *Philosophical Gourmet Report* ranks programs by specialty; read it alongside placement data and student reports.

**Don't stop at the canon.** This survey has necessarily left out much: Islamic and Jewish medieval philosophy beyond a few names ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)), most of Indian and Chinese philosophy after the classical period ([Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md)), African and Latin American philosophy, and whole fields such as aesthetics, philosophy of religion and philosophy of mathematics. Each is a world.

## Living it

1. **Write your handbook.** Use the six-part structure above to write a two- to five-page statement of your philosophy of life. Date it. Reread and revise it in a year.
2. **A practice for a month.** Choose one practice from a tradition other than the one you were raised in (an evening review, a weekly day of reflection, a meditation, a commonplace book) and keep it for thirty days. At the end, write a paragraph on what it did and what it was for.
3. **The time audit.** Following Seneca, track for one week how you actually spend your time. Then compare it with section 4 of your handbook. Where are the gaps between what you say matters and what you do?
4. **One argument a week.** For the next year, take one argument from this course each week, reconstruct it in numbered premises, and write your strongest objection. At the end you will have fifty-two short papers, and you will think more clearly than you do now.

## Summary

- The survey teaches six durable lessons: positions have **costs**; **arguments outlast conclusions**; problems have **histories**; philosophy gives birth to sciences; **disagreement persists** and calls for humility, not silence; traditions **converge** on some insights about suffering, judgment and habit.
- The **four courses** are four lenses: breadth and history (survey), a complete lived practice (Stoicism), the strongest challenge (nihilism) and a method for action (ethics). Each corrects the others.
- A philosophy of life has **five components**: a worldview held fallibly; an account of **well-being** (hedonism, desire satisfaction, objective list, hybrids; Nozick's experience machine; Aristotle's "complete life"; Russell's "love and knowledge"); an **ethics** built by reflective equilibrium, with Mill's experiments of living and attention to institutions; a response to **meaning** (supernaturalist, subjectivist, objectivist and hybrid views; Wolf; Nagel's absurd; James's belief that helps create the fact); and **practices** (Hadot's spiritual exercises, Confucian learning, Spinoza's "difficult as they are rare"), kept tied to ethics so they don't become adjustment techniques.
- On **time and death**, the traditions converge: Seneca's "we lose a great deal of it", Marcus's present moment, Epicurus, Montaigne, Heidegger, Parfit. The question is how to inhabit the time you have.
- Under **uncertainty**: proportion belief to evidence and stakes, look for robust actions, keep a commonplace book, find interlocutors, and remember Montaigne's ordinary model and Russell's enlarged mind.
- Keep studying through primary texts, the SEP and PhilPapers, podcasts, reading groups and teaching. Approach graduate study with eyes open. Follow the **reading path** above.

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — On what you have learned

**Socrates:** You have spent many weeks on philosophy, I hear, from Thales to the present day. Tell me, then: what do you now know that you did not know before?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I know a lot of arguments: the problem of induction, the Gettier cases, the hard problem of consciousness, the original position. I know what the main positions are on most big questions and what the objections are to each.

**Socrates:** Then you know a great deal about what others have thought. But do you know which of them is right?

**You:** Not for most of them. If I'm honest, the more I learned, the less sure I became.

**Socrates:** So you know that you don't know. That is something. When I was young, I thought the same discovery would be a misfortune; later I thought it was the beginning of wisdom. But tell me: has it changed how you live?

**You:** A little. I'm slower to dismiss people who disagree with me. I notice when I'm assuming something. I've started to wonder what I actually think a good life is.

**Hume:** If I may interrupt. Be careful, young friend, not to expect too much from it. I spent years in the deepest skepticism, and then I went to dinner, played backgammon and was merry with my friends, and the doubts seemed cold and ridiculous. Philosophy cannot carry the whole weight of a life.

**Socrates:** And yet you went back to your study after dinner, David.

**Hume:** I did. I couldn't help it.

**You:** Then here's my answer. What I've learned isn't a set of conclusions but a set of habits: to ask for the argument, to look for the cost of a view, to take seriously people who disagree, and to notice when I'm not sure. Hume is right that philosophy can't carry the whole weight of a life. But Socrates is right that a life never examined is carrying weight it doesn't know about. So I'll keep examining, and I'll keep going to dinner.

</details>

### Dialogue 2 — On whether a philosophy of life should be a system

**Epictetus:** You want a philosophy of life? Then choose one, and practice it. Some things are within your power and some are not; begin there, and do not turn aside. A man who tastes every school and commits to none will never make progress in any.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Epictetus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I see the point. Picking from every tradition could make me a tourist, choosing whatever's comfortable. But committing to one school means accepting parts I think are false, such as the Stoic belief in a providential cosmos.

**Epictetus:** Then drop the parts you cannot believe and keep the discipline. But keep it. A wrestler who changes his training every week loses every match.

**Montaigne:** Allow me to defend the tourist. I have practiced no system in my life. I tried the Stoics when I was young and wanted to be brave, and found I was no Cato. I read Sextus and wrote *Que sais-je?* on my medal. In the end I learned most from watching myself and the people around me, and from writing it down.

**Epictetus:** And did you become wise?

**Montaigne:** No. But I became, I think, a bit less foolish, and much better company.

**You:** Is there a middle way? I could choose a core, say Epictetus's distinction between what's up to me and what isn't, and practice it seriously, while keeping Montaigne's openness about everything else.

**Epictetus:** Provided you practice it. The doctrine is worth nothing unread in the body.

**Montaigne:** Provided you remain willing to discover that you were wrong about it.

**You:** Then that's my position. A philosophy of life needs a core of practice that I don't renegotiate every week, because otherwise nothing gets trained. But the theory around the core should stay open, because I learned in this course that every system has costs, including the one I'm drawn to. Epictetus gives me the discipline; Montaigne keeps me honest about it.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On believing that life is worth living

**James:** I told a room of young men that if they believe life is worth living, their belief will help create the fact. Do you think I was right?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer James in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** In some ways. If I believe life is worth living, I'll throw myself into things, and that might make my life better. But that sounds like a self-help slogan, not a truth. Believing something doesn't make it so.

**Russell:** Precisely my objection. The question whether life is worth living is either a question of fact, in which case we should believe whatever the evidence supports, or it is a matter of feeling, in which case there is nothing to believe. James wants to smuggle feeling in as fact.

**James:** Not smuggle; I admit it openly. Some facts can't come into being without a prior faith in their coming. A friendship, a team's victory, a revolution: none of them happens unless someone first trusts that it can. I ask whether the worth of a life is one of those facts.

**Russell:** And if it isn't?

**James:** Then I've lost nothing by the trust, and gained a life lived with energy. If it is, the doubter has lost the thing itself.

**Russell:** That's a wager, not an argument. And I notice you're not offering it to the man in real despair.

**James:** I was offering it to him above all. I had been that man.

**You:** Then I think you're both partly right. Russell is right that I shouldn't believe things about the world beyond the evidence: whether there's a God or an afterlife isn't settled by my wanting it. But James is right that "my life is worth living" isn't only a report about the world. It's partly a commitment, and commitments help make what they're about. Russell himself wrote that the good life is "inspired by love and guided by knowledge", and the love part isn't proved by evidence either. So I'll keep my beliefs about the world answerable to evidence, and treat my belief in the worth of my own life as a commitment I renew by acting on it.

</details>
