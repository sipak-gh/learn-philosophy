# Chapter 6 — Aristotle

In 367 BCE a seventeen-year-old from Stagira, a small town on the northern Aegean, arrived in Athens and enrolled in Plato's Academy. He stayed twenty years, until Plato died. Then he tutored the teenage son of Philip of Macedon, the boy who became Alexander the Great. Then he came back to Athens and opened a rival school. Along the way he invented formal logic, founded zoology, wrote the first systematic treatises on ethics, politics, rhetoric, poetics and psychology, and collected the constitutions of 158 Greek cities. For centuries educated Europeans called him simply "the Philosopher." Dante, placing the great pagans in Limbo, did not need to give his name:

> The Master I beheld of those who know,
> Sit with his philosophic family.
> All gaze upon him, and all do him honour.
>
> — Dante, *Inferno* 4.131–133 (tr. Longfellow)

Then the founders of modern science defined themselves against him, and "Aristotelian" became a byword for armchair physics. Both the worship and the contempt missed him. He was a working scientist with a tidy mind who believed that the world is intelligible and made of things, and that to understand a thing is to know what it is, what it is made of, what made it and what it is for. This chapter covers his logic and the *Categories*; substance, matter and form; potentiality and actuality; the four causes; nature and the Unmoved Mover; the soul; and, more briefly, his ethics, politics and theory of tragedy. His ethics gets a full chapter in [the Ethics course, Chapter 5](../learn-ethics/05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md).

## Life and school

Aristotle was born in 384 BCE. His father was court physician to the king of Macedon, which gave him a doctor's habits of observation and tied him all his life to the power that was swallowing Greek independence. Diogenes Laertius, our gossipy biographer (Chapter 1), says he "had a lisping voice," "very thin legs, they say, and small eyes," and that he "used to indulge in very conspicuous dress, and rings, and used to dress his hair carefully." None of it can be checked.

He spent 367–347 at the Academy. The relationship with Plato was close and argumentative. Diogenes reports that Plato complained "Aristotle has kicked us off just as chickens do their mother after they have been hatched." When Plato died and the headship went to Plato's nephew Speusippus, Aristotle left Athens. On the island of Lesbos he did much of his biology, studying the octopus and the dogfish in the lagoon at Pyrrha. In 343 Philip invited him to teach Alexander, then thirteen. There is little sign that either influenced the other: Aristotle went on believing the city-state was the natural unit of political life just as his pupil was making it obsolete.

In 335 he returned to Athens and founded his own school in a gymnasium called the Lyceum. Its members were called Peripatetics, from the covered walk (*peripatos*) where they met, or, in the more charming story, from Aristotle's habit of teaching while walking. The Lyceum was a research institute with a library, specimen collections and cooperative projects: histories of philosophy, medicine and mathematics, and the 158 constitutions. When Alexander died in 323, anti-Macedonian feeling exploded in Athens, and Aristotle was charged with impiety. He withdrew to his mother's estate at Chalcis, remarking (the story goes) that he would not let Athens sin twice against philosophy. He died there the next year, aged sixty-two or sixty-three.

His polished published dialogues, whose style Cicero compared to a golden river, are all lost. What survives is the treatises used inside the school: compressed lecture notes and working drafts, edited centuries later. When you find Aristotle dry, remember that you are reading a professor's notes, not his books. The sayings Diogenes collects show a sharper man. "It was a saying of his that the roots of education were bitter, but the fruit sweet." Asked what a friend is, he answered "One soul abiding in two bodies." And he said "that education was an ornament in prosperity, and a refuge in adversity."

## How Aristotle works

Plato starts from mathematics. Aristotle starts from biology and asks what we can learn by looking. His method is still good method:

1. **Collect the appearances** (*phainomena*): the observed facts, and also the *endoxa*, the reputable opinions of most people or of the wise.
2. **Set out the puzzles** (*aporiai*) that arise when these appearances conflict.
3. **Resolve the puzzles** so as to save as many of the appearances as possible, usually by drawing a distinction showing that each side was partly right.

He thinks ordinary belief and earlier philosophy are rarely simply wrong, so he begins most treatises with a survey of his predecessors. *Metaphysics* Book 1 is a main source for the Presocratics ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)).

He also believed philosophy begins in wonder. Taylor summarizes the opening of the *Metaphysics*: philosophy is "knowledge which we seek for the satisfaction which it carries with itself, and not as a mere means to utilitarian ends," and the impulse behind it is "curiosity or wonder, which Aristotle regards as innate in man." Knowledge is *theoretical* (first philosophy, later called metaphysics; mathematics; physics), *practical* (ethics, politics) or *productive* (rhetoric, poetics, the crafts). Logic is the *organon*, the "instrument," of all of them.

## Logic, briefly

Aristotle is the first author whose systematic formal logic survives, and he claimed the field as his own: on reasoning, he says at the end of the *Sophistical Refutations*, he found no earlier work at all. His theory of the syllogism was treated as complete for two thousand years. [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md) covered the syllogism itself. Three further points belong here.

First, the logic is designed for science. In the *Posterior Analytics* a science is a body of demonstrations: syllogisms whose premises are true, primary and explanatory, showing not merely *that* something is so but *why*. A science looks like geometry, with a few first principles and everything else derived.

Second, the first principles cannot themselves be demonstrated, or there would be an infinite regress. We grasp them by insight (*nous*) that grows out of repeated perception and experience: an empiricist account of where principles come from, joined to a rationalist ideal of science.

Third, the most basic principle of all is the principle of non-contradiction: the same attribute cannot at the same time both belong and not belong to the same subject in the same respect. You cannot prove it, since every proof assumes it, but you can defend it. As Taylor puts the strategy, you "examine the consequences of a denial of the axiom and to show that they include some which are false, or which your antagonist at least considers false." In *Metaphysics* Book 4 the point is sharper: once the denier says "man" and means man and not also not-man, he has conceded the principle. If he refuses to say anything definite, Aristotle says, he is no better off than a plant.

## The *Categories*: what there is

Plato asked what the Beautiful itself is. Aristotle asks a prior question: when we say that something *is*, how many different things might we mean? "Socrates is a man," "Socrates is pale" and "Socrates is in the marketplace" all use "is," but they say very different kinds of thing. The *Categories* sorts them out:

> Expressions which are in no way composite signify substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, or affection.
>
> — Aristotle, *Categories* 4, 1b25–27 (tr. Edghill)

Ten kinds of predicate: what a thing is (a horse), how much (six feet), what sort (pale), related to what (older), where (in the Lyceum), when (yesterday), in what posture (sitting), in what condition (shod), doing what (cutting), and undergoing what (being cut). Whether the list comes from grammar or from the world is still argued. What matters most is the asymmetry between the first category and the rest.

### Primary substance

> Substance, in the truest and primary and most definite sense of the word, is that which is neither predicable of a subject nor present in a subject; for instance, the individual man or horse.
>
> — Aristotle, *Categories* 5, 2a11–14 (tr. Edghill)

Something is *said of* a subject when it tells you what the subject is: "man" is said of Socrates. Something is *in* a subject when it exists only as a feature of something else, as this paleness exists in Socrates' skin. A primary substance is neither. It is the basic subject that everything else is said of or in. Its species and genus, man and animal, are *secondary substances*. Then comes a direct reversal of Plato:

> Thus everything except primary substances is either predicated of primary substances, or is present in them, and if these last did not exist, it would be impossible for anything else to exist.
>
> — Aristotle, *Categories* 5, 2b3–6 (tr. Edghill)

For Plato the most real things were the Forms, and particular horses were their shadows. For the Aristotle of the *Categories* the most real things are particular horses, and "horse" in general exists only because there are horses. Universals are real, but they exist *in* things. Aristotle had heard the theory of Forms from its inventor and rejected it, since, as he says of Plato and his friends, "both are dear to us, we are bound to prefer the truth" ([Chapter 5](05-plato.md)). Taylor sums up his verdict: the separate Forms "are, in fact, no more than" poetic metaphors. Science needs no "one over and above the many," only terms truly predicated of many things.

The *Categories* also gives a test for substance:

> The most distinctive mark of substance appears to be that, while remaining numerically one and the same, it is capable of admitting contrary qualities.
>
> — Aristotle, *Categories* 5, 4a10–12 (tr. Edghill)

A color cannot be both white and black, but Socrates is warm and then cold and is still Socrates. Substances persist through change. That is Aristotle's answer to Parmenides and Heraclitus: change is real, and it is not chaos, because something stays the same while its qualities come and go.

## Matter and form

But a horse is made of flesh and bone, and flesh of earth, water, air and fire. Is the horse basic, or the stuff? The *Physics* and *Metaphysics* answer with Aristotle's most important distinction, between *matter* (*hulē*, literally "timber") and *form* (*eidos* or *morphē*).

Take a bronze statue. Its matter is the bronze, and its form is the shape that makes the bronze a statue of Hermes rather than a lump. Melt it down and the bronze survives, but the statue does not. A house is bricks and timber arranged to shelter people. A living thing is flesh and bone organized to live the life of its kind. Every material object is a composite, and neither half exists on its own.

Matter is relative: bricks are the matter of the house, and clay the matter of the bricks. Form is what makes a thing what it is, so it is what we grasp when we know the thing. The form of an axe is its being an axe, which comes down to its ability to cut. This is why form in Aristotle is so close to *function*.

In *Metaphysics* Book 7, the hardest text in the corpus, Aristotle seems to conclude that substance in the primary sense is form, the "what it is to be" of a thing (Latin *essentia*, our "essence"). How this fits the *Categories*, and whether forms are universal or particular, has been argued since antiquity. Taylor thinks Aristotle never fully escaped Plato: the separate Forms are dismissed as "empty names," yet the source of all form turns out to be God, "a being which is 'pure' form and stands outside the whole development which its presence sets up."

## Potentiality and actuality

Matter and form describe a thing at a moment. To describe growth, Aristotle needs more. Parmenides had argued that change is impossible, since nothing comes from nothing. Aristotle replies that between being and not-being there is a third condition. An acorn is not an oak, but it is not simply not-an-oak, as a pebble is. It is an oak *potentially*. Taylor's gloss:

> Hence we may say of a given germ, "though this is not yet actually an oak, it is potentially an oak," meaning not merely that, if uninterfered with, it will in time be an oak, but also that by no interference can it be made to grow into an elm or a beech.
>
> — A. E. Taylor, *Aristotle* (1919), ch. 3

Change is the actualization of a potentiality, so it needs not something from nothing but a definite capacity becoming something definite. The actual is *energeia*, "being at work" (our "energy"), or *entelecheia*, "having one's end within." Actuality is prior: you understand the acorn only through the oak, and every potential oak came from an actual oak. And development has a stopping point. The acorn becomes the sapling, and the sapling becomes the oak, but "there is nothing related to the oak as the oak is to the sapling." The mature oak, which can produce acorns, is the *end* (*telos*), and that word opens the most controversial part of Aristotle's thought.

## The four causes

To know something is to know its cause. The Greek *aitia* first meant legal "responsibility." Taylor explains:

> Now when we ask, "what is responsible for the fact that such and such a state of things now exists?" there are four partial answers which may be given, and each of these corresponds to one of the "causes." A complete answer requires the enumeration of them all.
>
> — A. E. Taylor, *Aristotle* (1919), ch. 3

The four, with Aristotle's own standard example of a statue:

1. **Material cause**: what it is made of. The bronze.
2. **Formal cause**: what it is, its form or definition. The shape of Hermes.
3. **Efficient cause**: what started the change. The sculptor, or more precisely the art of sculpture in the sculptor.
4. **Final cause**: what it is for. Its *telos*: to honor the god, to adorn the temple.

They are better called the four *becauses*, four kinds of answer to "why?" Only the efficient cause matches our idea of a cause as a prior event. A full explanation of a natural thing, Aristotle claims, uses all four. He presents his predecessors as having each found one: the Milesians the material cause, the Pythagoreans and Plato the formal, Empedocles and Anaxagoras the efficient.

In nature three of the four causes tend to coincide. A man begets a man: the efficient cause, the parent, has the same form that the offspring will come to have, and that form, fully realized, is the end of the process. In craft the form exists first in the craftsman's mind:

> A house does not produce another house, but the house as existing in "idea" in the builder's mind sets him at work building, and so produces a corresponding house in brick or stone.
>
> — A. E. Taylor, *Aristotle* (1919), ch. 3

## Nature does nothing in vain

A *nature* (*phusis*) is an internal principle of change and rest. An acorn grows into an oak because of what it is. Plant a wooden bed and, if anything sprouts, it will be a tree, not a bed (*Physics* 2.1). Natural processes are directed toward ends from within, not by a designer's plan. "Nature," he says again and again, "does nothing in vain."

In *Physics* 2.8 Aristotle states the alternative with startling clarity. It does not rain *in order to* make the corn grow. Why not say the parts of animals arose the same way, and the ones that happened to fit together survived? Darwin quoted the passage in a footnote to the *Origin of Species* and commented:

> Wheresoever, therefore, all things together (that is all the parts of one whole) happened like as if they were made for the sake of something, these were preserved, having been appropriately constituted by an internal spontaneity; and whatsoever things were not thus constituted, perished and still perish." We here see the principle of natural selection shadowed forth, but how little Aristotle fully comprehended the principle, is shown by his remarks on the formation of the teeth.
>
> — Charles Darwin, *On the Origin of Species*, 6th ed. (1872), "An Historical Sketch," note, quoting Aristotle, *Physics* 2.8, 198b

Aristotle's reply to the Empedoclean view is that chance produces results only rarely, while teeth grow in the right way always or for the most part. What happens always or for the most part is not chance. So nature acts for an end.

Darwin's third option is chance *variation* plus non-chance *selection*, which over many generations yields regular, apparently purposive results with nothing aiming at them. Evolutionary theory kept Aristotle's question, "what is this *for*?", which biologists ask of kidneys and peacock tails, and replaced his answer, an inner directedness in each kind, with a history of selection (Dialogue 1).

Darwin himself was not dismissive. When William Ogle sent him a translation of *On the Parts of Animals* in 1882, he replied:

> From quotations which I had seen, I had a high notion of Aristotle's merits, but I had not the most remote notion what a wonderful man he was. Linnaeus and Cuvier have been my two gods, though in very different ways, but they were mere schoolboys to old Aristotle.
>
> — Charles Darwin, letter to William Ogle, 22 February 1882, in *The Life and Letters of Charles Darwin*, ed. Francis Darwin (1887)

The caricature that stones *want* to fall is unfair. Aristotelian ends are mostly the good of the organism itself, and he never ascribes intentions to stones. The real limit of his physics is that it is qualitative, not mathematical, and splits the universe into a perfect heaven and a corruptible earth, the division Galileo and Newton destroyed ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)).

## The Unmoved Mover

Aristotle thought the universe and its motion eternal. Every motion needs a mover, and an eternal, continuous motion such as the rotation of the heavens needs an eternal, continuous source. That source cannot itself be moved, or it would need a mover in turn. So there is an *unmoved mover*: pure actuality, with no potentiality and hence no matter. It is pure form. It is God.

How can something unmoved move anything? Not by pushing. In *Metaphysics* Book 12 it moves things as the beloved moves the lover. Taylor:

> Just as the good I desire and conceive, without itself "moving" "moves" my appetition, so God moves the universe by being its good.
>
> — A. E. Taylor, *Aristotle* (1919), ch. 3

The outermost sphere, imitating God's changelessness as closely as a body can, turns forever in a circle, and the rest of the cosmos follows.

What does God do? The only activity worthy of a perfect being is thought, and the only worthy object is the best thing, God's own thinking. So God's life is "thinking of thought itself" (*noēsis noēseōs*), unaware of the world it moves. This God does not know you or answer prayer, and, as Taylor notes, "He is not regarded by Aristotle as its Creator." Jewish, Christian and Muslim philosophers who took him over as the God of Abraham had to make large repairs; of Aquinas's synthesis Taylor says "one cannot help seeing the seams." The argument from motion became the first of Aquinas's Five Ways ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)).

## The soul as the form of the body

Here Aristotle looks most modern. For Plato, as for Descartes later, the soul is a separate thing that inhabits the body. Aristotle applies matter and form to living things instead. Taylor renders the definition from *De Anima* 2.1 and adds Aristotle's best illustration:

> "The soul is the first entelechy (or actual realisation) of a natural organic body." What this means is that the soul stands to the living body as all form realised in matter does to the matter of which it is the form, or that the soul is the "form" of the body. … Aristotle illustrates the relation by the remark that if the whole body was one vast eye, seeing would be its soul.
>
> — A. E. Taylor, *Aristotle* (1919), ch. 4

The soul is not a thing in the body. It is the body's being organized to live. If an axe were a natural body, cutting would be its soul. Asking whether soul and body are one, Aristotle adds, is like asking whether the wax and the shape stamped on it are one.

Living things form a hierarchy of capacities, each including the ones below:

- **Nutritive soul** (all living things, including plants): growth, nourishment, reproduction.
- **Sensitive soul** (animals): perception, desire, and in most animals movement and imagination.
- **Rational soul** (humans): thought and deliberation.

Perception, too, is explained by form: the soul "takes into itself the _form_ of the thing perceived without its _matter_, as sealing-wax receives the shape of an iron seal-ring without the iron."

This view, *hylomorphism* (from *hulē* and *morphē*), has had a revival. Many see in it a forerunner of *functionalism*, the view that mental states are defined by what they do rather than what they are made of ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)). A mind is to a brain roughly what sight is to an eye: neither a ghostly extra ingredient nor simply the tissue, but its organized activity.

One large loose end remains. In *De Anima* 3.5 Aristotle says that the "active" intellect is separable, unmixed, and immortal and eternal. Is this a part of each soul that survives death, or a single cosmic intellect shared by everyone, as Averroes argued? Medieval theologians, who wanted Aristotle's science and personal immortality at once, cared intensely ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)).

## Ethics: the human function

Aristotle's ethics is the subject of [the Ethics course, Chapter 5](../learn-ethics/05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md), which you should read for the details. Here is the skeleton, which grows directly out of the metaphysics.

The *Nicomachean Ethics* opens with a teleological claim: every art, inquiry, action and choice "aims, it is thought, at some good." Ends are ordered, and if the chain does not go on forever there is a highest good, sought for its own sake. Everyone calls it *eudaimonia*, usually translated "happiness" but closer to "flourishing." People disagree about what it is: pleasure, honor, wealth or contemplation.

To settle it, Aristotle uses the *function argument* (1.7). A harp-player and an eye each have a function (*ergon*, "work"), and their good lies in doing it well. The human function cannot be mere life, which plants share, or perception, which horses share. It must be the life of the part with reason. So:

> if all this is so, then the Good of Man comes to be "a working of the Soul in the way of Excellence," or, if Excellence admits of degrees, in the way of the best and most perfect Excellence.
>
> And we must add, ἐν βίῳ τελείῳ; for as it is not one swallow or one fine day that makes a spring, so it is not one day or a short time that makes a man blessed and happy.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1.7, 1098a (tr. Chase)

The Greek means "in a complete life." Happiness is not a feeling but an activity, sustained over a whole life.

Virtues of character, such as courage, temperance and generosity, are acquired by habit: we become brave by doing brave things, as builders become builders by building. Each is a disposition to feel and act in a *mean* between two vices, as courage lies between cowardice and rashness. The mean is relative to us. A fixed ration does not suit everyone: "for Milo it would be too little, but for a man just commencing his athletic exercises too much." (Milo was the most famous wrestler of antiquity.) The full definition:

> Virtue then is "a state apt to exercise deliberate choice, being in the relative mean, determined by reason, and as the man of practical wisdom would determine."
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 2.6, 1106b–1107a (tr. Chase)

The mean is not moderation. It is the *right* amount, at the right time, toward the right people, and sometimes the right amount of anger is a great deal (Dialogue 3). The final standard is the person of practical wisdom (*phronimos*), not a rulebook, which is why twentieth-century virtue ethics went back to Aristotle ([the Ethics course, Chapter 13](../learn-ethics/13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)).

Book 10 adds a twist. The highest happiness is contemplation, *theōria*, the activity of the divine in us and the nearest we come to the life of the Unmoved Mover:

> Yet must we not give ear to those who bid one as man to mind only man's affairs, or as mortal only mortal things; but, so far as we can, make ourselves like immortals and do all with a view to living in accordance with the highest Principle in us, for small as it may be in bulk yet in power and preciousness it far more excels all the others.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 10.7, 1177b (tr. Chase)

How this fits with the praise of practical virtue in the rest of the *Ethics* is much debated. You may notice that a lifelong researcher ended his ethics by telling his students that research is the best life.

## Politics: the political animal

For Aristotle ethics and politics are one inquiry, since laws are needed to make people good. The *Politics* opens with a natural history of the city. Households join into villages, and villages into the *polis*, which comes into being for the sake of life and exists for the sake of the good life. Then come his most famous sentences:

> Hence it is evident that a city is a natural production, and that man is naturally a political animal, and that whosoever is naturally and not accidentally unfit for society, must be either inferior or superior to man …. The gift of speech also evidently proves that man is a more social animal than the bees, or any of the herding cattle: for nature, as we say, does nothing in vain, and man is the only animal who enjoys it.
>
> — Aristotle, *Politics* 1.2, 1253a (tr. Ellis)

"Political animal" (*zōon politikon*) means more than "social animal." Bees are social. Humans are made for a *polis*, a self-governing community that deliberates about justice. Animals have voice, which signals pleasure and pain. Humans have speech (*logos*), by which "we are enabled to express what is useful for us, and what is hurtful, and of course what is just and what is unjust." The city is prior by nature to the individual, as the body is to the hand, and whoever can live outside a city "makes no part of a city, as a beast or a god."

This is the founding text of *communitarianism* ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)), the view that we are constituted by our communities and that the liberal picture of individuals contracting into society gets things backward.

### Natural slavery

Then the *Politics* turns to the household, and the argument goes badly wrong. The household needs tools, and some tools are alive. On the way, Aristotle imagines automation. If every instrument could do its work at command, like the self-moving statues of Daedalus, "the shuttle would then weave, and the lyre play of itself; nor would the architect want servants," nor the master slaves. Marx quoted the passage in *Capital*, and every debate about automation rediscovers it.

Then comes the doctrine that has done his reputation most harm. Some people, he argues, are slaves *by nature*: they lack the power to deliberate, and so benefit from being ruled, as the body benefits from rule by the soul.

> Those men therefore who are as much inferior to others as the body is to the soul, are to be thus disposed of, as the proper use of them is their bodies, in which their excellence consists; and if what I have said be true, they are slaves by nature, and it is advantageous to them to be always under government.
>
> — Aristotle, *Politics* 1.5, 1254b (tr. Ellis)

Later he applies the same reasoning to women: "a slave can have no power of determination, a woman but a weak one, a child an imperfect one" (1.13).

Aristotle knew the view was contested. He reports that some thought slavery was "contrary to nature," a matter of force, and he concedes that people enslaved in war are often not natural slaves. He never identifies a natural slave, and nothing in his biology shows that any exist. The doctrine is a valid argument from a false and unexamined premise, and it shows what teleology can do in the wrong hands: "nature does nothing in vain" slides into "what is, is natural," and that into "what is natural is right." Juan Ginés de Sepúlveda cited it in 1550 to justify the conquest of the Americas. Bartolomé de las Casas answered him in Aristotle's own terms: the peoples of the Americas plainly had the rational capacities that natural slaves were supposed to lack. Dialogue 2 takes this further.

### The best practical constitution

The rest of the *Politics* is more empirical. Aristotle classifies constitutions by who rules (one, few or many) and in whose interest (the common good or their own). That gives three correct forms, kingship, aristocracy and "polity," and three deviant forms, tyranny, oligarchy and democracy (rule by the poor in their own interest). Unlike Plato he wants a constitution for real cities, and his advice is to rely on the middle class:

> It is plain, then, that the most perfect political community must be amongst those who are in the middle rank, and those states are best instituted wherein these are a larger and more respectable part, if possible, than both the other; or, if that cannot be, at least than either of them separate; so that being thrown into the balance it may prevent either scale from preponderating.
>
> — Aristotle, *Politics* 4.11, 1295b (tr. Ellis)

People in the middle "will neither covet what belongs to others, as the poor do; nor will others covet what is theirs." This is the doctrine of the mean applied to class. Modern political scientists who link stable democracy to a large middle class are, knowingly or not, repeating it.

## Poetics: why we enjoy tragedy

Plato banished the poets because poetry imitates appearances and stirs up emotions reason should govern. Aristotle's short *Poetics* is in large part a reply. Imitation (*mimēsis*) is natural to us, and we enjoy accurate images even of painful things, because learning is pleasant. Then comes his definition of tragedy:

> Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude; in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament, the several kinds being found in separate parts of the play; in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions.
>
> — Aristotle, *Poetics* 6, 1449b (tr. Butcher)

"Purgation" translates *katharsis*, which can mean medical purging, religious purification or intellectual clarification. Either tragedy drains off excess pity and fear, or it teaches us what is truly pitiable and fearful and so trains the emotions, as the *Ethics* would want. Either way, the emotions are not a disease but something to be educated. Plot comes first; the best plot turns on a reversal and a recognition, and its hero falls through an error (*hamartia*), not wickedness. Then the most direct answer to Plato:

> The true difference is that one relates what has happened, the other what may happen. Poetry, therefore, is a more philosophical and a higher thing than history: for poetry tends to express the universal, history the particular.
>
> — Aristotle, *Poetics* 9, 1451b (tr. Butcher)

Plato had called poetry thrice removed from the truth. Aristotle says it shows what *kind* of person does what *kind* of thing. Every argument that you learn more about jealousy from *Othello* than from a news report descends from this sentence.

## The afterlife of Aristotle

His successor Theophrastus founded botany, but the Hellenistic schools ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) soon eclipsed the Lyceum. The story goes that Aristotle's manuscripts lay for generations in a cellar before Andronicus of Rhodes edited them in the first century BCE. Then came a long, strange career.

- **Late antiquity.** Porphyry's *Isagoge*, an introduction to the *Categories*, asked whether genera and species exist in things or only in the mind, and so started the medieval problem of universals. Boethius translated the logic into Latin around 510 CE, and for six centuries that was nearly all of Aristotle the Latin West knew.
- **The Islamic world.** In Baghdad, from the eighth century on, Aristotle was translated into Arabic, often via Syriac, and became *al-Muʿallim al-Awwal*, "the First Teacher." Al-Farabi, Avicenna and Averroes, known in Latin simply as "the Commentator," built on him, as Maimonides did for Jewish thought.
- **The Latin West.** From the twelfth century the full corpus reached the new universities, a complete system of the world at odds with doctrine on creation and the soul. Paris banned his natural philosophy in 1210 and required it by 1255. Aquinas made him "the Philosopher" of Catholic theology ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)).
- **The revolt.** The Scientific Revolution rejected natural places, celestial perfection and final causes in physics. Bacon called for a *New Organon*, and Galileo mocked the Aristotelians as Simplicio ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)).
- **The return.** In the twentieth century he came back in virtue ethics, philosophy of mind, metaphysics (essences and powers) and biology.

The scientists who overthrew Aristotle were doing what he did, saving the appearances, with better instruments and mathematics. His own rule, prefer the truth, licensed the revolt against him.

## Living it

- **Ask the four "why"s.** Faced with an object, an institution or a habit of your own, ask what it is made of, what it is, what produced it and what it is for. Most explanations stop at one. A meeting is made of people and time; it is a decision procedure; it was called by your manager; what is it *for*? Many meetings have no answer to the fourth question.
- **Find your function, cautiously.** Aristotle asks what you are distinctively good at, and what it would be to do it well over a whole life. Use the question without its dangerous cousin: never conclude from "people of type X are not doing Y" that they are *not made for* Y.
- **Locate the mean, case by case.** For an emotion you struggle with (anger, fear, generosity, candor), write down the two vices on either side. Then recall a specific recent case and ask which way you erred. The mean is relative to you and to the situation. A rule will not find it; practice will.
- **Habituate.** You become brave by doing brave things. Pick one small act of the virtue you lack and do it daily for a month. Aristotle's claim is that the feelings follow the actions, not the reverse.
- **Steelman before you refute.** Aristotle begins with what others got right. Before disagreeing with someone this week, state the true part of their view in words they would accept.

## Summary

- Aristotle (384–322 BCE) studied in Plato's Academy for twenty years, tutored Alexander, and founded the Lyceum, often called the first research institute. What survives are his school treatises, not his published works.
- His method is to collect appearances and reputable opinions, set out the puzzles, and resolve them by distinctions that save as much as possible.
- He wrote the first systematic formal logic that survives. A science is a system of demonstrations from first principles, which are grasped by insight that grows out of experience. The principle of non-contradiction cannot be proved but can be defended.
- The *Categories* distinguishes ten kinds of being and makes individual substances, this man or this horse, primary. Universals exist in things, not apart, and substances persist through change by taking on contrary qualities.
- Material things are composites of matter and form. Change is the actualization of potentiality, which answers Parmenides.
- A full explanation gives four causes: material, formal, efficient and final. Nature acts for ends from within. Darwin's natural selection replaced the mechanism of this teleology while keeping its questions.
- The eternal motion of the heavens requires an Unmoved Mover, pure actuality, which moves the world as an object of desire and spends eternity in "thinking of thought itself."
- The soul is the form of a living body, its organized capacity to live. That makes Aristotle a forerunner of functionalism, though one passage on the active intellect points beyond the body.
- Happiness is excellent rational activity over a complete life. Character virtues are habits lying in a mean relative to us, judged by the practically wise. Book 10 puts contemplation highest.
- Humans are political animals by nature, made for the *polis* by speech. His doctrine of natural slavery is a cautionary example of teleology serving prejudice. He favored a mixed constitution resting on a large middle class.
- Tragedy arouses pity and fear and works their *katharsis*. Poetry is more philosophical than history because it expresses the universal.
- Through Arabic and Latin commentators he became "the Philosopher." The Scientific Revolution overthrew his physics, and twentieth-century philosophy revived his ethics, his metaphysics and his view of the mind.

## Dialogues

These dialogues are imagined, in the tradition of Plato's. Answer before you open each continuation.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether anything in nature is for anything

**Aristotle:** You have teeth, sharp at the front and broad at the back. I say the front teeth are for cutting and the back teeth for grinding. You have read Darwin, so I am told you think I am wrong. Are they not for cutting and grinding?

**You:** They do cut and grind, but nothing designed them to. Random mutations produced variations, and the animals whose teeth worked better left more offspring. "For" is just a manner of speaking.

*Your turn: answer Aristotle in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Aristotle:** I did not say anything designed them. I said nature does nothing in vain. Do you deny that teeth exist because they cut and grind?

**You:** No, that's exactly what selection says. The teeth that cut well were kept because they cut well.

**Aristotle:** Then cutting appears in your explanation of why the teeth are there. Does rain fall because it makes the corn grow?

**You:** No. Rain falls because water vapor condenses. If no corn existed, it would fall just the same.

**Aristotle:** And if no animal ever cut its food, would sharp front teeth still be produced, generation after generation?

**You:** No. They would drift away, or never appear in the first place.

**Aristotle:** So there is a difference between the rain and the teeth. The effect of the rain plays no part in why it falls. The effect of the teeth plays a part in why they exist. That difference is what I meant by "that for the sake of which."

**You:** All right, there's a difference. But you thought the "for" was built into the nature of each kind, an inner striving. We think it's just the record of past selection. No striving, no goal.

**Aristotle:** Then you have improved my account of *why* the "for" holds, and I gladly accept the improvement. But tell me: when your physicians ask what the kidney is for, are they speaking loosely?

**You:** No, they mean something exact: what kidneys do that explains why they were selected. Without that you couldn't say a kidney is malfunctioning.

**Aristotle:** And a malfunctioning kidney, is it bad?

**You:** It's bad for the animal. That's why medicine exists.

**Aristotle:** Then you have kept my ends, my functions and my notion of a good proper to each living thing. You have only replaced my account of where they come from. I count that as my question surviving your answer.

*What we learned:* Darwin did not abolish function talk. He explained it. The difference between a trait that exists *because of* its effects and an event that merely *has* effects is real, and biology needs it. What disappears is Aristotle's inner striving toward form. Whether "good for the organism" survives as a real value or only as a manner of speaking is the next question, and it leads straight into ethics.

### Dialogue 2 — On how a great mind defended slavery

**Aristotle:** You seem angry with me about the first book of my *Politics*. But consider the argument. The soul rules the body, and that is good for the body. Reason rules appetite, and that is good for the whole person. If there were human beings with no capacity for deliberation, would it not be good for them to be ruled by those who have it?

**You:** Maybe, if such people existed. But they don't. Everyone you enslaved could deliberate. Many of them were Greeks captured in war, and you admit yourself that their slavery was unjust.

*Your turn: answer Aristotle in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Aristotle:** I do admit it. Then my error, you say, is not in the logic but in a premise?

**You:** Yes. The argument is valid. Its factual premise is false, and you never checked it. You dissected hundreds of animals, but you never investigated whether any human being actually lacks deliberation.

**Aristotle:** That is a hard charge against someone who prided himself on looking. Why do you think I did not look?

**You:** Because the conclusion suited you. Your city ran on slave labor. You needed slaves for the leisure that philosophy requires, and you said so yourself.

**Aristotle:** So a thinker's interests can bend which questions he thinks to ask?

**You:** Yes. And "nature" made it worse. Once you said slavery was natural, it looked like a fact rather than an institution someone had chosen.

**Aristotle:** Then the fault lies partly in my favorite word. But I also wrote that the shuttle might weave of itself. If it did, I said, masters would need no slaves.

**You:** You did, and it's the most honest sentence in the book. It admits that the slavery was about the work that needed doing, not about the slaves' natures.

**Aristotle:** And your own cities: do any of them hold that some people are fit only to labor and others only to think? Do your arguments about it ever borrow the word "natural"?

**You:** More often than I'd like. People say some groups are "naturally" suited to certain jobs. They say it about women, about the poor, about whole countries.

**Aristotle:** Then you may use my error as a test. When an argument concludes that the people who serve you were made to serve, examine its factual premise twice.

*What we learned:* The doctrine of natural slavery is valid reasoning from a false and unexamined empirical premise, and the premise was made comfortable by self-interest. The concept of "the natural" can turn an existing arrangement into a necessity. Aristotle's own shuttle passage concedes that the institution served the masters' needs. The general test is to check the facts most carefully when a conclusion flatters the people who draw it.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether the mean is just mediocrity

**Aristotle:** You told your friend you admired my ethics except for the doctrine of the mean, which you called "a recipe for lukewarm people." Tell me what you think I meant.

**You:** That virtue is moderation. Don't get too angry, don't be too generous, don't care too much. Always pick the middle option. It sounds safe, but great people are often extreme.

*Your turn: answer Aristotle in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Aristotle:** Suppose a man sees a child being beaten by a drunk in the street, and he feels mild irritation and walks on. Has he hit the mean of anger?

**You:** No. He should be furious and he should intervene.

**Aristotle:** Then the right amount of anger here is great. Is great anger moderate?

**You:** Not in the ordinary sense. So the mean isn't a midpoint.

**Aristotle:** I said it was relative to us and to the case: the right feeling, at the right time, toward the right person, for the right reason. Milo eats more than a beginner, and the trainer is not being immoderate when he feeds him. Now, your great people who are "extreme." Name one.

**You:** Someone who gives away nearly everything to the poor.

**Aristotle:** Does he give to the right people, for the right reason, in a way he can sustain?

**You:** Suppose he does.

**Aristotle:** Then he is generous, not extravagant, and he has hit the mean, however far it lies from what most people give. The man who gives everything to flatterers to be admired has missed it, even if the sums are the same.

**You:** Then what does the mean actually tell me? "Feel the right amount" sounds empty. Of course I should feel the right amount.

**Aristotle:** As a rule it is nearly empty, and I say so. I said the standard is what the person of practical wisdom would determine. The doctrine tells you where to look: for two ways of failing, not one. Most people know only one vice of each passion, the one they fear in themselves.

**You:** So the coward worries he's not brave enough, but never thinks about rashness, and the reckless person never thinks about cowardice.

**Aristotle:** Yes. Each person tends to drift toward one side, so each should lean a little toward the other, as one straightens a warped stick by bending it the opposite way.

*What we learned:* The mean is not moderation but appropriateness, and the appropriate response can be intense. Taken alone, the doctrine gives little guidance, and Aristotle knew it. That is why the final standard is the practically wise person. Its real use is diagnostic: every virtue can fail in two directions, and most of us watch for only one.

</details>
