# Chapter 15 — Nietzsche

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844–1900) wanted to philosophize "with a hammer," and he swung it at almost everything the previous fourteen chapters have built: Plato's eternal Forms, the Christian God, Descartes's transparent self, Kant's moral law, the Hegelian faith in historical progress, even the scholar's ideal of disinterested truth. Yet he also asked the most constructive question of the nineteenth century: if the old foundations of value collapse, how can human beings go on affirming life? This chapter is a short overview that places Nietzsche in the history of philosophy. He gets two full chapters in the nihilism course, [The death of God](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) and [Beyond nihilism](../learn-nihilism/07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md), and his critique of morality is treated in depth in the ethics course, [Nietzsche and the critique of morality](../learn-ethics/15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md). Read this chapter as the map and those as the territory.

Previous: [Chapter 14 — Hegel, Marx, and Their Critics](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md). Next: [Chapter 16 — Utilitarianism and Liberalism](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md).

## A life in brief

Nietzsche was the son of a Lutheran pastor who died when Friedrich was four. A brilliant student of classical philology, he was appointed professor at Basel at twenty-four, before he had finished his doctorate. He discovered Schopenhauer's *World as Will and Idea* in a Leipzig bookshop ([Chapter 14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md)) and became an intimate of Richard Wagner. Chronic illness (migraines, eye trouble, vomiting) forced him to resign in 1879. For ten years he lived on a small pension in boarding houses in Switzerland, Italy and the south of France, walking for hours a day and writing the books that made him famous after he could no longer know it. In January 1889, in Turin, he collapsed (legend says while embracing a horse being flogged) and spent his last eleven years in mental darkness, cared for by his mother and then his sister Elisabeth.

Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche, the widow of an antisemitic colonist, controlled his archive. She assembled notebooks into a book called *The Will to Power* and promoted her brother as a prophet of German nationalism; Hitler visited the archive in 1934. Nietzsche himself despised antisemites and German nationalism; he broke with Wagner partly over them, and *Ecce Homo* mocks "the Germans" on almost every page. Recovering Nietzsche from his sister was the work of postwar scholars, above all Walter Kaufmann.

His books fall into three periods: the early Wagnerian works (*The Birth of Tragedy*, 1872; *Untimely Meditations*); the "free spirit" aphorism books (*Human, All Too Human*, *Daybreak*, *The Gay Science*); and the mature works (*Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, *Beyond Good and Evil*, *On the Genealogy of Morality*, *Twilight of the Idols*, *The Antichrist*, *Ecce Homo*). He wrote in aphorisms, parables, poems and polemics rather than treatises, and this is part of his philosophy: he distrusted systems, and he wanted readers who would think for themselves.

## The Greeks and the justification of life

*The Birth of Tragedy* argues that Greek tragedy arose from the union of two artistic drives, the **Apollonian** (form, measure, the dream image, individuality) and the **Dionysian** (intoxication, music, the dissolution of the individual into the whole). The Greeks were not serene; they knew Silenus's wisdom that it is best never to have been born. Tragedy was their way of facing that horror without denying life. Socrates killed tragedy by replacing it with the optimistic belief that reason can understand and correct existence. The book's most famous claim is Nietzsche's answer to Schopenhauer's pessimism:

> only as an _æsthetic phenomenon_ is existence and the world eternally _justified_
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Birth of Tragedy* §5 (tr. William A. Haussmann)

Life cannot be justified morally (as Leibniz's theodicy tried; [Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)) or rationally (as Hegel's history tried). It can be affirmed only the way we affirm a great tragedy: suffering included.

## The death of God

The phrase "God is dead" first appears in *The Gay Science* §108, but its famous dramatization is §125, "The Madman," who runs into the marketplace with a lantern in broad daylight crying "I seek God!" The bystanders, atheists all, laugh at him. He replies:

> "Where is God gone?" he called out. "I mean to tell you! _We have killed him_,—you and I! We are all his murderers! But how have we done it? How were we able to drink up the sea? Who gave us the sponge to wipe away the whole horizon? What did we do when we loosened this earth from its sun?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom* §125 (tr. Thomas Common)

And:

> God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him! How shall we console ourselves, the most murderous of all murderers?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom* §125 (tr. Thomas Common)

Notice who the madman is addressing. He is not arguing with believers; he is warning the complacent unbelievers, who think they can drop God and keep everything else: Christian morality, the value of truth, the belief that history has a direction, the dignity of every soul. "God is dead" is not a metaphysical claim (Nietzsche thought the arguments for God had long since failed) but a cultural diagnosis: belief in the Christian God "has become unworthy of belief" (§343), and the consequences have not yet arrived. When they do, the result will be **nihilism**, the devaluation of the highest values. That diagnosis is the subject of the nihilism course: [The death of God](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md).

## The history of an error

In a single page of *Twilight of the Idols* Nietzsche retells the history of Western philosophy as the story of one idea, the "true world" behind the world of appearance. It is a good review of this course so far:

> 1. The true world, attainable to the sage, the pious man and the man of virtue,—he lives in it, _he is it._
>
> (The most ancient form of the idea was relatively clever, simple, convincing. It was a paraphrase of the proposition "I, Plato, am the truth.")
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "How the 'True World' Ultimately Became a Fable" (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Stage 2 is Christianity: the true world is promised to the virtuous after death. Stage 3 is Kant ([Chapter 13](13-kant.md)): the true world is unknowable but still a comfort and a command, the old sun seen through mist, "pale, northern, Königsbergian." Stage 4 is positivism: unattainable, so unknown, so no longer binding. Stage 5 abolishes it as a useless idea, and "Plato blushes for shame." Then the twist:

> 6. We have suppressed the true world: what world survives? the apparent world perhaps?... Certainly not! _In abolishing the true world we have also abolished the world of appearance!_
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Twilight of the Idols*, "How the 'True World' Ultimately Became a Fable" (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Without a "true" world to contrast it with, this world is no longer "mere appearance." It is the only world, and it is real. The task is to find meaning here.

## Perspectivism and the will to truth

Nietzsche turns the same suspicion on philosophy itself. *Beyond Good and Evil* opens by asking why we want truth at all, rather than untruth, and suggests that philosophers' systems are not impersonal results:

> It has gradually become clear to me what every great philosophy up till now has consisted of--namely, the confession of its originator, and a species of involuntary and unconscious auto-biography
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* §6 (tr. Helen Zimmern)

He even denies that a belief's falsehood settles whether we should keep it:

> The falseness of an opinion is not for us any objection to it: it is here, perhaps, that our new language sounds most strangely. The question is, how far an opinion is life-furthering, life-preserving, species-preserving
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* §4 (tr. Helen Zimmern)

The *Genealogy* states his **perspectivism**: there is no "view from nowhere," no knowing subject outside every interest. But this is not the claim that every view is as good as any other:

> There is only a seeing from a perspective, only a "knowing" from a perspective, and the more emotions we express over a thing, the more eyes, different eyes, we train on the same thing, the more complete will be our "idea" of that thing, our "objectivity."
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals* III §12 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

Objectivity is the ability to see from many perspectives, not from none. A popular slogan, "there are no facts, only interpretations," comes from an unpublished notebook (included in the compiled *Will to Power*, §481); it should not be treated as Nietzsche's settled doctrine without that context. The theme of "will to power," which he sometimes extended speculatively to all of nature (*Beyond Good and Evil* §36), was likewise developed mostly in notebooks his sister later published.

## The genealogy of morality

Nietzsche's most influential argument is historical-psychological. Morality has a history, and the history is not flattering. *Beyond Good and Evil* §260 distinguishes two types:

> There is MASTER-MORALITY and SLAVE-MORALITY,--I would at once add, however, that in all higher and mixed civilizations, there are also attempts at the reconciliation of the two moralities
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Beyond Good and Evil* §260 (tr. Helen Zimmern)

In **master morality**, the noble call themselves "good" (strong, proud, healthy, beautiful) and call others "bad" in the sense of low and pitiable, as an afterthought. In **slave morality** the order is reversed. The oppressed, unable to act against their masters, begin by calling the masters "evil," and only then call themselves "good" because they are not like them: meek, humble, patient. This reversal is driven by *ressentiment*, a resentment that cannot discharge itself in action:

> The revolt of the slaves in morals begins in the very principle of _resentment_ becoming creative and giving birth to values--a resentment experienced by creatures who, deprived as they are of the proper outlet of action, are forced to find their compensation in an imaginary revenge.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals* I §10 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

His parable is the lambs and the birds of prey:

> It is not surprising that the lambs should bear a grudge against the great birds of prey, but that is no reason for blaming the great birds of prey for taking the little lambs.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals* I §13 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

The lambs then invent the idea of a free "subject" behind every deed, so that the bird of prey can be blamed for being a bird of prey. Second, the Second Essay traces guilt and bad conscience to debt and to instincts turned inward. Third, the Third Essay asks why the **ascetic ideal** (self-denial, poverty, chastity, the scholar's selfless devotion to truth) has had such power. Its answer is that suffering itself was never the problem for human beings; meaningless suffering was. The ascetic ideal gave suffering a meaning, even at the cost of turning against life:

> man's will, his _horror vacui: he needs a goal_--and he will sooner will nothingness than not will at all.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals* III §1 (tr. Horace B. Samuel)

The genealogy is not meant to refute morality, which would be the "genetic fallacy." It is meant to loosen its grip, to show that values we take as given were made, by particular types of people for particular needs, and to raise the question of their **value**: do they make for healthier, more creative human lives or not? For the full argument and its critics, see [Nietzsche and the critique of morality](../learn-ethics/15-nietzsche-and-the-critique-of-morality.md).

## Beyond nihilism: Zarathustra, recurrence, amor fati

If God is dead and morality has a shameful pedigree, what remains? Nietzsche's answers are images more than doctrines.

**The Übermensch.** Zarathustra, a prophet who comes down from ten years of solitude in the mountains, tells the people in the market:

> I TEACH YOU THE SUPERMAN. Man is something that is to be surpassed. What have ye done to surpass man?
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue §3 (tr. Thomas Common)

> The Superman is the meaning of the earth. Let your will say: The Superman SHALL BE the meaning of the earth!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue §3 (tr. Thomas Common)

"Superman" is Common's rendering of *Übermensch* ("overhuman"); Kaufmann's "overman" avoids the comic-book associations. The Übermensch is not a biological race but an ideal of self-overcoming: a human being who creates values rather than receiving them. Its opposite is the **last man**, the contented, comfortable, risk-averse human being of a spiritless modernity:

> "We have discovered happiness"—say the last men, and blink thereby.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, Prologue §5 (tr. Thomas Common)

The crowd's response is to shout, "Give us this last man!"

**The three metamorphoses.** Zarathustra's first speech describes how the spirit becomes a camel, which kneels to take on the heaviest loads (duty, reverence, "thou shalt"); then a lion, which fights the dragon "Thou shalt" and wins freedom with a sacred "No"; and finally a child:

> Innocence is the child, and forgetfulness, a new beginning, a game, a self-rolling wheel, a first movement, a holy Yea.
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Thus Spake Zarathustra*, "The Three Metamorphoses" (tr. Thomas Common)

The lion can destroy old values but cannot create new ones; that takes the child's innocent, playful affirmation.

**Eternal recurrence.** In *The Gay Science* §341 Nietzsche proposes a thought experiment:

> What if a demon crept after thee into thy loneliest loneliness some day or night, and said to thee: "This life, as thou livest it at present, and hast lived it, thou must live it once more, and also innumerable times; and there will be nothing new in it, but every pain and every joy and every thought and every sigh, and all the unspeakably small and great in thy life must come to thee again
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom* §341 (tr. Thomas Common)

Would you gnash your teeth and curse the demon, or answer, "Thou art a God, and never did I hear aught more divine!"? Whatever its status as cosmology (Nietzsche toyed with proofs in his notebooks), its use is as a test of how far one affirms one's life, all of it, without reservation.

**Amor fati.** The ideal is not merely to bear what is necessary but to love it:

> I want more and more to perceive the necessary characters in things as the beautiful:—I shall thus be one of those who beautify things. _Amor fati_: let that henceforth be my love!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom* §276 (tr. Thomas Common)

> _amor fati_: the fact that a man wishes nothing to be different, either in front of him or behind him, or for all eternity. Not only must the necessary be borne, and on no account concealed,--all idealism is falsehood in the face of necessity,--but it must also be _loved_
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *Ecce Homo*, "Why I Am So Clever" §10 (tr. Anthony M. Ludovici)

Compare the Stoics' acceptance of fate ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md) and the [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/README.md)); the difference is that Nietzsche has no providential cosmos to make fate good. And alongside amor fati comes a demand to shape oneself:

> To "give style" to one's character—that is a grand and a rare art!
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Joyful Wisdom* §290 (tr. Thomas Common)

These are developed in the nihilism course: [Beyond nihilism](../learn-nihilism/07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md).

## Quotations and misquotations

Nietzsche is the most quoted and misquoted philosopher on the internet. Some checks:

- **"What doesn't kill you makes you stronger."** The original is first-personal and comes from "Maxims and Missiles" §8 in *Twilight*: "From the military school of life.—That which does not kill me, makes me stronger." It is about Nietzsche's own sickness, not a general law (much that does not kill us leaves us weaker).
- **"He who fights with monsters..."** is genuine: "He who fights with monsters should be careful lest he thereby become a monster. And if thou gaze long into an abyss, the abyss will also gaze into thee." (*Beyond Good and Evil* §146, tr. Zimmern).
- **"Without music life would be a mistake."** Genuine (*Twilight*, "Maxims and Missiles" §33).
- **"Those who were seen dancing were thought to be insane by those who could not hear the music."** Misattributed; it does not appear in Nietzsche's works.
- **"There are no facts, only interpretations."** From the notebooks (see above), not a published work.
- **"I am not a man, I am dynamite."** Genuine (*Ecce Homo*, "Why I Am a Fatality" §1), and Nietzsche adds at once that there is "nought of the founder of a religion" in him.

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Naturalist or postmodernist?** French readers in the 1960s (Deleuze, Foucault, Derrida) made Nietzsche the patron of a philosophy of difference and interpretation, suspicious of truth itself ([Chapter 23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md)). Anglophone scholars since the 1990s (Maudemarie Clark, Brian Leiter) read him as a philosophical naturalist who explains morality psychologically and causally, and whose perspectivism is compatible with ordinary truth.

**Is there a positive ethics?** Some readers find a virtue ethics of nobility, self-overcoming and creativity (Christine Swanton, Bernard Reginster). Others think Nietzsche's critique is aimed at the "higher types" only, and offers no general ethics (Leiter). Still others read him as an existentialist, demanding that each person create values ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)).

**Politics.** Nietzsche's contempt for democracy, socialism and equality, and his praise of "breeding" and rank, are real and not all of his sister's making. Readers disagree about whether these are separable from his psychology and his ideal of self-overcoming. Kaufmann's apolitical Nietzsche is now widely thought too tame.

## Living it

**Run the demon test.** Imagine living your last week again, exactly, forever. What would you be glad to repeat? What would make you gnash your teeth? Nietzsche's point is not guilt but direction: the answers show what to change and, more importantly, what to affirm.

**Do a genealogy of one of your values.** Pick something you feel strongly is good or bad (ambition, modesty, thrift, forgiveness). Where did you get it? Whom did it serve? Does it make your life stronger and more creative, or is it, in Nietzsche's sense, *ressentiment*: a way of condemning what you cannot have? The genealogy does not settle whether to keep it, but you will hold it differently.

**Give style to your character.** Write down one weakness that you cannot simply remove. Nietzsche suggests that a stylish character is one in which weaknesses are not hidden but given a place in a coherent whole. Where could yours fit?

## Summary

- Nietzsche: a philologist turned philosopher who wrote in aphorisms, wanted to philosophize with a hammer and was misappropriated by his sister and the Nazis.
- *The Birth of Tragedy*: Apollonian and Dionysian; existence justified only as an aesthetic phenomenon.
- The death of God: a cultural diagnosis addressed to complacent unbelievers; its consequence is nihilism.
- The "true world" as the history of an error from Plato through Christianity and Kant to positivism.
- Perspectivism: objectivity as many perspectives, not none; philosophies as confessions; the value of truth questioned.
- The genealogy of morality: master and slave morality, *ressentiment*, the lambs and birds of prey, the ascetic ideal, "will nothingness rather than not will."
- Beyond nihilism: the Übermensch and the last man; camel, lion and child; eternal recurrence as a test; amor fati; giving style to one's character.
- Watch out for misquotations.

### Dialogue 1 — On the demon's offer

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** Suppose a demon told you that you must live your life again, every pain and every joy, innumerable times. What would you say?

**You:** Honestly? I'd be horrified. There are years I would never want to live through again.

*Your turn: Before reading on, decide whether Nietzsche's thought experiment is meant to make you feel guilty, to tell you something about the universe, or something else. Write down your answer.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** Which years?

**You:** A long illness when I was a teenager. And the year my father died.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** Would you be who you are without them?

**You:** No. I learned patience from the illness. And I understood my father better after he died than when he was alive.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** Then to wish them away is to wish yourself away. That is the meaning of the demon's question: can you want your life *as a whole*, since its parts cannot be separated?

**You:** But that sounds like saying suffering is good. My father's death wasn't good.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** I did not say good. Morality wants to sort the world into good and evil and then condemn the evil. I ask you to love what is necessary, not to approve of it. I was sick most of my adult life. My sickness taught me to think.

**You:** You can say that about your own suffering. But what about suffering that teaches nothing, like a child dying of cancer?

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** I will not give you a theodicy. That is Leibniz's game. The demon does not ask you to justify the world. He asks whether *you* can say yes to life, including what you cannot justify.

**You:** Then it's not a claim about the universe but a test of my attitude.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** In the *Gay Science*, yes. Notice that I wrote "If that thought acquired power over thee... it would transform thee." Its effect is what matters.

**You:** Here's where I come out. I can't affirm every suffering, and I'm suspicious of anyone who says they do. But I can see that the parts of my life I'd most like to erase are woven into the parts I value most. The test shows me that my horror at repetition is partly a refusal to accept who I am. That's worth working on.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** A camel would have said "I must accept." A lion would have said "No." You are, perhaps, on the way to a child's "yes."

### Dialogue 2 — On whether compassion is ressentiment

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** You admire compassion, humility and forgiveness. Have you ever asked where those values came from?

**You:** From Christianity, I suppose, and from people who saw that cruelty and pride cause suffering.

*Your turn: Nietzsche will argue that these values arose from the resentment of the weak. Even if his history were right, would that show the values are bad? Write two or three sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** They came from those who could not strike back. The lambs called the birds of prey evil and their own weakness good. Humility made a virtue of necessity.

**You:** Suppose that's the history. It wouldn't follow that compassion is bad. That's the genetic fallacy: where an idea came from doesn't settle whether it's true or good.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** Agreed. I said the genealogy raises the question of the value of values. It does not answer it. So answer it: does your compassion make you stronger or weaker?

**You:** It depends. Sometimes it makes me act: I help someone. Sometimes it's just a feeling of pity that makes me feel virtuous and changes nothing.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** And sometimes it makes the other person smaller? Pity can humiliate those it touches.

**You:** Yes. I've felt that when people pitied me during my illness. I wanted to be treated as capable.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** And humility?

**You:** Sometimes humility is honest. Sometimes it's a way of avoiding risk, so no one can say I failed. And I've seen people use "being humble" to sneer at people who are ambitious.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** There is the *ressentiment*. You condemn what you do not dare to attempt.

**You:** But I don't accept your master morality either. The "noble" who treat others as cattle aren't admirable to me. You admired Napoleon; I don't.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** I admired the strength, not the victims.

**You:** Here's where I come out. Your genealogy is a good test to apply to my own motives: there is compassion that strengthens, and pity that diminishes; humility that is honest, and humility that is fear dressed up. I want the first of each pair. But I reject the idea that strength is admirable regardless of how it treats the weak, and nothing in your genealogy forces me to accept that.

**Friedrich Nietzsche:** Then you have done what I asked. You have not believed me; you have examined yourself. My books were written for readers like that.

</details>
