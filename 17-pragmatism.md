# Chapter 17 — Pragmatism

Pragmatism is the first philosophical movement born in the United States, and its central idea fits on an index card: to find out what an idea means, ask what difference it would make if it were true. That sounds modest. It turned out to be a solvent strong enough to dissolve some of the oldest disputes in metaphysics, to rewrite the theory of truth, to give a new account of religious belief, and to recast democracy as a method of collective inquiry rather than a voting procedure. This chapter follows the three founders, Charles Sanders Peirce, William James and John Dewey, through their main texts, sets out the objections that nearly sank them (Bertrand Russell and G. E. Moore were merciless), and ends with the revival led by Richard Rorty and the "new pragmatists" who argue with him. Along the way you get a practical tool you can use on your own beliefs this week. Previous: [Chapter 16 — Utilitarianism and Liberalism](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md). Next: [Chapter 18 — The Analytic Turn](18-the-analytic-turn.md).

## A club in Cambridge, a war behind it

In the early 1870s a handful of young men met in Cambridge, Massachusetts, to argue about philosophy. They called themselves, half in mockery, the Metaphysical Club. The members included Charles Sanders Peirce, a brilliant and prickly logician employed by the U.S. Coast Survey; William James, a medical graduate who had not yet decided whether he was a painter, a physiologist or a psychologist; Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr., who had been shot three times as a Union officer and would become the most famous judge in American history; the lawyer Nicholas St. John Green; and Chauncey Wright, a mathematician who computed tables for the *Nautical Almanac* and argued Darwin's case against every metaphysician he met. Decades later Peirce recalled it in a letter:

> In the sixties I started a little club called 'The Metaphysical Club.'—Wright was the strongest member and probably I was next.—Then there were Frank Abbott, William James and others.
>
> — Peirce, letter to Christine Ladd-Franklin, quoted in *Chance, Love, and Logic*, ed. Cohen, Introduction, note 11

(Peirce's memory of the date wobbles; the club's documented meetings are from 1872.) He added that "it was there that the name and the doctrine of pragmatism saw the light." Green kept pressing on them a definition from the Scottish psychologist Alexander Bain: a belief is that upon which a person is prepared to act. Peirce later said that pragmatism was little more than a corollary of Bain's definition. If believing is a readiness to act, then the content of a belief is fixed by the actions it would guide.

Louis Menand's prize-winning *The Metaphysical Club* (2001) adds a historical thesis: these men had lived through the Civil War, in which absolute certainties on both sides had produced some 620,000 to 750,000 dead, and pragmatism is a philosophy designed to keep ideas from hardening into causes worth killing for. Ideas are tools, judged by how well they work and revised when they fail. Scholars such as Misak think Menand overstates the political motive, since Peirce's motives were overwhelmingly technical, but both readings converge on the core: no belief is beyond revision.

## Peirce: doubt, belief and four ways to stop worrying

Charles Sanders Peirce (1839–1914; the name is pronounced "purse") was the son of Harvard's leading mathematician and probably the most original philosopher America has produced. He invented a notation for quantifiers independently of Frege, anticipated the logic of relations, founded modern semiotics, and wrote about statistics, geodesy and the history of science. He was also impossible to employ. He lost his one academic post, a lectureship at Johns Hopkins, in 1884, apparently after it emerged that he had lived with his second wife before his divorce was final, and died poor in Milford, Pennsylvania, leaving some 100,000 manuscript pages that scholars are still editing. Most of what he published in his lifetime came out as magazine articles. Two of them, in *Popular Science Monthly* in 1877 and 1878, are the founding documents of the movement.

"The Fixation of Belief" starts with a psychological contrast. Doubt and belief feel different and do different things. Belief is calm and guides action; doubt is an uneasy state we struggle to leave.

> The irritation of doubt causes a struggle to attain a state of belief. I shall term this struggle *inquiry*, though it must be admitted that this is sometimes not a very apt designation.
>
> — Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief" §IV, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

So far, so deflationary. Peirce then says something that sounds shocking: the "sole object of inquiry is the settlement of opinion." We may fancy that we want true opinion rather than merely firm opinion, but "as soon as a firm belief is reached we are entirely satisfied, whether the belief be false or true." The question then becomes: of the various ways of settling opinion, which actually works? Peirce lists four.

1. **Tenacity.** Hold on to whatever you already believe and avoid anything that might shake it. Peirce gives this method its due, with a famous image:

   > When an ostrich buries its head in the sand as danger approaches, it very likely takes the happiest course. It hides the danger, and then calmly says there is no danger; and, if it feels perfectly sure there is none, why should it raise its head to see?
   >
   > — Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief" §V, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

   The method fails in practice, not in logic: "the social impulse is against it." We meet people who think otherwise, and the doubt returns.
2. **Authority.** Let an institution, a state or a church fix what everyone believes, and suppress dissent. This works far better and has governed most of human history. But no authority can regulate every opinion, and people who compare societies see that their beliefs are an accident of birthplace.
3. **The *a priori* method.** Believe what is "agreeable to reason," what fits together elegantly in a system. This is how metaphysicians work, and Peirce says it makes inquiry into "something similar to the development of taste": fashion swings back and forth, and the method never settles anything.
4. **The method of science.** Fix belief by something outside us, something on which our thinking has no effect.

The fourth method rests on a hypothesis:

> There are real things, whose characters are entirely independent of our opinions about them; whose realities affect our senses according to regular laws, and, though our sensations are as different as our relations to the objects, yet, by taking advantage of the laws of perception, we can ascertain by reasoning how things really are, and any man, if he have sufficient experience and reason enough about it, will be led to the one true conclusion.
>
> — Peirce, "The Fixation of Belief" §V, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

Peirce admits that he cannot prove this hypothesis by the method it supports (that would be circular). He argues instead that the practice never produces doubts about the hypothesis, that every method of settling belief already presupposes that there is "some *one* thing to which a proposition should conform," and that the other three methods undermine themselves. This is an early *transcendental* or *presuppositional* argument: whoever is inquiring at all is already committed to realism about the object of inquiry.

### Anti-Cartesianism and fallibilism

Ten years earlier, in "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" (1868), Peirce had attacked the starting point of modern philosophy, Descartes' method of universal doubt (see [Chapter 11 — Descartes and the Rationalists](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). Real doubt needs a reason. You cannot doubt at will, any more than you can decide to be frightened. Paper doubt is theater:

> Let us not pretend to doubt in philosophy what we do not doubt in our hearts.
>
> — Peirce, "Some Consequences of Four Incapacities" (1868), excerpted as "The Rules of Philosophy," in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

The positive side of this is **fallibilism**: any of our beliefs might turn out wrong, but we doubt them only when experience gives us a reason. Inquiry does not start from zero and build up on unshakable foundations. It starts in the middle, from everything we currently believe, and repairs the ship at sea. Peirce's later slogan, which he wanted "inscribed upon every wall of the city of philosophy," was *Do not block the way of inquiry* (from an unpublished manuscript of about 1899, now *Collected Papers* 1.135). Peirce also gave the first careful account of a third kind of inference alongside deduction and induction: **abduction** (he also called it *retroduction* or hypothesis), the inference to an explanatory guess. You see wet streets and infer rain. Abduction is how new ideas enter science; induction then tests them. Philosophers of science now call the refined version "inference to the best explanation" (see [Chapter 21 — Knowledge and Science](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

## The pragmatic maxim

"How to Make Our Ideas Clear" distinguishes three grades of clarity. The first is familiarity: you can use the word "hard" correctly. The second is definition: you can say what "hard" means in other words, as the rationalists demanded. Peirce's third grade is new:

> Consider what effects, which might conceivably have practical bearings, we conceive the object of our conception to have. Then, our conception of these effects is the whole of our conception of the object.
>
> — Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" §II, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

This is the **pragmatic maxim**. Apply it to hardness and you get a test, not a definition: a hard thing is one that "will not be scratched by many other substances." Behind the maxim lies the Bain-Green view of belief as habit:

> Thus, we come down to what is tangible and practical, as the root of every real distinction of thought, no matter how subtile it may be; and there is no distinction of meaning so fine as to consist in anything but a possible difference of practice.
>
> — Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" §II, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

The maxim bites. Peirce's provocative example is the Eucharist. Catholics say the bread becomes the body of Christ while keeping all the sensible properties of bread; Protestants deny it. Peirce:

> It is foolish for Catholics and Protestants to fancy themselves in disagreement about the elements of the sacrament, if they agree in regard to all their sensible effects, here or hereafter.
>
> — Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" §II, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

Here is the argument in standard form:

1. The meaning of a concept is exhausted by the conceivable practical effects of its object (the maxim).
2. Two concepts whose objects have the same conceivable practical effects therefore have the same meaning.
3. "Bread" and "body of Christ under the appearance of bread" are said to have the same conceivable effects.
4. So the two claims do not differ in meaning, and the dispute is verbal.

A Catholic theologian will reject premise 3 (the effects include grace, which is "hereafter" and not sensible), or premise 1. That is exactly the right place for the argument to go: the maxim forces disputants to say what their difference *comes to*. Readers of [Chapter 18 — The Analytic Turn](18-the-analytic-turn.md) will see the logical positivists' verification principle as a stricter cousin of this maxim. The difference is that Peirce counts "conceivable" effects and habits of action, not only observations, and he never declared metaphysics meaningless as such. He wanted it cleaned up, not abolished.

### The hidden diamond and the end of inquiry

Peirce immediately hits a hard case. Suppose a diamond is crystallized inside a cushion of soft cotton and burned up before anyone ever presses on it. "Would it be false to say that that diamond was soft?" In 1878 Peirce said the question was merely about how to talk. Later in life he regretted this answer, and the regret matters. If hardness is a *would-be*, a real disposition that holds whether or not it is ever tested, then pragmatism needs real general facts about what would happen, not only what does happen. Peirce called this his "scholastic realism," and it is one reason he later renamed his doctrine. In a 1905 article in *The Monist* he announced the word "pragmaticism," which he hoped was ugly enough to keep kidnappers away (by then James and the Italian and British pragmatists had run off with the original).

The maxim applied to "truth" and "reality" gives Peirce's most quoted definition. He imagines investigators measuring the speed of light by unrelated methods, getting different answers at first, and converging over time:

> The opinion which is fated to be ultimately agreed to by all who investigate, is what we mean by the truth, and the object represented in this opinion is the real. That is the way I would explain reality.
>
> — Peirce, "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" §IV, in *Chance, Love, and Logic*

Two objections arrive at once.

**The buried-secrets objection.** Some truths will never be found. How many hairs were on Caesar's head when he crossed the Rubicon? No inquiry will ever settle it, so on Peirce's definition there seems to be no truth of the matter. Peirce says that inquiry "if only pushed far enough" would settle such questions, which invites the worry that "far enough" smuggles truth back into the definition. Misak's reconstruction is the most influential reply: a true belief is one that *would not be improved* by further inquiry, however long it continued. That is a claim about what belief aims at, not a reductive definition, and it leaves room for questions that have no answer.

**The independence objection.** Truth is supposed to be independent of what anyone thinks; Peirce makes it what investigators think. He saw this one coming. Reality, he replies, "is independent, not necessarily of thought in general, but only of what you or I or any finite number of men may think about it." The final opinion is not up to anyone.

## James: temperament, squirrels and cash value

Peirce's essays sank without a trace for twenty years. Then, in an 1898 lecture at Berkeley, his friend William James (1842–1910) gave the movement its name in public, credited Peirce, and made it famous. James was everything Peirce was not: a beloved Harvard professor, a stylist (his brother was the novelist Henry James, and people joked that William wrote psychology like a novelist while Henry wrote novels like a psychologist), and a man who had come through a suicidal depression in his late twenties partly by deciding to believe in free will. His *Principles of Psychology* (1890) founded American psychology. *Pragmatism* (1907), a set of public lectures, was a bestseller.

James opens not with logic but with people.

> The history of philosophy is to a great extent that of a certain clash of human temperaments.
>
> — William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture I

He draws up two columns. The **tender-minded** are "Rationalistic (going by 'principles'), Intellectualistic, Idealistic, Optimistic, Religious, Free-willist, Monistic, Dogmatical." The **tough-minded** are "Empiricist (going by 'facts'), Sensationalistic, Materialistic, Pessimistic, Irreligious, Fatalistic, Pluralistic, Sceptical." Most of us, James observes, want something from both columns: the facts of science and the hopes of religion. Pragmatism is offered as a mediator.

### The squirrel

Lecture II begins with one of the best stories in the history of philosophy. On a camping trip James came back from a walk to find his friends in a "ferocious metaphysical dispute." A squirrel clings to one side of a tree; a man walks around the tree trying to see it; the squirrel keeps circling so that the tree stays between them. Does the man go round the squirrel? The campers were evenly split and furious. James settled it with a distinction:

> "Which party is right," I said, "depends on what you *practically mean* by 'going round' the squirrel. If you mean passing from the north of him to the east, then to the south, then to the west, and then to the north of him again, obviously the man does go round him, for he occupies these successive positions. But if on the contrary you mean being first in front of him, then on the right of him, then behind him, then on his left, and finally in front again, it is quite as obvious that the man fails to go round him..."
>
> — William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture II

Some of the hotter disputants, James reports, called this "a shuffling evasion," but most were satisfied. The story is trivial, he admits, but it is a model of "the pragmatic method," which is "primarily a method of settling metaphysical disputes that otherwise might be interminable." The method asks of every grand word what work it does:

> You must bring out of each word its practical cash-value, set it at work within the stream of your experience. It appears less as a solution, then, than as a program for more work, and more particularly as an indication of the ways in which existing realities may be *changed*.
>
> — William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture II

"Cash-value" was a gift to James's critics, who took it as proof that pragmatism was the philosophy of the American businessman. James meant a metaphor for convertibility: a banknote is worth something because it can be exchanged for goods; a concept is worth something because it can be exchanged for experiences. He borrowed a second image from the Italian pragmatist Giovanni Papini: pragmatism

> lies in the midst of our theories, like a corridor in a hotel. Innumerable chambers open out of it. In one you may find a man writing an atheistic volume; in the next someone on his knees praying for faith and strength; in a third a chemist investigating a body's properties.
>
> — William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture II

The corridor image makes pragmatism a method that is neutral between doctrines. James did not keep that neutrality for long.

### Truth happens to an idea

In Lecture VI James turns the method on truth itself. The traditional view, the **correspondence theory**, says a belief is true when it agrees with reality. James does not deny that, but he asks what "agreement" amounts to in practice:

> The truth of an idea is not a stagnant property inherent in it. Truth *happens* to an idea. It *becomes* true, is *made* true by events. Its verity is in fact an event, a process: the process namely of its verifying itself, its veri-*fication*.
>
> — William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture VI

And the most notorious summary:

> 'The true,' to put it very briefly, is only the expedient in the way of our thinking, just as 'the right' is only the expedient in the way of our behaving.
>
> — William James, *Pragmatism*, Lecture VI

Earlier he had put it this way: "The true is the name of whatever proves itself to be good in the way of belief, and good, too, for definite, assignable reasons" (Lecture II). Read carelessly, this says that whatever is comforting to believe is true. Read with the qualifications James immediately adds, it is more interesting. "Expedient" means expedient "in the long run and on the whole," and experience "has ways of *boiling over*, and making us correct our present formulas." The absolutely true, "meaning what no farther experience will ever alter," remains as an "ideal vanishing-point towards which we imagine that all our temporary truths will some day converge." That is Peirce's end of inquiry, kept as a regulative ideal.

## The trouble with truth: Russell, Moore and James's reply

The British reaction was swift. In 1908 Bertrand Russell published "Transatlantic Truth" (later reprinted as "William James's Conception of Truth" in his *Philosophical Essays*, 1910), and G. E. Moore read a long paper, "Professor James' 'Pragmatism,'" to the Aristotelian Society.

**Russell's first objection: the criterion is harder than the fact.** If "p is true" means "believing p has good consequences," then to know whether p is true I must know the consequences of believing it, which is usually much harder than knowing p. Russell's example: it is far easier to settle whether popes have always been infallible than to settle whether the effects of believing in their infallibility are on the whole good.

**Russell's second objection: the existence slide.** On James's definition, "A exists" could be true even if A does not exist, provided believing it worked well; Russell later used Santa Claus as the example (*History of Western Philosophy*, 1945).

**Moore's objection: verification and utility come apart from truth.** Moore separated James's claims into four: that all true ideas can be verified, that all verifiable ideas are true, that all true ideas are useful, and that all useful ideas are true. He argued that each is false. Against the first:

> It seems to me, then, that we very often have true ideas which we cannot verify; true ideas, which, in all probability, no man ever will be able to verify.
>
> — G. E. Moore, "Professor James' 'Pragmatism'" (1908), in *Philosophical Studies*

His examples were humdrum on purpose, such as disputed historical events whose evidence has vanished. And useful falsehoods are everywhere, from placebos to flattering self-images.

James answered in *The Meaning of Truth* (1909), with visible irritation:

> We affirm nothing as silly as Mr. Russell supposes. Good consequences are not proposed by us merely as a sure sign, mark, or criterion, by which truth's presence is habitually ascertained, tho they may indeed serve on occasion as such a sign; they are proposed rather as the lurking motive inside of every truth-claim...
>
> — William James, *The Meaning of Truth*, "Two English Critics"

He was offering not a test to apply before believing but an account of why we have the concept of truth at all. Pragmatists, he insisted, assume a real world that their beliefs must deal with; a belief about A "works" only if it leads us successfully to A.

How should you score this exchange? Interpreters split three ways.

- **The critics' reading** (Russell, Moore, most analytic philosophers until the 1970s): James defined truth as utility, and obvious counterexamples refute him.
- **The charitable reading** (Hilary Putnam and Ruth Anna Putnam, among others): James described how truth functions in inquiry and presupposed correspondence rather than replacing it; his rhetoric outran his doctrine.
- **The Peircean reading** (Misak): James slid from Peirce's long-run, community-wide notion of truth to the short-run, individual "what works for me," and the slide is a real error.

A fair verdict is that James had a defensible position (truth is the property of beliefs that would survive all further experience, and our concept of it earns its keep by guiding action) and an indefensible slogan ("the true is only the expedient"). The slogan is what people remember.

## The will to believe

James's most influential essay predates *Pragmatism*. "The Will to Believe" (1896) was addressed to students at Yale and Brown and aimed at a single opponent: the English mathematician W. K. Clifford, whose essay "The Ethics of Belief" (1877) had become the manifesto of Victorian scientific agnosticism.

### Clifford's shipowner

Clifford begins with a story. A shipowner is about to send an old emigrant ship to sea. He has doubts about her seaworthiness. He talks himself out of them, trusting Providence and the honesty of builders:

> In such ways he acquired a sincere and comfortable conviction that his vessel was thoroughly safe and seaworthy; he watched her departure with a light heart, and benevolent wishes for the success of the exiles in their strange new home that was to be; and he got his insurance-money when she went down in mid-ocean and told no tales.
>
> — W. K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" §I, in *The Scientific Basis of Morals*

Clifford's verdict: he "was verily guilty of the death of those men," and his sincerity is no excuse, "because he had no right to believe on such evidence as was before him." Then Clifford adds a twist. Suppose the ship had arrived safely. The shipowner would be exactly as guilty, because his fault lay in how he formed the belief, not in its outcome. From this Clifford draws a universal principle, today called **evidentialism**:

> To sum up: it is wrong always, everywhere and for any one, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.
>
> — W. K. Clifford, "The Ethics of Belief" §I, in *The Scientific Basis of Morals*

(James's own quotation of this line in "The Will to Believe" reads "for every one"; Clifford's text has "for any one." Small misquotations of famous lines are a hazard even for great philosophers.)

### James's options

James accepts that most questions should be settled by evidence. His argument concerns a special class of choices, and it needs vocabulary. A hypothesis is **live** if it "appeals as a real possibility" to the person considering it; liveness is relative to a thinker and "measured by his willingness to act." A choice between hypotheses, an **option**, is *genuine* when it is living, **forced** (there is no standing outside it: "either accept this truth or go without it") and **momentous** (the opportunity is unique, the stakes significant, the decision irreversible). Now the thesis:

> *Our passional nature not only lawfully may, but must, decide an option between propositions, whenever it is a genuine option that cannot by its nature be decided on intellectual grounds; for to say, under such circumstances, "Do not decide, but leave the question open," is itself a passional decision,—just like deciding yes or no,—and is attended with the same risk of losing the truth.*
>
> — William James, "The Will to Believe" §IV

Put as an argument:

1. In a genuine option that evidence cannot settle, you must either believe, disbelieve, or suspend judgment.
2. Suspending judgment has the same practical effect as disbelieving (you lose the good the belief would bring if true).
3. So suspension is not neutral; it is itself a choice made on non-evidential grounds, namely a preference for avoiding error over gaining truth.
4. Nothing in reason requires that preference over the opposite one.
5. So in such options it is permissible to let your "passional nature" (desires, hopes, temperament) decide.

Premise 4 is the philosophical heart, and James states it with one of his best lines:

> Believe truth! Shun error!—these, we see, are two materially different laws; and by choosing between them we may end by coloring differently our whole intellectual life.
>
> — William James, "The Will to Believe" §VII

Clifford, James says, is a man whose horror of being duped rules him; but "worse things than being duped may happen to a man in this world." Clifford's advice "is like a general informing his soldiers that it is better to keep out of battle forever than to risk a single wound."

James adds a second kind of case: facts that faith helps to bring about. Will this person like me? Will this team pull together?

> There are, then, cases where a fact cannot come at all unless a preliminary faith exists in its coming. *And where faith in a fact can help create the fact*, that would be an insane logic which should say that faith running ahead of scientific evidence is the 'lowest kind of immorality' into which a thinking being can fall.
>
> — William James, "The Will to Believe" §IX

### Objections and the contemporary debate

- **The license objection.** James seems to permit wishful thinking. He replies that his conditions are strict: the option must be live, forced and momentous, and evidence must be unable to settle it. Critics answer that people can talk themselves into thinking almost anything meets these conditions.
- **The doxastic voluntarism objection.** Can you believe at will? Try believing right now that it is raining in your room. Most philosophers since Bernard Williams's "Deciding to Believe" (1970) agree that you cannot directly. James's defenders reply that he meant something gentler: you can choose to act on a hypothesis, attend to it, and let belief follow, as Pascal recommended with his wager.
- **The religious objection.** Is religion a forced option? An agnostic seems to occupy a stable middle position. James's answer is that if religion's promised goods come only to those who commit, agnosticism forfeits them exactly as disbelief does.

The debate is alive. Contemporary **pragmatic encroachment** theorists (Jeremy Fantl and Matthew McGrath, *Knowledge in an Uncertain World*, 2009) argue that whether you *know* something can depend on how much is at stake for you. **Moral encroachment** theorists (Rima Basu and others) argue that moral considerations can bear on what you ought to believe about people. Susanna Rinard defends a thoroughgoing pragmatism about belief. Evidentialists such as Richard Feldman and Earl Conee hold Clifford's line. The ethics of belief is one of the places where pragmatism still sets the terms.

### Religion by its fruits

James's own answer to the religious question came in *The Varieties of Religious Experience* (1902), the Gifford Lectures. James defines religion deliberately narrowly, as

> *the feelings, acts, and experiences of individual men in their solitude, so far as they apprehend themselves to stand in relation to whatever they may consider the divine*.
>
> — William James, *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, Lecture II

He contrasts the **healthy-minded**, "who need to be born only once," with the **sick souls**, "who must be twice-born in order to be happy" (Lecture VIII). The twice-born have looked into the abyss (James quotes his own anonymous case history of panic fear) and found that happiness requires a second birth through despair. Readers of the Nihilism course will recognize the territory; see [the Nihilism course, Chapter 15](../learn-nihilism/15-beyond-nihilism.md) for where these responses lead.

James then applies the pragmatic method to religious experience itself. Its value must be judged, as Jonathan Edwards had said, "By their fruits ye shall know them, not by their roots" (Lecture I). Whether a mystical state comes from God or from a nervous disorder does not decide its worth; its effects on the life do. In Lecture XVI he lists four marks of mystical states: **ineffability**, a **noetic quality** (they seem to be states of knowledge), **transiency**, and **passivity**. He concludes that mystical states are authoritative for the person who has them and not for anyone else. It is a careful, modest conclusion.

## Dewey: inquiry, habit and democracy

John Dewey (1859–1952) lived long enough to see the Civil War's aftermath and the atomic bomb. He began as a Hegelian, became the leading philosopher of American progressivism, founded the University of Chicago Laboratory School, co-founded the American Civil Liberties Union and the New School, chaired the commission in Mexico City that investigated Stalin's charges against Trotsky (at 77), and wrote on logic, ethics, art, education, religion and politics. He called his version of pragmatism **instrumentalism**: ideas are instruments for transforming problematic situations.

### Inquiry

Dewey's *How We Think* (1910), written for teachers, defines reflective thought as

> *Active, persistent, and careful consideration of any belief or supposed form of knowledge in the light of the grounds that support it, and the further conclusions to which it tends*...
>
> — John Dewey, *How We Think*, chapter 1

and then breaks it into stages:

> Upon examination, each instance reveals, more or less clearly, five logically distinct steps: (*i*) a felt difficulty; (*ii*) its location and definition; (*iii*) suggestion of possible solution; (*iv*) development by reasoning of the bearings of the suggestion; (*v*) further observation and experiment leading to its acceptance or rejection; that is, the conclusion of belief or disbelief.
>
> — John Dewey, *How We Think*, chapter 6

This is Peirce's doubt-belief cycle turned into a general theory of intelligent action. In the mature *Logic: The Theory of Inquiry* (1938) Dewey defines inquiry as the controlled transformation of an indeterminate situation into a determinate, unified one, and he replaces "truth" with **warranted assertibility**, the status a claim earns when inquiry supports it. Notice the shift. For Dewey the thing that is unsettled is not just a mind in doubt; it is a *situation*, an organism-in-an-environment in trouble. Thought is how organisms cope. Here Dewey is Darwin's heir, and he said so in a 1909 lecture:

> We do not solve them: we get over them. Old questions are solved by disappearing, evaporating, while new questions corresponding to the changed attitude of endeavor and preference take their place.
>
> — John Dewey, "The Influence of Darwin on Philosophy," in *The Influence of Darwin on Philosophy*

He meant the old disputes about fixed essences and final purposes in nature. You will meet the same thought in Wittgenstein ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)): some philosophical problems are not answered but dissolved. Dewey drew the moral for his discipline in 1917:

> Philosophy recovers itself when it ceases to be a device for dealing with the problems of philosophers and becomes a method, cultivated by philosophers, for dealing with the problems of men.
>
> — John Dewey, "The Need for a Recovery of Philosophy," in *Creative Intelligence*

### Habit and ends-in-view

*Human Nature and Conduct* (1922) is Dewey's moral psychology, and it is underread. Its central concept is **habit**, not in the sense of mechanical routine but of an acquired, organized disposition to respond. Our character is a web of habits; reason does not sit above them as a separate faculty but works through them when they collide or fail. Dewey attacks the split between means and ends that runs through ethics from Aristotle to the utilitarians:

> Means and ends are two names for the same reality. The terms denote not a division in reality but a distinction in judgment.
>
> — John Dewey, *Human Nature and Conduct*, Part One, II

Ends are not fixed targets outside activity. They are

> ends-in-view or aims. They arise out of natural effects or consequences which in the beginning are hit upon, stumbled upon so far as any purpose is concerned.
>
> — John Dewey, *Human Nature and Conduct*, Part Three, VI

We notice that we like some consequences and dislike others, and then aim at them; and in pursuing them we discover new consequences and revise the aims. An end is a plan for reorganizing present activity, and it is tested like a hypothesis. This is the root of Dewey's **ethical experimentalism**: moral principles are tools for analyzing situations, not commands. Compare it with Kant's categorical imperative ([Chapter 13 — Kant](13-kant.md)) and with the debates in [the Ethics course, Chapter 22](../learn-ethics/22-building-your-ethics.md), where you build your own framework.

### Democracy as a way of life

For Dewey democracy is not primarily an institution:

> A democracy is more than a form of government; it is primarily a mode of associated living, of conjoint communicated experience.
>
> — John Dewey, *Democracy and Education*, chapter 7

The point of schools, on this view, is not to transmit facts but to grow citizens who can inquire together. In *The Public and Its Problems* (1927), Dewey answered Walter Lippmann, whose *Public Opinion* (1922) and *The Phantom Public* (1925) argued that modern societies are too complex for ordinary citizens and should be run by experts. Dewey agreed that the public was "eclipsed," scattered and unable to recognize itself, but rejected rule by experts as the cure:

> In this sense the cure for the ailments of democracy is more democracy.
>
> — John Dewey, *The Public and Its Problems*, chapter 5

The line has a source Dewey knew well. His friend Jane Addams (1860–1935), founder of Hull House in Chicago and later winner of the Nobel Peace Prize, had written twenty-five years earlier:

> No attempt is made to reach a conclusion, nor to offer advice beyond the assumption that the cure for the ills of Democracy is more Democracy...
>
> — Jane Addams, *Democracy and Social Ethics*, Introduction

Addams is now recognized, thanks above all to Charlene Haddock Seigfried's *Pragmatism and Feminism* (1996), as a pragmatist philosopher in her own right. Her method was to test ethical ideas against the lives of immigrant neighbors, and her conclusion was that social ethics is learned only by "mixing on the thronged and common road where all must turn out for one another, and at least see the size of one another's burdens."

## Pragmatism's long afterlife

By the 1930s pragmatism looked finished. The logical positivists, many of them refugees from Hitler, arrived in American universities with sharper tools and a narrower agenda (see [Chapter 18 — The Analytic Turn](18-the-analytic-turn.md)). But pragmatist themes kept resurfacing inside analytic philosophy itself. W. V. O. Quine's "Two Dogmas of Empiricism" (1951), the most famous analytic paper of the century, ends by espousing "a more thorough pragmatism": our beliefs face experience as a whole web, and any of them can be revised. Wilfrid Sellars attacked "the myth of the given." Hilary Putnam, who began as a hard-nosed realist, ended as a self-described pragmatist.

### Rorty

The loudest revival came from Richard Rorty (1931–2007). His *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature* (1979) argued that modern philosophy since Descartes had been organized around a bad picture: the mind as a mirror, knowledge as accurate representation, and philosophy as the discipline that checks the mirror's accuracy. Drop the mirror, Rorty proposed, and epistemology gives way to "conversation"; truth becomes a compliment we pay to beliefs that our peers let pass, not a relation between mind and world.

*Contingency, Irony, and Solidarity* (1989) turned this into a picture of the self. Everyone has a **final vocabulary**, the words in which they justify their deepest commitments. The **ironist** knows that her final vocabulary is contingent, that she could have been raised with another and has no neutral argument for hers. The **liberal**, in Rorty's borrowing from the political theorist Judith Shklar, is someone who thinks "cruelty is the worst thing we do." The liberal ironist keeps both commitments: she cannot ground her hatred of cruelty in human nature or reason, and she holds it anyway. Solidarity, for Rorty, is made, not found, by literature, journalism and ethnography that help us see strangers as "one of us."

The objections to Rorty are as famous as his books:

- **Relativism.** If truth is what one's peers allow, then a society of Nazis makes Nazism "true." Rorty denies he is a relativist (he rejects the very question of whether beliefs match a non-human reality, so he cannot be saying that truth is relative to it), but many readers find the denial evasive.
- **Self-refutation.** Is the claim that there is no mind-independent truth itself meant to be true in the old sense? Rorty answers that it is a proposal, recommended by its fruits.
- **Betrayal of the tradition.** Susan Haack, in "Vulgar Pragmatism: An Unedifying Prospect" (1993), and Misak argue that Rorty abandoned Peirce's central commitment: inquiry aims at something that is not up to us. On their reading Rorty is a "vulgar pragmatist" who kept James's slogans and dropped Peirce's logic.

### The new pragmatists and beyond

Today's pragmatism is plural. **Neo-Peirceans** (Misak, Haack, Isaac Levi) defend a theory of truth tied to inquiry. **Robert Brandom** (*Making It Explicit*, 1994) develops "analytic pragmatism": the meaning of a concept is its role in the social game of giving and asking for reasons. **Huw Price** defends a "global expressivism" in which we explain what various kinds of talk *do* for us rather than what they represent. **Cornel West**, in *The American Evasion of Philosophy* (1989), reads the tradition as a form of cultural criticism and proposes a "prophetic pragmatism." Historians have also recovered the tradition's breadth: W. E. B. Du Bois studied with James at Harvard (see [Chapter 22 — Political Philosophy](22-political-philosophy.md)), and Alain Locke, the first Black Rhodes Scholar, developed a pragmatist theory of value and cultural pluralism.

The common thread is the one the Metaphysical Club started with. Beliefs are habits of action. Meanings are fixed by consequences. Inquiry starts from where we are, and nothing is beyond revision.

## Living it

Pragmatism is unusually easy to put to work, because it is a method before it is a doctrine.

1. **Run the maxim on a dispute you care about.** Pick an argument you keep having, with a partner, a colleague or yourself. Ask Peirce's question: if one side were right, what would anyone do, see or experience differently? If the answer is "nothing," you have a squirrel dispute; make James's distinction and stop. If the answer is "something," you now know exactly what evidence would decide it.
2. **Audit how you fixed three beliefs.** Choose three beliefs that matter to you (about politics, about yourself, about a person). For each, ask which of Peirce's four methods actually produced it: tenacity, authority, the *a priori* method of what seemed elegant, or something outside you that could have shown you wrong. Be honest. Peirce's point was not that the first three are always wrong, but that only the fourth can correct itself.
3. **Classify your hard choices.** For a decision you face, test James's conditions. Is it live, forced and momentous? Can evidence settle it in time? If not, James permits you to decide on your hopes, but Clifford's shipowner reminds you to check first whether you are simply avoiding an inspection you could afford.
4. **Treat a value as an end-in-view.** Pick one aim you hold, such as "be more patient" or "write more." Following Dewey, state it as a hypothesis: "If I do X in situation Y, I expect consequence Z, which I value." Try it for two weeks and record what happens. Then revise the aim, not just your effort. This is what Dewey meant when he called moral principles tools for inquiry.

The common discipline is to stop asking whether an idea is impressive and to start asking what it does.

## Summary

- Pragmatism began in the Metaphysical Club (Cambridge, Massachusetts, early 1870s) with Bain's definition of belief as a readiness to act.
- Peirce's "Fixation of Belief" compares four methods for settling opinion (tenacity, authority, *a priori*, science) and argues that only science, which answers to something independent of us, can correct itself. Real doubt needs a reason; fallibilism replaces Cartesian foundations.
- The **pragmatic maxim**: our conception of an object is our conception of its conceivable practical effects. Peirce defined truth as the opinion fated to be agreed by all who investigate; Misak's reconstruction is that a true belief would not be improved by further inquiry.
- James made pragmatism famous: temperaments, the squirrel, "cash-value," and the claim that truth *happens* to an idea. Russell and Moore objected that verification and usefulness diverge from truth; James replied that he was explaining the point of the concept, not a test for applying it.
- "The Will to Believe": in genuine (live, forced, momentous) options that evidence cannot settle, our passional nature may decide; Clifford's evidentialism is a choice too. The ethics of belief remains an active field (pragmatic and moral encroachment).
- Dewey's instrumentalism: inquiry transforms problematic situations; habits, ends-in-view, ethical experimentalism; democracy as a way of associated living; with Addams, "the cure for the ills of Democracy is more Democracy."
- Rorty revived pragmatism as anti-representationalism and liberal irony; Haack and Misak object that he abandoned Peirce's commitment to inquiry aimed at something not up to us. Brandom, Price and West carry the tradition on.

**You:** …

**You:** I think so. I have a good sense of people, and he has done nothing to make me suspicious.

**Clifford:** My shipowner also had nothing to make him suspicious, except the doubts he had decided to stop listening to. Did you look at his previous ventures? Speak to anyone he had worked with?

**You:** No. It felt insulting to check.

**Clifford:** Then your conviction is sincere and comfortable, like the shipowner's. If the partnership fails and your savings sink with it, will your sincerity help you?

**You:** No. But if it succeeds, I was right.

**Clifford:** You were lucky. Suppose the ship had come home safely. Was the owner any less guilty? His fault lay in how he came to believe, not in how the voyage ended.

**James:** Forgive me for interrupting, Clifford. You are right that this is a case for evidence. The question can be settled by inquiry, cheaply, and nothing is lost by waiting a week. That is not a genuine option.

**You:** So you agree with him, Professor James?

**James:** Here, yes. But notice something else in your situation. A partnership is one of those facts that faith helps create. If you meet your partner with suspicion at every turn, you may make him into the kind of partner who cannot be trusted. Some trust must run ahead of evidence, or no partnership exists.

**Clifford:** Then let him extend the trust in action, and keep his belief proportioned to the evidence. One can treat a man generously without believing what one has no right to believe.

**You:** Can I really separate how I treat him from what I believe about him?

**James:** That is the right question. Clifford assumes belief is a private verdict in a court of evidence. I say belief is a readiness to act. If you act as if he is trustworthy, in what sense do you not believe it?

**Clifford:** In this sense: that you remain alert to evidence, and would change your conduct if it came.

**James:** Then we agree more than we thought. The disagreement is over cases where evidence cannot come in time, or cannot come at all, without the faith first.

**You:** So my position should be: check what can be checked, because that is cheap and the stakes are high. Then extend practical trust that is responsive to what I learn, knowing that the trust itself is part of what makes the partnership work.

**Clifford:** That I can accept, if "responsive" is meant seriously.

**James:** And I can accept it if you admit that the trust is not a mere bet. It is a belief of the kind that helps make itself true.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether truth is what works

**Russell:** Professor James says that the true is "only the expedient in the way of our thinking." You found that reasonable when you read it. Is it true that the Earth orbits the Sun?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Russell in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes. And believing it works: it predicts eclipses, it lets us launch satellites.

**Russell:** Very well. Is it true that you had toast for breakfast on this day eleven years ago?

**You:** I have no idea. Either I did or I didn't.

**Russell:** Exactly so. One of those two propositions is true. Does believing it have any consequences at all, good or bad?

**You:** None that I can see.

**Russell:** Then on James's account neither is true, since neither proves itself "good in the way of belief." Yet you just told me one of them must be. Moore made the same point: we very often have true ideas we cannot verify.

**You:** Perhaps James would say that the truth of the toast proposition consists in what would happen if we could check it, if we had a recording.

**Peirce:** Allow me. That is very nearly my view, and it is not James's. I never said that truth is what works for you. I said it is what inquiry would settle on if pushed far enough. The toast question may be one inquiry cannot reach.

**Russell:** Then you must say the question has no truth-value, which is absurd, or that "far enough" means "to the truth," which is circular.

**Peirce:** Or I can say what my more careful friends say for me: a true belief is one that would not be improved by any further inquiry. That is not a definition from which you can derive which beliefs are true. It tells you what believing aims at.

**Russell:** That is a more modest thesis. It is no longer a rival to the plain view that a belief is true when it corresponds to a fact.

**Peirce:** Perhaps not a rival. A clarification of what "corresponds" comes to, for creatures who can only find out by inquiring.

**You:** So the slogan "truth is what works" is wrong. But there is a weaker idea that survives: that we have a concept of truth because of the role it plays in inquiry, and that no belief, however useful, is safe from correction by further experience.

**Russell:** I could sign the second half of that. I should want to see the first half worked out before I signed it.

**You:** That sounds like a good research project rather than a refutation.

### Dialogue 3 — On deciding how to live, as an experiment

**Dewey:** You told me you are torn between staying in your well-paid job and going back to school to train as a teacher. You want to know which is the right choice. What would make one of them right?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Dewey in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I suppose the right one is whichever matches my true values. I just don't know what they are.

**Dewey:** You speak as if your values were already there, finished, like coins in a drawer you have not opened. Where would you look for them?

**You:** In myself. In what I really want.

**Dewey:** And when you look, what do you find?

**You:** Conflicting wants. Security. Meaning. Not disappointing my parents. Time with my children.

**Dewey:** Good. That conflict is your felt difficulty, the first step of any inquiry. The second is to locate and define it. Which of these wants actually conflict, and in what concrete situations?

**You:** Security and meaning conflict over money. Time with my children conflicts with the school schedule.

**Dewey:** Now we have a problem instead of a mood. Third step: suggestions. Are there only two options?

**You:** I could teach evening classes for a year while keeping the job. Or volunteer as a tutor.

**Dewey:** Each of those is a hypothesis about what would satisfy you. Fourth step: reason out what each would involve. Fifth: try one and observe. Means and ends are two names for the same reality. You will not discover what "meaning" is for you by staring inward; you will discover it by seeing which activities you find you want to continue.

**You:** But what if I choose wrongly and lose years?

**Dewey:** You may. Every experiment can fail. But consider the alternative you began with: waiting until your "true values" announce themselves. That is not caution. It is another choice, and it has its own costs.

**James:** It is a forced option, then, though not one evidence can settle in advance.

**Dewey:** Partly. It can be broken down into smaller options that evidence *can* settle, at least partly. That is what intelligence is for.

**You:** So my position is that I do not have to find my values before choosing. I can treat "meaning" as an end-in-view, design a small test, and let the result revise both my plan and my sense of what I want. And I should be suspicious of any framing that leaves me with only two all-or-nothing options.

**Dewey:** Then you have stopped asking which choice is right in itself and started asking which course of action would resolve your situation best. That is the only question there was.

</details>
