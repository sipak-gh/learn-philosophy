# Chapter 20 — Philosophy of Mind

You are reading this sentence. Light is hitting your retinas, neurons are firing in your visual cortex, and somehow there is also *an experience*: the black shapes on the page, the voice in your head that says the words, perhaps a faint boredom or curiosity. How do these two descriptions fit together? Is the experience the same thing as the neural firing, or something extra? Could a computer have it? Would you still be you if every cell in your body were replaced? These are the questions of the philosophy of mind, now the busiest crossroads in philosophy, where metaphysics meets neuroscience, psychology and artificial intelligence. This chapter traces the main theories from Descartes' dualism to functionalism, then turns to the problem of consciousness (Nagel's bat, Jackson's Mary, Chalmers' hard problem), to mental content, to personal identity (Locke, Hume, Parfit) and briefly to free will, which is treated in full in the Ethics course. Previous: [Chapter 19 — Phenomenology and Existentialism](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md). Next: [Chapter 21 — Knowledge and Science](21-knowledge-and-science.md).

## The problem

The mind-body problem is really three problems, and it helps to keep them apart.

1. **Consciousness.** Some states are *felt*: the taste of coffee, the sting of a burn, the blue of the sky. Philosophers call the felt qualities **qualia** (singular *quale*) or **phenomenal character**. How can physical processes give rise to felt experience at all?
2. **Intentionality.** Mental states are *about* things (see Brentano in [Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)). Your belief that Paris is in France is about Paris. How can a pattern of neural activity be about anything?
3. **Mental causation.** Your wanting a coffee seems to cause you to stand up. If the physical world is causally closed, every physical event having a sufficient physical cause, how can a mental state make a difference, unless it is itself physical?

Every theory in this chapter is an attempt to fit these three features into a single picture of the world. None has succeeded to general satisfaction.

## Descartes and dualism

René Descartes ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)) gave the problem its modern form. From the certainty of his own thinking he concluded that he was a thinking thing whose nature does not depend on the body:

> ...I thence concluded that I was a substance whose whole essence or nature consists only in thinking, and which, that it may exist, has need of no place, nor is dependent on any material thing; so that "I," that is to say, the mind by which I am what I am, is wholly distinct from the body...
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part IV (tr. John Veitch)

This is **substance dualism**: there are two kinds of thing, extended matter (*res extensa*) and unextended thinking substance (*res cogitans*). Descartes' arguments, developed more carefully in the *Meditations* (1641), are worth stating:

- **The conceivability argument.** I can clearly and distinctly conceive of my mind existing without my body (I can doubt that I have a body, but not that I think). Whatever I can clearly conceive apart, God could create apart. So mind and body are really distinct.
- **The divisibility argument.** Bodies are divisible into parts; the mind is not (there is no half of a thought or a self). So they differ in nature.

The conceivability argument has a shape that will come back in Chalmers' zombie argument, and so does the standard reply: that I can conceive of A without B shows something about my concepts, not necessarily about the things. A person can conceive of water without H₂O, if they do not know chemistry.

### The interaction problem

If mind and body are utterly different, how do they affect each other? In 1643 Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia, in a famous correspondence, pressed Descartes on exactly this. Physical things move only by contact and pushing; how could an unextended soul, which has no surface, push the animal spirits in the nerves? Descartes' answer, that the union of mind and body is a primitive notion understood through the senses rather than the intellect, did not satisfy her, and she suggested it would be easier to grant the soul matter and extension. He located the interface in the pineal gland at the center of the brain, which only relocated the mystery.

Descartes' successors tried other answers. Nicolas Malebranche's **occasionalism** held that mind and body never interact: God moves my arm on the occasion of my willing it. Leibniz's **pre-established harmony** held that they run in parallel like two perfectly synchronized clocks. Spinoza's **dual-aspect monism** held that mind and body are one substance seen under two attributes. All three are ways of keeping the correlation between mental and physical without interaction, and all three seem a high price to pay.

### Machines and the test of language

Descartes believed that animal bodies, and human bodies, are machines. What separates us is the soul, and he proposed tests by which a soul could be detected. A machine might be built to say words in response to stimuli, he says:

> ...but not that it should arrange them variously so as appositely to reply to what is said in its presence, as men of the lowest grade of intellect can do.
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part V (tr. John Veitch)

His second test is flexibility: machines act "solely from the disposition of their organs," while reason "is an universal instrument that is alike available on every occasion." This loosely anticipates the Turing test and today's debates about artificial general intelligence, three centuries early. Large language models now pass Descartes' first test in a way he thought impossible, which makes his second test (and the question of whether passing either shows a mind) newly urgent.

A century later the French physician Julien Offray de La Mettrie drew the opposite conclusion from the same mechanism: "Let us then conclude boldly that man is a machine" (*Man a Machine*, 1748, tr. Gertrude C. Bussey). He was forced to flee to Prussia.

## The steam whistle: epiphenomenalism

Nineteenth-century physiology made the brain's role undeniable and sharpened the problem of mental causation. If the nervous system obeys physical law, and physical law accounts for every movement of the body, what is left for the mind to do? The biologist T. H. Huxley, "Darwin's bulldog," gave a famous answer in 1874:

> The consciousness of brutes would appear to be related to the mechanism of their body simply as a collateral product of its working, and to be as completely without any power of modifying that working as the steam-whistle which accompanies the work of a locomotive engine is without influence upon its machinery.
>
> — T. H. Huxley, "On the Hypothesis that Animals are Automata, and its History," in *Science and Culture*

And of humans: "We are conscious automata." This is **epiphenomenalism**: mental states are caused by brain states but cause nothing themselves. Your pain does not make you wince; the brain state that causes the pain also causes the wince.

William James found this incredible. He imagined a complete physiology of Shakespeare:

> If we knew thoroughly the nervous system of Shakespeare, and as thoroughly all his environing conditions, we should be able to show why at a certain period of his life his hand came to trace on certain sheets of paper those crabbed little black marks which we for shortness' sake call the manuscript of Hamlet.
>
> — William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, vol. 1, Chapter V

On the automaton theory, all this could be explained without mentioning a single thought in Shakespeare's mind. James's main counterargument was evolutionary. Consciousness is too elaborate and too closely tuned to the organism's interests to be an idle by-product. Pleasures go with what benefits us, pains with what harms us; if feelings did nothing, natural selection could not have shaped them so well. Consciousness, James concluded, is a "fighter for ends." The argument still has force against modern epiphenomenalism, as does the simpler objection that if qualia cause nothing, they cannot cause our talk about them, so our words "I am in pain" would not be produced by the pain.

## Behaviorism and the identity theory

### Mind as behavior

In the early twentieth century psychology tried to become a science by ignoring the inner life. J. B. Watson and later B. F. Skinner studied only observable stimulus and response. Philosophers developed a parallel view, **logical behaviorism**: statements about the mind are really statements about behavior and dispositions to behave. Gilbert Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949), with its attack on the ghost in the machine ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)), is the classic. To be vain is not to have a hidden feeling but to be disposed to talk about yourself, fish for compliments, and so on. To know French is to be able to do certain things.

Behaviorism dissolved the interaction problem and the problem of other minds at a stroke. But it faced decisive objections:

- **Pretenders and stoics.** An actor can behave exactly as if in pain without being in pain; Hilary Putnam imagined "super-Spartans" who feel pain but have trained themselves never to show it. Behavior and mind come apart in both directions.
- **Holism.** A belief issues in behavior only together with desires, and vice versa. If you believe it is raining, you take an umbrella *if* you want to stay dry *and* believe umbrellas help. Any behavioral analysis of one mental state must mention others, so the mental is never eliminated.
- **The inner life itself.** The joke goes that two behaviorists make love, and afterward one says to the other: "That was good for you. How was it for me?"

### Mind as brain

In the late 1950s U. T. Place ("Is Consciousness a Brain Process?", 1956) and J. J. C. Smart ("Sensations and Brain Processes", 1959) proposed the **mind-brain identity theory**: mental states simply *are* brain states. Pain is (to use a famously inaccurate placeholder) C-fiber firing. The identity is not known by analyzing the word "pain," any more than "lightning is electrical discharge" or "water is H₂O" was known by analyzing words. It is a scientific discovery. The theory respected the causal closure of physics (mental causes are physical causes) and Ockham's razor (no extra stuff).

Its problem came from an unexpected direction. Octopuses feel pain, presumably, but have very different nervous systems. A Martian, or a future robot, might feel pain with no neurons at all. If pain *is* C-fiber firing, then creatures without C-fibers cannot be in pain. This is the **multiple realizability** objection (Putnam, 1967): mental kinds seem to be realizable in many different physical kinds, so they cannot be identical to any one of them.

## Functionalism and the computer

The answer that dominated philosophy and cognitive science from the 1960s onward is **functionalism**. A mental state is defined by its *functional role*: its typical causes, its effects on other mental states, and its effects on behavior. Pain is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage, causes distress and the desire to make it stop, and causes wincing and avoidance. In humans that role is played by certain neural states; in octopuses by others; in a robot, perhaps by silicon circuits. The analogy is software and hardware. The mind is to the brain as a program is to a computer: the same program can run on different machines.

Functionalism explains multiple realizability, keeps mental causation (functional states are causes), and makes the mind a proper object of cognitive science, which models mental processes as computations. It also makes artificial minds possible in principle.

### The Turing test

In 1950 the mathematician Alan Turing, who had helped break the German Enigma code and defined the theoretical computer, replaced the question whether machines can think, which he thought too vague to discuss, with an **imitation game**. An interrogator communicates by text with a human and a machine and tries to tell which is which. If the machine regularly fools the interrogator, we should grant that it thinks. Turing answered nine objections in advance, including the theological one, the heads-in-the-sand objection, the argument from consciousness, and Lady Lovelace's objection that a machine can only do what it is told. He predicted that by the end of the century one would be able to speak of machines thinking without being contradicted. Note that the test is behaviorist in spirit: it asks only what the machine does.

### The Chinese room

In 1980 John Searle offered a thought experiment that is still the most discussed argument in the field. Imagine yourself locked in a room with a rulebook written in English. Slips of paper covered in Chinese characters come in through a slot. You look up the characters in the rulebook, which tells you which other characters to copy and pass back out. You do not understand a word of Chinese. But the rulebook is so good that the people outside, native speakers, are convinced that whoever is in the room understands Chinese perfectly.

Searle's point is that you are doing exactly what a computer does: manipulating symbols according to formal rules (syntax). And you understand nothing. So running a program, however sophisticated, is not sufficient for understanding (semantics): syntax alone never adds up to semantics. Searle calls the view he is attacking **strong AI**: the claim that an appropriately programmed computer literally has a mind.

The replies are as famous as the argument:

- **The systems reply.** You do not understand Chinese, but the whole system (you, the rulebook, the paper, the room) does. Searle: then memorize the rulebook and do it all in your head. You are now the whole system and still understand nothing.
- **The robot reply.** Understanding requires causal connection to the world. Put the program in a robot with cameras and arms. Searle: then the room receives more symbols (from the cameras), and you still do not know what they mean.
- **The brain simulator reply.** Suppose the program simulates the neuron-by-neuron activity of a Chinese speaker's brain. Searle: it simulates the formal structure but not the causal powers of neurons; a simulation of a storm does not make anyone wet.
- **The intuition reply.** Searle relies on our intuition that the person in the room does not understand. But our intuitions about a system that would be billions of times slower and larger than anything we can imagine may be worthless.

Searle's own view, **biological naturalism**, holds that consciousness and intentionality are real, biological features of brains, caused by their specific causal powers, as digestion is a feature of stomachs. Whether a non-biological system could have those powers is, for Searle, an empirical question; what is certain is that running a program is not enough. Today the argument is applied, for and against, to large language models: systems that pass many versions of Turing's test by predicting text from statistical patterns. Whether they understand anything is exactly the question Searle and Turing disagreed about.

### Eliminativism

A more radical response to the failure of reduction is to deny that the mental states of everyday psychology exist at all. Paul and Patricia Churchland's **eliminative materialism** treats "folk psychology," our everyday talk of beliefs and desires, as a theory, and a bad one, likely to be replaced by neuroscience the way "phlogiston" and "demonic possession" were replaced. Critics answer that the view seems self-undermining: the Churchlands *believe* that there are no beliefs, and they *want* us to accept it.

## Consciousness: the hard problem

Functionalism handles intentionality and causation fairly well. What it seemed to leave out is experience. Three arguments made this vivid.

### What is it like to be a bat?

Thomas Nagel's 1974 paper proposed a definition that has become standard:

> ...an organism has conscious mental states if and only if there is something that it is like to *be* that organism—something it is like *for* the organism.
>
> — Thomas Nagel, "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?" (1974)

Bats navigate by echolocation, a sense utterly unlike any of ours. You could learn every fact about bat neurophysiology and sonar processing, and you would still not know what it is like for the bat to perceive the world by echoes. Physical science describes the world from an objective standpoint, from no particular point of view. Experience is essentially tied to a point of view. So it is hard to see how an objective physical theory could ever capture the subjective character of experience. Nagel did not conclude that physicalism is false, but that we have no idea how it could be true, as a pre-Socratic would have had no idea how matter could be energy.

### Mary's room

Frank Jackson's **knowledge argument** (1982) turned the point into a formal argument. Mary is a brilliant neuroscientist who has lived all her life in a black-and-white room, learning about the world through black-and-white screens. She knows every physical fact about color vision: wavelengths, the retina, the visual cortex, what people say when they see red. One day she is released and sees a ripe tomato. Does she learn something new?

Most people say yes: she learns *what it is like* to see red. But by hypothesis she already knew all the physical facts. So there are facts that are not physical facts, and **physicalism**, the view that everything is physical, is false.

Jackson later recanted and became a physicalist. The main physicalist replies are:

- **The ability hypothesis** (David Lewis, Laurence Nemirow). Mary does not learn a new fact; she gains new abilities: to recognize, imagine and remember red. Knowing-how, not knowing-that.
- **The acquaintance hypothesis** (Earl Conee). She gains acquaintance with a property she already knew about by description (compare Russell in [Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)).
- **The phenomenal concept strategy** (Brian Loar and others). Mary learns an old fact in a new way, under a new kind of concept. Compare learning that Hesperus is bright when you already knew Phosphorus is: one fact, two concepts.
- **Deny the intuition** (Daniel Dennett). If Mary really knew *all* the physical facts, she would not be surprised at all. Our sense that she would be comes from failing to imagine what knowing *everything* would be like.

### Zombies and the hard problem

David Chalmers (1995, 1996) named the **hard problem of consciousness**. The "easy" problems, which are hard enough in practice, concern functions: how the brain discriminates stimuli, integrates information, reports internal states, controls attention. These can in principle be explained by specifying a mechanism that performs the function. The hard problem is why the performance of these functions is accompanied by experience at all. Why is there something it is like? Even after all the functions are explained, the question remains open.

Chalmers' **zombie argument** has Descartes' structure:

1. It is conceivable that there be a **philosophical zombie**: a being physically identical to you, molecule for molecule, behaving exactly as you do, but with no conscious experience at all. Inside, all is dark.
2. If zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible.
3. If zombies are possible, then the physical facts do not necessitate the facts about consciousness, and physicalism is false.

Physicalists usually reject premise 2: conceivability is a poor guide to possibility (recall water and H₂O). Or they reject premise 1, arguing that once you really understand the physical story, zombies will turn out to be as inconceivable as a cube with seven faces. Joseph Levine's more modest claim, the **explanatory gap**, is that even if consciousness is physical, we currently have no explanation of *why* a given brain process feels the way it does, and no idea what such an explanation would look like.

### Where things stand

The positions on consciousness today form a spectrum:

- **Type-B physicalism**: consciousness is physical, but the identity is known *a posteriori* and the explanatory gap reflects our concepts, not the world.
- **Illusionism** (Dennett, Keith Frankish): phenomenal consciousness, in the sense that generates the hard problem, does not exist. What exists is a powerful *representation* that we have it. The task is to explain why it seems so obvious that we have qualia.
- **Property dualism** (Chalmers): there is one kind of substance but two kinds of properties, physical and phenomenal, linked by fundamental psychophysical laws.
- **Russellian monism and panpsychism.** Physics tells us only the structure and dispositions of matter, what it does, not what it intrinsically is. Perhaps the intrinsic nature of matter is experiential, or proto-experiential, and consciousness in brains is built from it. The idea goes back to Russell's **neutral monism**:

> The stuff of which the world of our experience is composed is, in my belief, neither mind nor matter, but something more primitive than either.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Analysis of Mind*, Lecture I

Panpsychism, once a joke, is now defended by Galen Strawson, Philip Goff and others. Its main problem is the **combination problem**: how do micro-experiences combine into the unified experience of a person?

- **Mysterianism** (Colin McGinn): the problem has a solution, but human minds are not built to grasp it, as a dog cannot grasp calculus.

Meanwhile, the science of consciousness proceeds by searching for the **neural correlates of consciousness**, with rival theories such as global workspace theory (Bernard Baars, Stanislas Dehaene), integrated information theory (Giulio Tononi), and higher-order theories (David Rosenthal). Large "adversarial collaborations" now test their predictions against each other.

## Content: what makes thoughts about things

Back to intentionality. If thinking is computation over symbols, what makes the symbols mean anything? Jerry Fodor's **language of thought** hypothesis holds that thinking takes place in an internal, language-like code ("Mentalese"); naturalistic theories of content try to explain what makes a Mentalese symbol mean *cow*, for instance by its being reliably caused by cows, or by its having the evolutionary function of indicating cows.

Then a puzzle about where meaning lives. In "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), Hilary Putnam imagined **Twin Earth**, a planet exactly like Earth except that the clear liquid in its rivers and lakes is not H₂O but a different compound, XYZ. In 1750, before chemistry, your twin on Twin Earth is in an identical brain state to yours when each of you thinks "water is wet." Yet your word refers to H₂O and your twin's to XYZ. So the meaning of "water," and the content of the thought, is not fixed by what is in the head. Putnam's conclusion:

> Cut the pie any way you like, "meanings" just ain't in the head!
>
> — Hilary Putnam, "The Meaning of 'Meaning'" (1975), in *Mind, Language and Reality* (1975)

This is **externalism** about mental content. Tyler Burge extended it to social environment: what your word "arthritis" means depends on your community's usage, even if you misunderstand it. Andy Clark and David Chalmers went further with the **extended mind** (1998). Otto, who has Alzheimer's, writes everything in a notebook he always carries; when he wants to go to the museum, he looks up the address. Inga remembers the address in the usual way. If Inga's memory counts as part of her mind, why not Otto's notebook, which plays the same role? And if Otto's notebook, then perhaps your phone.

## Personal identity

What makes you, now, the same person as the child in your old photographs? Not the same matter (most of your atoms have been replaced) and not unchanging properties (you have changed in nearly every way). The question matters practically: it bears on responsibility, on survival, on what you should care about in your future.

### Locke: consciousness

Locke ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)) defined a person as a mental, not a biological, kind:

> ...which, I think, is a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places; which it does only by that consciousness which is inseparable from thinking...
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, II.xxvii.11

Personal identity, for Locke, consists in **continuity of consciousness**, which in practice means memory: you are the same person as whoever's thoughts and actions you can remember from the inside. He separated three questions: same *substance* (soul or matter), same *man* (living human organism), and same *person*. His thought experiment:

> For should the soul of a prince, carrying with it the consciousness of the prince's past life, enter and inform the body of a cobbler, as soon as deserted by his own soul, every one sees he would be the same PERSON with the prince, accountable only for the prince's actions: but who would say it was the same MAN?
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, II.xxvii.17

"Person," Locke added, is a "forensic" term: it concerns accountability, reward and punishment.

Two classic objections:

- **The brave officer** (Thomas Reid, 1785). A boy is flogged for stealing apples; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers capturing a standard as an officer but has forgotten the flogging. By Locke's criterion the general is the officer, and the officer is the boy, but the general is not the boy. Identity is transitive, so the criterion is broken. Modern Lockeans reply by appealing to overlapping chains of memory (psychological *continuity*, not direct connection).
- **Circularity** (Joseph Butler, 1736). Genuine memory presupposes personal identity: I can genuinely remember only *my own* experiences. So memory cannot constitute identity. Reply: define "quasi-memory," a memory-like state caused in the right way by an earlier experience, without presupposing that it was mine.

### Hume: the bundle

Hume ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)) looked inward for the self that is supposed to have all these experiences and could not find it:

> For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature*, I.iv.6

Humans, he concluded, are "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement." The mind is "a kind of theatre" where perceptions appear and pass, though Hume immediately warns that there is no stage either. Our belief in a simple, enduring self is a fiction produced by the imagination, smoothing over resemblance and causal connection between perceptions. The view closely parallels the Buddhist doctrine of **anattā** (no-self), on which the person is a stream of five aggregates ([Chapter 8 — Classical India and China](08-classical-india-and-china.md)). Hume himself was dissatisfied: in the Appendix to the *Treatise* he confessed that his account of what unites the perceptions was defective, and that he could not see how to fix it.

### Parfit: what matters

Derek Parfit's *Reasons and Persons* (1984) brought these questions back with a set of thought experiments that are now standard.

- **Teletransportation.** You step into a machine on Earth. It scans your body, destroys it, and sends the information to Mars, where a replica is built from new matter, with your memories, character and plans. The replica steps out feeling exactly as you would. Have you traveled to Mars, or died and been replaced? Now suppose a malfunction: the scanner does not destroy the original. You are still on Earth, and someone talks to you from Mars, claiming to be you.
- **Fission.** Your brain's two hemispheres are each capable of supporting your full psychology (this is roughly true in some real cases). Your body is dying, and each hemisphere is transplanted into a different body. Both resulting people are psychologically continuous with you. Which one is you? Not both, since they are two people with different futures; not one, since they are symmetrical; not neither, since if only one transplant had succeeded, you would surely have survived.

Parfit's conclusion is radical. Personal identity is not a deep further fact, an all-or-nothing matter like the presence of a Cartesian ego. It consists in nothing more than psychological continuity and connectedness, which come in degrees. In fission cases, "Is it me?" may have no answer, and it does not matter. **Identity is not what matters** in survival; what matters is psychological continuity, whether or not it takes the form of identity. Parfit reported that he found this liberating. He wrote that the walls of the glass tunnel in which he had seemed to live disappeared; he now cared less about his own death and more about the lives of others. The view has consequences for ethics: if the connections between me now and me in forty years are weaker than I thought, prudence looks more like concern for another person, and the boundaries between persons look less important to utilitarian calculation (see [the Ethics course, Chapter 11](../learn-ethics/11-contemporary-consequentialism.md)).

The main rival is **animalism** (Eric Olson, Paul Snowdon): you are a human animal, and you persist as long as that organism lives. Animalism handles the question of what is thinking your thoughts (the animal is, so if you are not the animal, there are two thinkers in your chair), but it implies that you would not go with your brain in a transplant, which many find hard to accept. **Narrative** views (Marya Schechtman) hold that a person is constituted by a self-told life story, closer to the practical concerns that make identity matter.

## Free will, briefly

The mind-body problem meets ethics in the problem of free will. If every event, including every decision, is determined by prior causes according to physical law, can you ever act freely, and be responsible? The main positions:

- **Hard determinism**: determinism is true and incompatible with free will, so no one is free (d'Holbach; today Derk Pereboom's "hard incompatibilism," which adds that indeterminism would not help either).
- **Libertarianism** (in the metaphysical sense): free will exists and is incompatible with determinism, so determinism is false at least for human choices (Thomas Reid; today Robert Kane, Timothy O'Connor).
- **Compatibilism**: free will is compatible with determinism, because freedom means acting on your own reasons and desires without compulsion, not escaping causation (Hobbes, Hume, Mill; today Harry Frankfurt's hierarchical theory, and the "reasons-responsiveness" theory of John Martin Fischer). This is the majority view among philosophers.

Huxley's "conscious automata" passage, quoted above, is itself a compatibilist claim: we are "endowed with free will in the only intelligible sense of that much-abused term—inasmuch as in many respects we are able to do as we like." Neuroscientific findings such as Benjamin Libet's (a buildup of brain activity, the "readiness potential," beginning a few hundred milliseconds before the moment subjects later reported as their awareness of deciding to move) are often said to disprove free will; most philosophers think they show much less. The full treatment, including P. F. Strawson's argument that responsibility is grounded in our reactive attitudes, is in [the Ethics course, Chapter 18](../learn-ethics/18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md).

## Living it

1. **Two descriptions.** Pick one strong experience today, such as pain, music or anger. Write two descriptions of it: one from the first-person point of view, one as a neuroscientist would describe it. Then ask what, if anything, the second leaves out. You are now doing Nagel's and Jackson's experiment on yourself.
2. **The machine test.** Have a conversation with an AI system about something you know well. Apply Descartes' two tests and Searle's question: does it reply appositely, does it act flexibly, and is there any evidence of understanding rather than skilled symbol use? Notice what kind of evidence could possibly settle the question, and whether you apply the same standard to people.
3. **Parfit's exercise.** Write a letter to yourself twenty years from now. Then ask how connected that future person will be to you: memories, values, plans. Parfit thought that seeing the connections as matters of degree could make you kinder to your future self (and less anxious about death) at the same time.
4. **The bundle.** For five minutes, try Hume's experiment: look for the self that has your experiences. Report honestly what you find. Compare the result with the meditative traditions in [Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md).

## Summary

- The mind-body problem divides into consciousness, intentionality and mental causation.
- **Descartes' substance dualism** rests on conceivability and divisibility arguments; Elisabeth's **interaction problem** remains its main difficulty. His language and flexibility tests anticipate the Turing test.
- **Epiphenomenalism** (Huxley's steam whistle) makes the mind causally idle; James objected that evolution could not have shaped an idle consciousness.
- **Behaviorism** failed on pretenders, holism and the inner life; the **identity theory** (Place, Smart) failed on **multiple realizability**.
- **Functionalism** defines mental states by causal role, like software. The **Turing test** asks only for behavior; Searle's **Chinese room** argues that syntax is not sufficient for semantics. **Eliminativism** denies folk psychology.
- **Consciousness**: Nagel's bat (subjectivity), Jackson's Mary (the knowledge argument), Chalmers' **hard problem** and zombies; replies include the ability hypothesis, phenomenal concepts, illusionism, property dualism, Russellian monism/panpsychism and mysterianism.
- **Content**: Putnam's Twin Earth supports **externalism**; Clark and Chalmers' **extended mind** puts Otto's notebook in his mind.
- **Personal identity**: Locke's psychological criterion (prince and cobbler), Reid's brave officer and Butler's circularity objections, Hume's **bundle**, Parfit's teletransporter and fission (**identity is not what matters**), animalism.
- **Free will**: hard determinism, libertarianism and compatibilism; see the Ethics course for the full debate.

### Dialogue 1 — On what Mary learns

**Jackson:** Mary knows every physical fact about color vision. She walks out of the black-and-white room and sees a red tomato. Does she learn anything?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Jackson in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes, obviously. She learns what red looks like. No amount of reading could have told her that.

**Jackson:** Then she learns a fact she didn't know. But she knew every physical fact. So the fact she learns is not physical, and physicalism is false.

**You:** That seems too quick. Maybe what she gets isn't a new fact.

**Lewis:** Exactly. She gains abilities: to recognize red when she sees it again, to imagine it, to remember it. That is know-how, not knowledge of a new fact. You don't refute physicalism by showing that people can learn to ride bicycles.

**You:** But it feels like more than an ability. When she sees red, it seems she discovers something about the world, or about her experience, that was true all along.

**Dennett:** Let me ask you something. Do you really imagine Mary knowing *all* the physical facts? Every detail of how her brain would respond, every association, every reaction? I suspect you imagined her knowing a great deal, which is not the same. If she truly knew everything, she might see the tomato and say, "Just as I expected."

**You:** I admit I can't really imagine knowing *everything* physical. But that cuts both ways. If I can't imagine it, I also can't be confident she'd be unsurprised.

**Jackson:** Then you see why I found the argument so compelling, and why I eventually changed my mind about it. The intuition is strong, but it's an intuition about a case none of us can properly imagine.

**You:** So here's where I land. The argument shows that there's a difference between knowing about an experience and having it. What's disputed is whether that's a difference in *facts* or only in *ways of knowing* the same fact. If it's the second, as with Hesperus and Phosphorus, physicalism survives. To decide, I'd need a theory of what a fact is, which is a bigger question than I thought.

### Dialogue 2 — On the teletransporter

**Parfit:** You are about to step into the teletransporter to Mars. Your body here will be destroyed; an exact replica will be built there. Are you worried?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Parfit in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes. I think I'd be killed, and someone else who thinks he's me would wake up on Mars.

**Parfit:** What would make him someone else? He has your memories, your character, your plans, your love for your family. He will go on with your life exactly as you would.

**You:** But not my body. Not my brain.

**Parfit:** Suppose your brain cells were gradually replaced over a year by exact copies, as in fact your atoms are replaced over time. Would you die?

**You:** No. That's like ordinary life.

**Parfit:** And if they were replaced all at once, in a second?

**You:** That's harder. I want to say speed shouldn't matter, but it seems to.

**Locke:** Speed does not matter. What makes the same person is the same consciousness. If the one on Mars remembers your life from the inside, he is you.

**Reid:** And if the machine malfunctions, and makes two replicas, one on Mars and one on Venus? Both remember your life. Both cannot be you, since they are not each other.

**Parfit:** That is my point. In such a case "Is it me?" has no good answer. But nothing is missing. All the facts are known: two people, each fully continuous with you psychologically. What would you want to know in addition?

**You:** Whether *I* will experience waking up on Mars.

**Parfit:** That is to ask about a further fact, a Cartesian ego that goes one way or the other. If there is no such thing, the question is empty.

**You:** Then let me put it differently. If what I care about in the future is that my projects continue, that the people I love are loved, and that someone with my values carries on, the replica gives me all of that. If what I care about is that *this* stream of experience continues, I don't know whether it would, and I'm not sure the question even makes sense. Your view asks me to shift what I care about from the second to the first. I can see the case for that. I'm still not stepping into the machine.

**Parfit:** Most people say that. Notice it is a feeling, not an argument.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether the chatbot understands

**Searle:** You've been talking with an AI system all afternoon. Do you think it understands you?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Searle in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It certainly seems to. It answered follow-up questions, picked up on jokes, corrected itself when I pointed out mistakes. If a person did all that, I'd say they understood.

**Searle:** And what is the system actually doing?

**You:** As far as I know, predicting the next piece of text from patterns in an enormous amount of training data.

**Searle:** Then it is manipulating symbols according to rules, as I was in my Chinese room. I produced perfect Chinese without understanding a word. Why think the system is different?

**Turing:** Because you are applying a standard to the machine that you never apply to people. You have no access to anyone's understanding except through what they say and do. If the machine's conversation is indistinguishable from a person's, refusing it understanding is a prejudice.

**Searle:** I have access to *my own* understanding, and I know that symbol manipulation is not it. I was in the room.

**Turing:** You were a part of the room. Your neurons don't understand English either.

**You:** That's the systems reply, isn't it? And Searle answers that he could memorize the rules and still not understand.

**Searle:** Precisely.

**You:** But I'm not sure I trust intuitions about a person memorizing billions of rules. Nobody could. Still, Turing, the imitation game only tests behavior. Can a system that has never seen, touched, or wanted anything mean "red" or "hungry" the way I do?

**Turing:** Then specify which further test you want, and we'll build a machine to pass it.

**You:** Here's where I come out. Behavior is the best evidence I have for understanding, in people or machines, so I shouldn't dismiss the system out of hand. But it's evidence, not a definition, and this system was built specifically to produce the behavior by a method quite unlike the one that produces it in me. That gives me a reason to be more cautious than I would be with a stranger. The real question is what understanding *is*, and neither the test nor the room answers that by itself.

</details>
