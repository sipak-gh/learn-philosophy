# Chapter 13 — Kant

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804) spent almost his whole life in Königsberg, the Baltic port in East Prussia (now Kaliningrad, Russia) where he was born, taught and died; apart from a few years as a private tutor in country houses nearby, he never left the province. The legend that the housewives set their clocks by his afternoon walk is probably exaggerated; the reality is stranger. Until his mid-fifties he was a respected, sociable lecturer on everything from physical geography to fireworks, known for his wit at dinner parties and for a theory of the origin of the solar system. Then, after more than a decade of near-silence, he published in 1781 the *Critique of Pure Reason*, followed within nine years by the *Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals*, the *Critique of Practical Reason* and the *Critique of the Power of Judgment*. These books divide the history of philosophy into before and after. Kant set out to settle the dispute between the rationalists and the empiricists of the last two chapters, to answer Hume, to secure the foundations of Newtonian science, and to make room for freedom, morality and faith, all at once. His solution, which he compared to the Copernican revolution, is that the world we know is partly shaped by the mind that knows it. This chapter is a guided tour of the first *Critique*, with shorter visits to the rest of the system. Kant's ethics gets a full chapter in the ethics course ([Kant: Duty, Dignity, and the Moral Law](../learn-ethics/09-kant.md)), so it is treated briefly here.

## The crisis Kant inherited

Kant was trained in the rationalist system of Leibniz as codified by Christian Wolff ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). Metaphysics was taught as a deductive science proving the existence of God, the immortality of the soul and the structure of the world from pure concepts. But the rationalists disagreed with one another endlessly, and the first *Critique* opens with a portrait of their predicament:

> Human reason, in one sphere of its cognition, is called upon to consider questions, which it cannot decline, as they are presented by its own nature, but which it cannot answer, as they transcend every faculty of the mind.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the First Edition (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

> The arena of these endless contests is called Metaphysic. Time was, when she was the queen of all the sciences
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the First Edition (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

Meanwhile mathematics and physics advanced steadily. Why could metaphysics not do the same? Then Kant read Hume ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)), probably in German translation:

> I openly confess, the suggestion of David Hume was the very thing, which many years ago first interrupted my dogmatic slumber, and gave my investigations in the field of speculative philosophy quite a new direction.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics*, Preface (tr. Paul Carus)

Hume had argued that the idea of necessary connection between cause and effect cannot come from experience, since we observe only constant conjunction, nor from reason, since the denial of any causal claim is not a contradiction. If Hume was right, the law of causality, on which all of Newton's physics rests, has no rational foundation. Kant generalized the problem: are there any truths that are both informative about the world *and* knowable independently of experience? If not, metaphysics is impossible. If so, how?

## Two distinctions and a question

Kant combines two distinctions. The first concerns how a judgment is **known**:

- **A priori**: knowable independently of experience (though experience may be needed to acquire the concepts). Marks: necessity and strict universality.
- **A posteriori**: knowable only through experience.

The second concerns what a judgment **says**:

- **Analytic**: the predicate is contained in the concept of the subject ("All bodies are extended"; "All bachelors are unmarried"). Denying it is a contradiction. It clarifies but adds nothing.
- **Synthetic**: the predicate adds something not contained in the subject ("All bodies are heavy"; "This bachelor is tall"). It extends knowledge.

Combine them:

| | Analytic | Synthetic |
|---|---|---|
| **A priori** | "All bodies are extended." Unproblematic. | "7 + 5 = 12"; "Every event has a cause." Kant's question. |
| **A posteriori** | Impossible: no need to consult experience for what is contained in a concept. | "The cat is on the mat." Unproblematic. |

Hume's fork recognized only two cells: relations of ideas (analytic a priori) and matters of fact (synthetic a posteriori). Kant argued that the most important truths fall in the remaining cell, the **synthetic a priori**. His example is arithmetic:

> We might, indeed at first suppose that the proposition 7 + 5 = 12 is a merely analytical proposition, following (according to the principle of contradiction) from the conception of a sum of seven and five. But if we regard it more narrowly, we find that our conception of the sum of seven and five contains nothing more than the uniting of both sums into one
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Introduction V (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

Nothing in the concept "the sum of 7 and 5" contains the concept "12"; we must count, using fingers or dots, that is, using intuition. Yet the result is necessary and known a priori. Geometry ("a straight line is the shortest between two points") and the basic principles of physics ("in all changes of the material world the quantity of matter remains unchanged"; "every event has a cause") are, Kant claims, also synthetic a priori. So:

> The proper problem of pure reason, then, is contained in the question: "How are synthetical judgements à priori possible?"
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Introduction VI (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

Whether such judgments exist is one of the great disputes of modern philosophy. Frege tried to show that arithmetic is analytic; non-Euclidean geometry and relativity undermined the claim that Euclid's geometry is necessarily true of space; the logical positivists denied that there are any synthetic a priori truths at all ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)); and Quine attacked the analytic/synthetic distinction itself ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

## The Copernican revolution

Kant's solution begins with a concession to empiricism and a twist:

> But, though all our knowledge begins with experience, it by no means follows that all arises out of experience. For, on the contrary, it is quite possible that our empirical knowledge is a compound of that which we receive through impressions, and that which the faculty of cognition supplies from itself
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Introduction I (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

If our knowledge must conform to objects, we can never know anything about them a priori, since we would have to wait and see. So Kant reverses the assumption:

> It has hitherto been assumed that our cognition must conform to the objects; but all attempts to ascertain anything about these objects à priori, by means of conceptions, and thus to extend the range of our knowledge, have been rendered abortive by this assumption. Let us then make the experiment whether we may not be more successful in metaphysics, if we assume that the objects must conform to our cognition.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the Second Edition (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

> We here propose to do just what COPERNICUS did in attempting to explain the celestial movements. When he found that he could make no progress by assuming that all the heavenly bodies revolved round the spectator, he reversed the process, and tried the experiment of assuming that the spectator revolved, while the stars remained at rest.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the Second Edition (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

A rough analogy: if you wear blue-tinted glasses that you can never remove, you can know a priori that everything you will ever see will be blue-tinted. You know this not by examining things but by knowing the glasses. Kant's claim is that space, time and certain basic concepts such as substance and cause are like the glasses: the forms through which anything must pass to become an object of experience for us. We can know a priori that every object of experience will conform to them, because they are conditions of anything being an object of experience at all.

Kant calls this inquiry **transcendental**: it concerns not objects but the conditions of the possibility of our knowing objects a priori. The view that results is **transcendental idealism**, combined with what he calls **empirical realism**. Within experience, tables and planets are perfectly real, public and law-governed, and not ideas in my head as Berkeley thought. But the framework of space, time and causality in which they appear is contributed by the mind, and we cannot know what things are like **in themselves**, independently of how they appear to beings like us.

## The faculties

Kant distinguishes two sources of knowledge. **Sensibility** is receptive: through it objects are *given* to us in **intuitions** (singular, immediate representations; Kant's term *Anschauung* has nothing to do with hunches). **Understanding** is active: through it objects are *thought* by means of **concepts** (general representations). Neither is enough without the other, and this sentence sums up Kant's verdict on both rationalism and empiricism:

> Thoughts without content are void; intuitions without conceptions, blind.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Logic, Introduction I (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

The rationalists tried to know the world with concepts alone and produced empty systems. The empiricists treated the mind as a passive recipient and could not explain the unity and necessity in our knowledge. Knowledge requires both.

### The Transcendental Aesthetic: space and time

"Aesthetic" here means the theory of sensibility (from Greek *aisthēsis*, perception). Kant argues that **space and time are forms of intuition**, not properties of things in themselves or relations between them. His arguments:

1. Space is not derived from experience, since to perceive things as outside me and outside one another I must already represent them in space:

> Space is not a conception which has been derived from outward experiences. For, in order that certain sensations may relate to something without me (that is, to something which occupies a different part of space from that in which I am); in like manner, in order that I may represent them not merely as without, of, and near to each other, but also in separate places, the representation of space must already exist as a foundation.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic §2 (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

2. I can imagine empty space, but I cannot imagine there being no space.
3. Space is represented as a single infinite whole of which particular spaces are parts, which is how intuitions, not concepts, behave.
4. Geometry gives us synthetic a priori knowledge of space; this is possible only if space is the form of our own sensibility.

The same arguments apply to time, the form of inner sense. The conclusion:

> objects are quite unknown to us in themselves, and what we call outward objects, are nothing else but mere representations of our sensibility, whose form is space, but whose real correlate, the thing in itself, is not known by means of these representations, nor ever can be
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Aesthetic §4 (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

Here critics pounce. Even if space is a form of our intuition, why could it not *also* be a feature of things in themselves? This is the "neglected alternative," pressed by Adolf Trendelenburg in the nineteenth century. And the geometry argument is weakened by the discovery of consistent non-Euclidean geometries, one of which turns out (in general relativity) to describe physical space better than Euclid's.

### The Transcendental Analytic: categories and the self

If sensibility supplies the forms of space and time, the understanding supplies twelve pure concepts or **categories**, derived (Kant claims) from the forms of logical judgment. They include **substance**, **cause** and **community** (reciprocal interaction), as well as unity, plurality, reality and necessity. They are the concepts without which we could not think of objects at all.

But what entitles us to apply them to experience? The **Transcendental Deduction**, the hardest and most important passage in the book (Kant rewrote it completely for the second edition), argues that the categories are conditions of the unity of consciousness. The key premise:

> The "I think" must accompany all my representations, for otherwise something would be represented in me which could not be thought; in other words, the representation would either be impossible, or at least be, in relation to me, nothing.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Analytic §12 (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

All my experiences must be able to be recognized as mine, belonging to a single consciousness; Kant calls this the **transcendental unity of apperception**. That unity is possible only if my representations are connected according to rules, and the categories are those rules. So any experience that I can call mine must be of a world of objects related by substance and cause. The argument answers Hume's bundle theory too ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)). Hume looked for the self among his perceptions and could not find it; Kant replies that the self is not one perception among others but the form of unity that makes them all perceptions of one subject. We never observe it, because it is what does the observing.

### Answering Hume on causation

In the Second Analogy, Kant applies this to causation. Consider the difference between looking at a house (scanning from roof to basement or basement to roof; the order of my perceptions is up to me) and watching a ship move downstream (I must see it upstream first, then downstream; the order is fixed). What distinguishes an objective event from a mere succession of my perceptions? Only that the order is governed by a rule: given the earlier state, the later one *must* follow. So the concept of necessary causal connection is not an inference from experience, as Hume thought, and cannot be undermined by Hume's skepticism about such inferences. It is a condition of our experiencing events in an objective time order at all.

Has Kant refuted Hume? He has shown, if the argument works, that every event must have *some* cause. He has not shown which particular causes produce which effects; for that we still need induction, and Hume's problem about particular inductive inferences remains.

## The limits of reason

The second half of the *Critique*, the **Transcendental Dialectic**, explains why metaphysics went wrong. Reason naturally seeks the **unconditioned**: the complete series of conditions for whatever is given. This produces three ideas: the **soul** (the unconditioned subject), the **world** as a totality, and **God** (the unconditioned ground of everything). These ideas are useful as **regulative** ideals that guide inquiry, but when we take them to give knowledge of objects beyond experience, reason falls into **transcendental illusion**, a natural and inevitable illusion like the moon looking larger at the horizon.

Kant's image of the metaphysician:

> The light dove cleaving in free flight the thin air, whose resistance it feels, might imagine that her movements would be far more free and rapid in airless space. Just in the same way did Plato, abandoning the world of sense because of the narrow limits it sets to the understanding, venture upon the wings of ideas beyond it, into the void space of pure intellect.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Introduction II (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

The resistance of experience is not an obstacle to knowledge but its condition.

1. **The Paralogisms.** Rational psychology argues from "I think" to the soul being a simple, persisting, immaterial substance (as Descartes did, [Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). Kant shows these inferences trade on an ambiguity: the formal unity of the "I think" is mistaken for intuition of a substance.

2. **The Antinomies.** Reason produces pairs of equally good proofs of contradictory conclusions. The first:

> The world has a beginning in time, and is also limited in regard to space.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, First Antinomy, Thesis (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

> The world has no beginning, and no limits in space, but is, in relation both to time and space, infinite.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, First Antinomy, Antithesis (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

Others concern whether there are simple parts, whether there is freedom alongside natural causality, and whether there is a necessary being. Kant's diagnosis is that both sides assume the world is a thing in itself, a completed whole. Once we see that the world of appearances is never given as a whole, the first two antinomies dissolve. The third, freedom versus determinism, gets a different resolution: as appearances, our actions are fully determined by natural causes, but as things in themselves we may be free. Kant does not claim to prove we are free, only that freedom is not contradicted by science.

3. **The critique of rational theology.** Kant classifies all proofs of God as ontological, cosmological or physico-theological (the design argument), and argues the last two depend on the first. He then refutes the ontological argument of Anselm and Descartes ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md), [Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)) with a famous claim:

> Being is evidently not a real predicate, that is, a conception of something which is added to the conception of some other thing. It is merely the positing of a thing, or of certain determinations in it.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Dialectic, "Of the Impossibility of an Ontological Proof" (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

> A hundred real dollars contain no more than a hundred possible dollars.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Transcendental Dialectic, "Of the Impossibility of an Ontological Proof" (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

(Kant's word is *Taler*.) The concept of a hundred dollars is the same whether or not they exist; existence makes a difference to my finances, not to my concept. So existence cannot be one of the perfections included in the concept of God. Frege and Russell later formalized the point: existence is not a property of individuals but of concepts (being instantiated) and is expressed by a quantifier ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)).

The result is that we can neither prove nor disprove God, freedom or immortality. In a sentence that has been read both as pious and as subversive:

> I must, therefore, abolish knowledge, to make room for belief.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Pure Reason*, Preface to the Second Edition (tr. J. M. D. Meiklejohn)

The German is *Glauben*, also translated "faith." Theoretical reason clears the ground; practical reason, the reason that governs action, will build on it.

## Practical reason, briefly

The ethics course covers Kant's moral philosophy in depth ([Kant: Duty, Dignity, and the Moral Law](../learn-ethics/09-kant.md)). In outline: the *Groundwork* (1785) begins with a claim about value:

> Nothing can possibly be conceived in the world, or even out of it, which can be called good, without qualification, except a good will.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, First Section (tr. Thomas Kingsmill Abbott)

Even if a good will achieved nothing, "like a jewel, it would still shine by its own light." Morality consists of **categorical imperatives**, commands that bind every rational being regardless of desires, as opposed to **hypothetical imperatives** ("if you want X, do Y"). There is one supreme principle, stated in several formulas:

> Act only on that maxim whereby thou canst at the same time will that it should become a universal law.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Second Section (tr. Thomas Kingsmill Abbott)

> So act as to treat humanity, whether in thine own person or in that of any other, in every case as an end withal, never as means only.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Fundamental Principles of the Metaphysic of Morals*, Second Section (tr. Thomas Kingsmill Abbott)

The connection to the first *Critique* is **autonomy**. A moral agent gives the law to herself through reason. This is possible only if she is free, and freedom, which theoretical reason could only show to be possible, is guaranteed for practical purposes by our consciousness of the moral law: I ought, therefore I can. The *Critique of Practical Reason* (1788) adds that we must **postulate** God and immortality as conditions of the highest good, virtue rewarded with happiness. Its Conclusion opens with the sentence now inscribed on a memorial plaque at Kant's tomb beside Königsberg Cathedral:

> Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and the more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above and the moral law within.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Practical Reason*, Conclusion (tr. Thomas Kingsmill Abbott)

## Beauty, the sublime and judgment

The *Critique of Judgement* (1790) founded modern aesthetics. A judgment of taste ("this rose is beautiful") is puzzling. It is based on a feeling of pleasure, so it is subjective; yet we speak as if everyone ought to agree, unlike "this wine is pleasant to me." Kant's analysis has four "moments." Beauty pleases **disinterestedly**, without desire for the object's existence or use. It pleases **universally**:

> The beautiful is that which pleases universally, without a concept.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Judgement* §9 (tr. J. H. Bernard)

It has the form of **purposiveness without purpose**:

> Beauty is the form of the purposiveness of an object, so far as this is perceived in it without any representation of a purpose.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Judgement* §17 (tr. J. H. Bernard)

A flower or a melody seems designed for our faculties, as if made to be understood, though no purpose can be named. And its pleasure is **necessary**: it arises from the free play of imagination and understanding, which, because these faculties are shared, we can expect in everyone.

The **sublime** is different:

> We call that sublime which is absolutely great.
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Critique of Judgement* §25 (tr. J. H. Bernard)

Facing a storm at sea or the vastness of the night sky, the imagination fails to grasp the whole, and we feel pain at our smallness. But reason can think the infinite, and we feel a higher pleasure at a power in ourselves that exceeds all nature. Pascal's terror at infinite spaces ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)) becomes, for Kant, an experience of our own dignity.

## Enlightenment and perpetual peace

In 1784, a Berlin magazine asked "What is Enlightenment?" Kant's answer begins: Enlightenment is humanity's exit from its self-incurred immaturity (*Unmündigkeit*), the inability to use one's own understanding without another's guidance. Its motto is *Sapere aude!*, "dare to know," which Kant glosses as "have courage to use your own understanding." Why do so many remain immature?

> Faulheit und Feigheit sind die Ursachen, warum ein so großer Teil der Menschen, nachdem sie die Natur längst von fremder Leitung freigesprochen (naturaliter maiorennes), dennoch gerne zeitlebens unmündig bleiben
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Beantwortung der Frage: Was ist Aufklärung?* (1784); "Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why so great a part of humanity, long after nature has freed them from the guidance of others, nevertheless gladly remain immature for life"

It is comfortable, Kant adds, to have a book that understands for me, a pastor who has a conscience for me, a doctor who judges my diet for me. Michel Foucault's essay of the same title (1984) asks what such a "critique of the present" means after Kant ([Chapter 23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md)).

In *Perpetual Peace* (1795), Kant proposed conditions for ending war: standing armies should be abolished over time; no state should interfere by force in another's constitution; and three "definitive articles," the first of which is:

> "The civil constitution of each state shall be republican."
>
> — Immanuel Kant, *Perpetual Peace*, First Definitive Article (tr. M. Campbell Smith)

The second calls for a federation of free states, the third for a "cosmopolitan right" of hospitality to strangers. Republics, Kant argued, are slow to go to war because the citizens who must pay and fight decide. This is the ancestor of "democratic peace theory" in international relations and of the League of Nations and the United Nations ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)).

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Two worlds or two aspects?** Is the thing in itself a second, hidden object behind each appearance (the "two-world" reading, which makes Kant close to Berkeley plus an unknowable extra), or are appearances and things in themselves the same things considered in two ways, as they relate to our sensibility and as they might be considered apart from it (Henry Allison's "two-aspect" reading)? P. F. Strawson tried to keep Kant's analysis of the conditions of experience while discarding transcendental idealism altogether; many analytic Kantians follow him.

**Transcendental arguments.** Kant's distinctive argument form (X is a condition of the possibility of experience; we have experience; therefore X) was revived by Strawson against skepticism. Barry Stroud ("Transcendental Arguments," 1968) argued that such arguments can at best show how we must *think*, not how the world must *be*, unless they rely on verificationism or idealism.

**Kant's racism.** Kant lectured for decades on anthropology and physical geography, and these lectures contain racial hierarchies and contemptuous claims about non-Europeans. Robert Bernasconi, Charles Mills and Pauline Kleingeld have debated whether this infects his moral universalism or contradicts it; Kleingeld argues that Kant revised his views in the 1790s, notably in *Perpetual Peace*'s condemnation of colonialism.

**Kant and science.** Neo-Kantians (Hermann Cohen, Ernst Cassirer) and later Michael Friedman argued that relativity and quantum theory require not abandoning Kant but "relativizing" the a priori: frameworks that make empirical inquiry possible but that change in scientific revolutions ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

## Living it

**Find your glasses.** Kant's central idea is that what we experience is shaped by the forms we bring to it. Pick a recurrent experience (a conflict at work, your reaction to the news, your view of a relative) and ask which "forms" you bring to it that the experience itself did not supply: the categories you sort people into, the causal story you assume, the timeframe you use. You cannot step outside all forms, Kant says, but you can become aware of the ones that are yours rather than everyone's.

**Dare to know.** For one week, notice each time you let a "guardian" think for you: a reviewer, an algorithm, an expert, a chatbot. Kant does not ask you never to rely on others; that would be impossible. He asks that you be capable of judging for yourself and choose when to defer. Pick one question you have outsourced and work it out yourself.

**Seek the sublime.** Spend an evening under a dark sky, or by the sea in bad weather, and notice both feelings Kant describes: the sense of being overwhelmed, then the second feeling that the mind that grasps its own smallness is somehow not small. Write a paragraph on whether Kant's description fits your experience.

## Summary

- Kant inherited a stalemate between rationalists and empiricists and was woken from his "dogmatic slumber" by Hume's critique of causation.
- Analytic/synthetic and a priori/a posteriori: the key question is how synthetic a priori judgments (mathematics, the principles of physics) are possible.
- The Copernican revolution: objects conform to our cognition; transcendental idealism and empirical realism; things in themselves are unknowable.
- Sensibility and understanding: "Thoughts without content are void; intuitions without conceptions, blind." Space and time are forms of intuition; the categories are rules of thought; the "I think" and the unity of apperception; causation as a condition of objective time order (the answer to Hume).
- The Dialectic: reason's ideas of soul, world and God produce illusion when used to claim knowledge; the paralogisms; the antinomies; existence is not a real predicate. Knowledge is limited "to make room for belief."
- Practical philosophy: the good will, the categorical imperative, humanity as an end, autonomy, the starry heavens and the moral law ([the Ethics course, Chapter 9](../learn-ethics/09-kant.md)).
- Aesthetics: beauty as disinterested, universal, purposive without purpose; the sublime as the mind's discovery of its superiority to nature.
- Enlightenment as exit from self-incurred immaturity; republics, federation and cosmopolitan right as the path to peace.

## Dialogues

### Dialogue 1 — On whether you could ever know reality as it is

**Immanuel Kant:** You told me that physics describes the world as it really is, independently of us. Does it?

**You:** It is our best attempt. Physics tells us that a table is mostly empty space with particles and fields, not the solid brown thing I see.

*Your turn: Kant will ask whether physics escapes the forms of human experience. Write two or three sentences on whether scientific description gives us things as they are in themselves.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Physics uses instruments and mathematics, so it goes beyond the limits of our senses. We cannot see electrons, but we know they are there.

**Immanuel Kant:** How do you know an electron is there?

**You:** By its effects, on detectors, which we then observe.

**Immanuel Kant:** So the electron is known as the cause of certain appearances, located in space and time, and measured in magnitudes. Is that not still the world as it must appear to beings with our forms of intuition and our categories? You have extended the range of experience, not escaped it.

**You:** But modern physics doesn't even keep your forms. Relativity says space and time are not separate, and Euclid's geometry is not true of large-scale space. Quantum mechanics seems to break ordinary causation. So the mind does not impose the forms you said it did.

**Immanuel Kant:** That is a serious objection. I claimed that Euclidean geometry is necessarily true of space as we intuit it. If physics finds otherwise, either my account of intuition was too narrow, or physical space is not the space of intuition. Which do you think?

**You:** I think you overreached about geometry. But I notice that relativity and quantum mechanics still describe events in some spatiotemporal framework, with laws. Maybe the general idea survives even if your specific forms do not.

**Immanuel Kant:** Then you have arrived where some of my successors did: there are conditions that make any experience and any science possible, but they may change as science develops. The question remains whether we can get beyond all such conditions to the thing in itself.

**You:** I don't see how. Any description I give will be in some concepts, from some point of view. But it seems strange to say there is a reality we can know nothing about. How do you even know it exists?

**Immanuel Kant:** That objection was made in my own lifetime, by Jacobi: without the thing in itself one cannot enter my system, and with it one cannot stay. I say only that appearance implies something that appears. I do not claim to know what it is.

**You:** Then here is my position. The idea that all knowledge is shaped by the knower's forms is convincing, and science does not escape it. But I am less sure the forms are fixed, and I am suspicious of the unknowable thing in itself. Perhaps "the world as it is in itself" is just the limit of ever better descriptions, not a hidden object.

**Immanuel Kant:** That is close to what I called a regulative idea: a goal that guides inquiry but is never reached. You may be more Kantian than you think.

</details>

### Dialogue 2 — On thinking for yourself in an age of experts and machines

**Immanuel Kant:** I wrote that it is comfortable to be immature: to have a book that understands for me, a pastor who has a conscience for me. What would I add today?

**You:** A search engine that knows for me, an app that decides my route, and a chatbot that writes my essays.

*Your turn: is relying on such tools a failure of Enlightenment, or a reasonable division of labor? Write two or three sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It's a division of labor. I can't check everything myself. I trust my doctor and my map app the way I trust a bridge engineer. Refusing to rely on experts would be foolish.

**Immanuel Kant:** I agree. Immaturity is not relying on others; it is the *inability* to use your understanding without guidance. Could you still find your way without the app?

**You:** In my own city, probably. In a new city, no.

**Immanuel Kant:** And if the app sent you into a river?

**You:** I would notice. Mostly.

**Immanuel Kant:** "Mostly." That is the question. Do you still have a judgment of your own that can check the guide, or has the guide become your judgment?

**You:** With maps, I can check. With the chatbot, it is harder. It writes fluently on everything, and I can't easily tell when it is wrong.

**Immanuel Kant:** Then that is where your immaturity is most comfortable, and most dangerous. Let me also ask about the public use of reason. I said that a scholar must be free to publish arguments to the whole reading public. Where does that happen today?

**You:** Online, in theory. In practice, the platforms decide what gets seen, and people mostly read what confirms what they already think.

**Immanuel Kant:** Then the freedom to publish is not enough. Enlightenment needs readers who are willing to think, and a public that rewards argument. Neither is guaranteed.

**You:** So what should I do?

**Immanuel Kant:** What I said in 1784: have the courage to use your own understanding. Not to know everything yourself, but to keep the capacity to judge, and to exercise it where it matters.

**You:** Then here is my position. Relying on experts and tools is not immaturity, as long as I keep enough understanding to check them and to know when I can't. For the things that matter most, my beliefs about right and wrong, my vote and my work, I should think them through myself, even if a machine could do it faster. And I should ask of every tool I use whether it makes me more capable of judging, or less.

**Immanuel Kant:** That is a good maxim. Could you will it as a universal law?

**You:** I think I could. It would make for a slower world, but a freer one.

</details>
