# Chapter 19 — Phenomenology and Existentialism

While Russell and the Vienna Circle were turning philosophy toward logic, a different revolution was starting in Germany. Edmund Husserl argued that philosophy had been so busy building theories about the world that it had forgotten to look at how the world actually shows up in experience. His method, **phenomenology**, the careful description of experience from the first-person point of view, was taken up by his student Martin Heidegger and turned into a new account of what it is to be a human being. In occupied Paris, Jean-Paul Sartre, Simone de Beauvoir, Maurice Merleau-Ponty and Albert Camus used it to ask how to live in a world without given meaning, and **existentialism** became the most famous philosophy of the twentieth century, discussed in cafés and fashion magazines as well as seminar rooms. This chapter explains the method, the main thinkers and their arguments, and why both movements still matter for philosophy of mind, ethics and the way you live. The existentialists' encounter with nihilism is treated in depth in the Nihilism course; here the focus is on the philosophical core. Previous: [Chapter 18 — The Analytic Turn](18-the-analytic-turn.md). Next: [Chapter 20 — Philosophy of Mind](20-philosophy-of-mind.md).

## Back to the things themselves

### Brentano and intentionality

The story begins with Franz Brentano (1838–1917), a former priest who taught in Vienna and whose students included Husserl, Freud and the founder of Czechoslovakia, Tomáš Masaryk. In *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874) Brentano proposed a mark of the mental. Every mental state, he said, is *about* something: you see a tree, fear a dog, hope for rain, think of a unicorn. This aboutness he called **intentionality**, borrowing a term from medieval philosophy. (It has nothing to do with intentions in the sense of plans.) Physical things are not about anything; a stone does not point beyond itself. And the object of a mental act need not exist: you can think of a unicorn or fear a burglar who is not there.

Intentionality remains one of the central puzzles of the philosophy of mind ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)): how can a lump of matter like a brain be *about* Paris? Phenomenology began as the attempt to describe intentional experience exactly.

### Two precursors: James and Bergson

Two thinkers outside Germany had already insisted that experience, examined honestly, looks nothing like the atoms-of-sensation picture of the British empiricists. William James, in a chapter Husserl read closely:

> Consciousness, then, does not appear to itself chopped up in bits. Such words as 'chain' or 'train' do not describe it fitly as it presents itself in the first instance. It is nothing jointed; it flows. A 'river' or a 'stream' are the metaphors by which it is most naturally described.
>
> — William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, vol. 1, Chapter IX

And the French philosopher Henri Bergson (1859–1941), who won the Nobel Prize in literature and whose lectures in Paris were so crowded that society ladies sent servants to hold seats, distinguished the time of clocks and physics, a series of identical points laid out like space, from the **duration** (*durée*) we live:

> Pure duration is the form which the succession of our conscious states assumes when our ego lets itself *live*, when it refrains from separating its present state from its former states.
>
> — Henri Bergson, *Time and Free Will*, Chapter II (tr. F. L. Pogson)

Bergson's example is a melody. Each note is heard in light of those before it, the whole melts into an organic unity, and you cannot cut it into instants without destroying what you hear. Phenomenology would make this kind of observation into a discipline.

### Husserl's method

Edmund Husserl (1859–1938) trained as a mathematician, studied with Brentano, and spent his career at Göttingen and Freiburg trying to make philosophy a "rigorous science." His motto, from the *Logical Investigations* (1900–1901), was *zu den Sachen selbst*: "to the things themselves." Instead of arguing from theories about what experience must be (sense-data, brain states, ideas in the mind), describe what is actually given, exactly as it is given.

The main tool is the **phenomenological reduction**, or **epoché** (a Greek skeptics' word for suspending judgment; see [Chapter 7 — Hellenistic Philosophy](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)). In ordinary life we live in the **natural attitude**: we take for granted that the world exists out there, and we are absorbed in dealing with it. The epoché asks you to "bracket" that assumption. You do not deny that the world exists, as a skeptic might; you suspend the question, so as to attend to how the world *appears*, to the structures of experience in which anything at all is given to you.

Try it with a coffee cup. In the natural attitude you just see a cup. Bracket its existence and attend to the seeing, and you notice things that were always there:

- You never see the whole cup. You see one side, and the back is **co-intended** as there, hidden, anticipated. Turn the cup and new sides appear while the old ones slip into the background. A physical object is given only in **adumbrations** (*Abschattungen*, "shadings" or profiles) that point beyond themselves.
- The perception has a **horizon**: the cup sits on a table, in a room, in a world, all implicitly present.
- The experience has a temporal structure: each moment of perception holds on to the just-past (**retention**) and leans toward what is about to come (**protention**). This is Husserl's version of James's stream and Bergson's melody, worked out in the *Lectures on the Phenomenology of Inner Time-Consciousness* (1928).
- Each act of consciousness (the **noesis**: seeing, remembering, imagining) correlates with an object-as-intended (the **noema**: the cup as seen, as remembered, as imagined).

A second tool is **eidetic variation**: to find the essence (*eidos*) of something, vary it in imagination and see which changes destroy it. Can a cup be blue instead of white? Yes. Can it have no back? No. Can a melody be heard without temporal succession? No. What survives all variation is essential.

Husserl's mature works took a turn many followers disliked. *Ideas I* (1913) and the *Cartesian Meditations* (1931) presented phenomenology as a **transcendental idealism** in which the world is "constituted" by a pure, transcendental ego, a modern version of Descartes' starting point ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). His late *Crisis of European Sciences* (1936), written after he had been stripped of his university privileges by the Nazi regime because of his Jewish birth, introduced the **lifeworld** (*Lebenswelt*): the pre-scientific world of everyday experience from which all science starts, and which modern science, since Galileo's mathematization of nature, has forgotten and covered over with its abstractions.

Two of Husserl's assistants deserve mention. Edith Stein wrote her dissertation on **empathy**, the experience of grasping another person's experience as theirs; she later converted to Catholicism, became a Carmelite nun, and was murdered at Auschwitz in 1942. She is now a canonized saint. The other assistant was Martin Heidegger.

## Heidegger: being-in-the-world

Martin Heidegger (1889–1976) dedicated *Being and Time* (1927) to Husserl and then, in effect, repudiated him. The book is difficult, unfinished, full of coinages, and probably the most influential work of European philosophy in the twentieth century. Its question is the oldest in metaphysics: what does it mean *to be*? Heidegger thought the tradition since Plato had forgotten the question by treating being as a kind of thing, the highest or most general entity. The way back to it, he argued, is through the one entity for whom its own being is an issue: us.

He calls this entity **Dasein** (German for "existence," literally "being-there"), to avoid the loaded words "subject," "consciousness," "mind" or "man." Dasein's being is **existence**: it is not a thing with fixed properties but always ahead of itself, projecting possibilities, taking a stand on who it is.

### The hammer

Husserl started with a perceiver looking at a cup. Heidegger thought this was already a distortion. Most of the time we do not *observe* things; we *use* them. When a carpenter hammers, the hammer is not an object of attention at all. It withdraws into the activity. Heidegger calls this mode of being **ready-to-hand** (*zuhanden*). The hammer shows up as for-driving-nails, related to nails, boards, the house being built, and ultimately the purposes of the person building it. Only when something breaks down, when the hammer is too heavy or the head flies off, does it become **present-at-hand** (*vorhanden*), an object with properties to be stared at.

The lesson is that the theoretical stance of science and traditional philosophy, a detached subject looking at objects, is derivative. Our basic way of being is **being-in-the-world**, practical, absorbed, skilled coping in a meaningful environment. There is no gap between mind and world to be bridged, so Descartes' problem of the external world never properly arises. The "scandal of philosophy," Heidegger said in reply to Kant, is not that no proof of the external world has been given, but that such proofs are expected and attempted. The philosopher Hubert Dreyfus later used this analysis to argue, in *What Computers Can't Do* (1972), that the symbolic artificial intelligence of his day would fail, because human intelligence is not rule-following over representations but embodied coping. The debate continues in [Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md).

### The They, anxiety and death

Who am I, in everyday life? Mostly, Heidegger says, I am **the They** (*das Man*, "the one"): I do what one does, think what one thinks, find shocking what one finds shocking. This is not a moral failing but the normal condition; you learn to be a person by learning what one does. But it means everyday life is largely **inauthentic** (*uneigentlich*, literally "not one's own").

What breaks the grip of the They is **anxiety** (*Angst*). Fear has an object (a dog, an exam). Anxiety has none; it is about being-in-the-world as such. In anxiety the familiar world loses its significance and you feel "not at home" (*unheimlich*, uncanny). And above all, anxiety reveals **death**. Death is the one possibility that is radically my own (no one can die my death for me), certain, of indefinite timing, and the end of all my possibilities. The They tranquilizes it ("one dies," someday, not yet). **Being-toward-death**, facing one's mortality without evasion, individualizes Dasein, frees it from the They, and makes possible an **authentic** existence in which you own your choices. Authenticity in Heidegger is a matter of *how* you choose, not *what*: he gives it no substantive content, which critics call empty and defenders call the point ([the Nihilism course, Chapter 9](../learn-nihilism/09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md)). Compare the Stoic meditation on death ([the Stoicism course, Chapter 12](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md)) and Tolstoy's *The Death of Ivan Ilyich*, which Heidegger cites.

Heidegger's 1929 lecture "What Is Metaphysics?" developed the theme of anxiety and "the nothing" that Carnap attacked ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)); the full story is in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 9](../learn-nihilism/09-heidegger-and-the-nothing.md). His later work turned to language, poetry and a critique of technology as a way of revealing that turns everything, including people, into "standing reserve" for use.

### The Nazi problem

In April 1933 Heidegger became rector of the University of Freiburg and joined the Nazi Party. His rectoral address called for the university to serve the German people's destiny; he implemented the regime's policies, including measures against Jewish colleagues, and he never publicly retracted or apologized after 1945. The publication of his *Black Notebooks* from 2014 onward showed that his antisemitism was not a passing opportunism but was woven into his philosophical reflections on the history of being. Students of Heidegger must decide what follows. One view (Emmanuel Faye) holds that the philosophy is contaminated at its core. Another (Hannah Arendt, his former student and lover, in a qualified way; Richard Rorty) holds that powerful insights can be separated from a terrible man. A third asks what in the philosophy, its contempt for "the They," its stress on rootedness and destiny, its distance from ordinary moral argument, made it so useless as a defense against Nazism. All three questions are worth asking.

## The roots of existentialism

Existentialism took phenomenology's method and Heidegger's analysis of existence and turned them into an ethics of freedom. But its themes are older.

**Blaise Pascal** (1623–1662), mathematician and Catholic apologist, described the human situation in terms the existentialists would recognize: a creature lost between infinity and nothingness, distracted from its condition by "diversion," and terrified by a universe indifferent to it.

> The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §206 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

> Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking reed.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* §347 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

**Søren Kierkegaard** (1813–1855) is usually called the first existentialist (see [Chapter 14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md) and [the Nihilism course, Chapter 8](../learn-nihilism/08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md)). Against Hegel's system, which absorbed every individual into the march of world history, he insisted that the most important truths are those an individual must live, appropriate with passion and choose. His complaint about his own age was not that it was wicked:

> Let others complain that the times are wicked. I complain that they are paltry; for they are without passion.
>
> — Søren Kierkegaard, *Either/Or*, "Diapsalmata," in *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

Kierkegaard's analysis of anxiety as the dizziness of freedom, his attack on "the crowd," and his insistence that faith requires a leap that no reasoning can justify all passed into Heidegger and Sartre, minus the God.

**Fyodor Dostoevsky**'s underground man (1864) refuses to accept that human beings can be explained by reason and self-interest, because a person will act against both just to prove that he is free:

> I admit that twice two makes four is an excellent thing, but if we are to give everything its due, twice two makes five is sometimes a very charming thing too.
>
> — Fyodor Dostoevsky, *Notes from the Underground*, Part I, Chapter IX (tr. Constance Garnett)

And **Friedrich Nietzsche**'s madman announced the event that set the existentialist problem: "God is dead! God remains dead! And we have killed him!" (*The Joyful Wisdom* §125, tr. Thomas Common). If there is no God, there is no given human nature, no cosmic purpose and no external source of value. What then? Nietzsche's answer is the subject of [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md) and the Nihilism course ([Chapters 6](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md) and [7](../learn-nihilism/07-nietzsche-beyond-nihilism.md)). The French existentialists gave another.

## Sartre: condemned to be free

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905–1980) learned phenomenology in Berlin in 1933–34 and returned to write novels, plays and the 700-page *Being and Nothingness* (1943), written during the German occupation. After the liberation, in October 1945, he gave a public lecture in Paris so crowded that people fainted. Published as *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, it is the clearest short statement of his view, though Sartre later regretted its simplifications.

### Existence precedes essence

A paper knife, Sartre says, is made by an artisan who has a concept of it in mind: its essence (what it is for, how it is made) comes before its existence. If God created human beings, the same would be true of us: there would be a human nature, a purpose we were made for. But if there is no God, there is at least one being whose existence comes before its essence. Sartre's formula is that for human beings "existence precedes essence": we first exist, turn up in the world, and only afterward define ourselves by what we do. There is no human nature that settles what you should be. You are what you make of yourself.

The consequences follow quickly:

- **Radical freedom.** Since nothing fixes what you are, you are always free to choose, and you cannot escape choosing; refusing to choose is a choice. In a phrase that became famous, we are condemned to be free.
- **Total responsibility.** You are responsible for what you are, and, Sartre adds, in choosing for yourself you choose an image of what a human being should be, and so choose for all humanity.
- **Anguish, abandonment, despair.** Anguish is the awareness of this responsibility; abandonment is the lack of any outside authority (God, human nature, a moral code) to make the choice for you; despair is acting without guarantees that things will turn out as you hope.

Sartre's example: during the occupation a student came to him torn between joining the Free French in England to avenge his brother and staying to care for his mother, whose only consolation he was. Christian doctrine says love your neighbor, but which neighbor? Kant says never treat anyone merely as a means, but either choice uses someone. Even consulting a feeling does not help; you discover how strong the feeling is only by acting on it. Sartre's advice, in effect, was that the student was free and had to choose, which is to say invent. Whether this is liberating or an abdication of moral reasoning is a central question in [the Ethics course, Chapter 16](../learn-ethics/16-existentialist-ethics.md).

### Being-for-itself and nothingness

Sartre's metaphysics distinguishes **being-in-itself** (*en-soi*), the solid, self-identical being of things, which simply is what it is, from **being-for-itself** (*pour-soi*), consciousness, which is never simply what it is. Consciousness is always conscious *of* something it is not (intentionality again), and it can always step back from itself, question, negate, imagine otherwise. Sartre calls this capacity **nothingness**. You wait for your friend Pierre in a café; he is not there, and his absence fills the café, which organizes itself around the gap. Only a being that can relate to what is not could experience that.

### Bad faith

The most famous section of *Being and Nothingness* describes **bad faith** (*mauvaise foi*): lying to yourself about your freedom. A waiter in a café moves a little too precisely, a little too quickly, bends forward a little too eagerly; he is *playing at being* a waiter, trying to make himself a waiter the way an inkwell is an inkwell. A woman on a first date lets her companion take her hand and leaves it there, inert, neither accepting nor refusing, treating her hand as a thing so as to avoid deciding. In both cases a person treats themselves as a fixed object (in-itself) to escape the anxiety of being free.

But bad faith can run the other way, too. Sartre's homosexual man who refuses to admit what he is, and his "champion of sincerity" friend who demands that he confess "I am a homosexual" as if that were a fixed essence, are both in bad faith. Human beings are always both **facticity** (the given facts: your body, past, situation) and **transcendence** (your capacity to go beyond them). Bad faith collapses one into the other, either "I'm just like that" or "my past doesn't count at all."

### The look

Sartre's account of other people starts with an experience. You are peering through a keyhole, absorbed, and then you hear footsteps behind you. Suddenly you *are* something, a voyeur, in someone else's eyes. You experience yourself as an object for another freedom that you cannot control. **The look** (*le regard*) reveals that other minds exist (so the philosophical problem of other minds is answered in shame, not argument), but it also makes human relations a permanent conflict of freedoms, each trying to escape being objectified by the other. Sartre's play *No Exit* (1944), with its famous closing line about hell and other people, dramatizes this. Sartre said later that the line was widely misunderstood: the point is that we depend on others' judgments to know ourselves, and that a hell is made when those relationships go bad.

Sartre later tried, in the *Critique of Dialectical Reason* (1960), to reconcile his philosophy of freedom with Marxism, and he spent his last decades as a political activist; he refused the Nobel Prize in 1964. His positions on Stalin's camps and other matters drew sharp criticism, including from former allies such as Camus and Merleau-Ponty.

## Beauvoir: situated freedom and the second sex

Simone de Beauvoir (1908–1986) was Sartre's lifelong companion and intellectual partner, and for decades she was treated as his follower. Recent scholarship (Margaret Simons, Kate Kirkpatrick, Sonia Kruks) has shown that she developed key ideas independently and corrected Sartre on the point where his philosophy was weakest: the relation between freedom and **situation**.

In *The Ethics of Ambiguity* (1947) Beauvoir argued that existentialism is not a license for anything. The human condition is ambiguous: we are both free subjects and material objects, both separate and dependent on others. To will oneself free, authentically, is also to will the freedom of others, because my projects need a world of free people to take them up. Oppression is the deliberate closing off of others' possibilities, and it is the central evil. She also argued, against Sartre's early view that the prisoner in chains is as free as his jailer, that situations can make freedom almost impossible. The woman in a harem, raised never to question her condition, is not in bad faith for failing to rebel; her situation has mystified her. Freedom is real but can be crushed or expanded by social conditions.

*The Second Sex* (1949), a founding text of modern feminism, applies this analysis to women. Its question is "What is a woman?" Its answer begins with an asymmetry: man is defined as the human norm, the Subject, the Absolute, and woman is defined relative to him, as the **Other**. Through biology, psychoanalysis, history, myth and the stages of a woman's life, Beauvoir shows how this otherness is produced and maintained. Its most famous sentence opens the second volume:

> One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.
>
> — Simone de Beauvoir, *The Second Sex*, Volume II (tr. H. M. Parshley, 1953)

The claim is not that biology is irrelevant but that being a woman, in the full social sense, is not a natural fact. It is a situation into which one is raised, shaped by expectations, institutions and the gaze of others, and which one takes up, resists, or transforms. The sentence anticipated the later distinction between sex and gender and is the root of much feminist philosophy (see [Chapter 22 — Political Philosophy](22-political-philosophy.md) and [the Ethics course, Chapter 14](../learn-ethics/14-care-and-feminist-ethics.md)).

Beauvoir's analysis was extended by **Frantz Fanon** (1925–1961), a psychiatrist from Martinique, in *Black Skin, White Masks* (1952). In its fifth chapter, on the lived experience of the Black man, Fanon describes a child on a train in France pointing at him and calling out to his mother in fear. In that moment he experiences his body as fixed from outside by a white gaze that has already decided what he is, a crushing version of Sartre's look. Fanon used phenomenology to analyze racism as a structure of lived experience, a project continued today by critical phenomenologists such as Lewis Gordon, Linda Martín Alcoff, Sara Ahmed and Lisa Guenther.

## Merleau-Ponty: the body that knows

Maurice Merleau-Ponty (1908–1961) took phenomenology in the direction that has proved most fruitful for science. *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945) argues that the subject of perception is not a disembodied consciousness, as in Descartes and in much of Husserl and Sartre, but the **lived body** (*le corps propre*).

Consider how you reach for a cup. You do not calculate its coordinates and then instruct your arm; your hand simply goes to it, shaped in advance to its size and weight. Merleau-Ponty calls this **motor intentionality**: the body's own practical grasp of the world, below the level of explicit thought. A skilled typist does not know where the keys are in a way she could state, but her fingers know. A blind man's cane is not an object he perceives but an extension of his body through which he perceives the pavement. Habits are not mechanical reflexes but bodily knowledge, a way the world is arranged for you.

Two of Merleau-Ponty's favorite cases come from neurology. Patients with a **phantom limb** feel an amputated arm, and try to use it, because the body schema still projects a world in which the arm has work to do. And Husserl's example of one hand touching the other shows that the body is both subject and object at once: the touching hand can become the touched, though never quite at the same moment. Late in life Merleau-Ponty called this reversibility the **flesh** of the world.

The idea that cognition is rooted in bodily skill and action is now a research program, known as **embodied** or **enactive cognition**, carried on by Francisco Varela, Evan Thompson, Alva Noë and Shaun Gallagher, among others. It is one of phenomenology's main legacies in [Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md).

## Camus: the absurd

Albert Camus (1913–1960), born into poverty in French Algeria, rejected the label "existentialist," and his famous break with Sartre in 1952 over Soviet communism confirmed the distance. But his name is permanently attached to the movement. *The Myth of Sisyphus* (1942) begins with the claim that there is only one truly serious philosophical problem: suicide, the question whether life is worth living.

The **absurd**, for Camus, is not a fact about the world or about us but the confrontation between the human demand for meaning and clarity and the unreasonable silence of the world. It is Pascal's infinite spaces without Pascal's God. Camus rejects two escapes. Physical suicide surrenders to the absurd. "Philosophical suicide," the leap of faith of Kierkegaard or the existentialist theologians, abandons reason to buy a meaning the world does not offer. The third option is **revolt**: to live with the absurd, lucidly, without appeal, and to draw from it freedom and passion. His image is Sisyphus, condemned to push a rock up a hill forever. Sisyphus knows his task is futile, and in that lucid knowledge he is superior to his fate. The book's last line has become one of the most quoted in modern philosophy:

> One must imagine Sisyphus happy.
>
> — Albert Camus, *The Myth of Sisyphus* (tr. Justin O'Brien, 1955)

In *The Rebel* (1951) Camus turned revolt into a political ethics of limits, against revolutionary movements that justify murder in the name of history. The full treatment of Camus, and the question of whether his answer to nihilism works, is in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 10](../learn-nihilism/10-camus-and-the-absurd.md); Sartre and Beauvoir appear there in [Chapter 11](../learn-nihilism/11-sartre-and-beauvoir.md).

## Legacies and critiques

Existentialism as a movement faded by the 1960s, replaced in France by structuralism, which attacked its central figure, the free individual subject (see [Chapter 23 — Continental Philosophy Today](23-continental-philosophy-today.md)). But phenomenology and existentialism left lasting marks.

- **In philosophy of mind and cognitive science**: Heidegger's and Merleau-Ponty's accounts of skilled coping and embodiment, Husserl's analysis of time-consciousness and the first-person perspective, and the idea of "neurophenomenology" (Varela) pairing first-person description with brain science.
- **In ethics**: authenticity as an ideal (Charles Taylor's *The Ethics of Authenticity*, 1991), Beauvoir's ethics of reciprocal freedom, and Emmanuel Levinas's ethics of the **face** of the other, which issues a demand prior to any theory.
- **In politics and social theory**: feminist, anti-racist and decolonial phenomenology; Hannah Arendt's analysis of action and plurality.
- **In medicine and psychiatry**: phenomenological psychopathology, which describes how depression or schizophrenia alter the structure of lived time, space and selfhood.

The standard criticisms are worth stating clearly:

1. **Method.** Can introspective description deliver necessary truths about experience? Daniel Dennett argues that first-person reports are theories, often wrong, and proposes "heterophenomenology," taking reports as data about what subjects believe about their experience, not as authoritative descriptions.
2. **Obscurity.** Heidegger's and Sartre's prose made them easy targets for Carnap and later analytic critics. Defenders reply that new ideas need new words, and that the core claims (the primacy of practice, the structure of bad faith) can be stated plainly, as this chapter has tried to do.
3. **Freedom.** Sartre's early theory of absolute freedom seems to ignore how much of us is shaped by bodies, habits, upbringing and social structure. Beauvoir, Merleau-Ponty and Fanon made this objection from within the movement, and the later Sartre largely accepted it.
4. **Ethics.** If there are no values except those we choose, can existentialism condemn anything? Sartre's claim that in choosing for myself I choose for all looks like Kant reappearing through the back door. Beauvoir's ethics of reciprocal freedom is the most developed answer.

## Living it

Phenomenology is a practice before it is a theory, and existentialism is an ethics. Four exercises:

1. **Bracketing.** Spend ten minutes describing a single ordinary experience (drinking coffee, walking to the door) without explaining it, naming causes, or judging it. Note the hidden sides of objects, the horizon, the way each moment holds the last. Then notice how much of your normal description was theory.
2. **Bad faith audit.** Write down three sentences you use about yourself that begin "I'm just the kind of person who…" For each, ask whether it states facticity (a real constraint) or an evasion of a choice you are still making. Then ask the reverse question: where do you pretend that your past or your situation does not constrain you at all?
3. **Being-toward-death.** Heidegger and the Stoics agree that remembering death clarifies life. Write a short answer to the question: if you had one year left, which of your current projects would you keep because they are yours, and which do you pursue only because "one does"?
4. **The look.** Notice one situation this week where you change what you do because you feel watched. Ask whether the watcher's view is a source of knowledge about yourself or a trap you could step out of. Then ask how you look at others, and whether your gaze fixes them.

## Summary

- Brentano made **intentionality**, the aboutness of mental states, the mark of the mental. James's stream of thought and Bergson's duration described experience as flowing, not atomic.
- Husserl's phenomenology describes experience "as given," using the **epoché** (bracketing the natural attitude) and eidetic variation. Objects appear in profiles against horizons; time-consciousness has retention and protention. His late work introduced the **lifeworld**.
- Heidegger replaced the detached subject with **Dasein** as **being-in-the-world**: things are first **ready-to-hand** in use, only later **present-at-hand**. Everyday life belongs to **the They**; **anxiety** and **being-toward-death** make **authenticity** possible. His Nazism raises unresolved questions about the philosophy.
- Pascal, Kierkegaard, Dostoevsky and Nietzsche anticipated existentialist themes: anxiety, passion, freedom against reason, the death of God.
- Sartre: **existence precedes essence**; we are condemned to be free and wholly responsible. **Bad faith** denies either our freedom (transcendence) or our facticity. **The look** of the other makes us objects.
- Beauvoir: freedom is **situated** and can be oppressed; to will oneself free is to will others' freedom. Woman is constructed as the **Other**; "one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." Fanon extended this to racism.
- Merleau-Ponty: the **lived body** and **motor intentionality** are the basis of perception; the root of today's embodied cognition.
- Camus: the **absurd** is the clash between our demand for meaning and the world's silence; the answer is lucid **revolt**, not suicide or a leap of faith.

### Dialogue 1 — On being a waiter

**Sartre:** You told me you are an accountant. What did you mean by that?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Sartre in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** That I work as an accountant. It's what I do for a living. It's also, honestly, who I am. I'm careful, I like numbers, I'm good at it.

**Sartre:** Are you an accountant the way this table is a table?

**You:** No. The table can't stop being a table. I could quit tomorrow.

**Sartre:** Then you are not an accountant in the mode of being one. You play at being an accountant, as the waiter plays at being a waiter. Each morning you choose it again, whether you notice or not.

**You:** That sounds exhausting. And false. I don't wake up and decide to be an accountant. I have a mortgage, a family, fifteen years of training. Those aren't choices I make each morning.

**Sartre:** They are facts, your facticity. But what they *mean* depends on you. The mortgage is a reason to stay only because you have chosen to value the house over, say, a life as a painter.

**Beauvoir:** Jean-Paul, you go too fast. A coal miner's son in 1930 who never learned that he could be anything else is not in bad faith when he goes down the mine. Situations can close off possibilities so completely that freedom has almost nothing to work with.

**Sartre:** He is still free to give his situation a meaning: to accept it, to resent it, to organize.

**Beauvoir:** That freedom is real but thin. Mystification and oppression can make it thinner. That is why freedom is a political question and not only a personal one.

**You:** So where does that leave me? I'm not a coal miner in 1930.

**Beauvoir:** No, you have more room than most. That is exactly why "I'm just an accountant" deserves scrutiny in your case.

**You:** Then I'll put it this way. Saying "I am an accountant" is fine as a description of what I'm doing. It's bad faith if I use it to avoid asking whether I should keep doing it, as if it were my nature. But it would also be bad faith to pretend that my obligations and training don't count. The honest version is: I'm choosing to remain an accountant, for reasons I can state, and I could choose otherwise at a cost I can also state.

**Sartre:** That is a sentence a free man could say.

### Dialogue 2 — On the cup

**Husserl:** Look at the cup on your desk. What do you see?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Husserl in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** A white coffee cup. It has a chip on the handle.

**Husserl:** Do you see the back of the cup?

**You:** Not right now, no. But it's there.

**Husserl:** How do you know?

**You:** Because cups have backs. If I turn it, I'll see it.

**Husserl:** So the back is given to you, not as seen, but as anticipated. Your perception contains more than your eyes receive. Now put aside, for a moment, the question whether the cup really exists. Do not deny it; just leave it aside. What remains?

**You:** The way it looks. The front side, the anticipation of the back, the desk around it, the room. And the fact that I've been seeing it for a few seconds, each moment connected to the one before.

**Heidegger:** But this is already artificial. Did you see the cup this morning when you were drinking your coffee and reading the news?

**You:** Not really. I just drank from it. I noticed it only when I chipped it on the sink.

**Heidegger:** Then its primary way of being is not as something seen at all. It is ready-to-hand, part of the morning. Only in breakdown does it become an object to stare at, as it is now in Husserl's seminar.

**Husserl:** Martin, you are describing an attitude, and description is my method. You merely chose a different example.

**Heidegger:** I chose the example that shows your starting point is a derivative mode of being.

**You:** So both of you are describing experience, but you disagree about which experience is basic. Husserl starts from careful perception, which is clear and easy to analyze. Heidegger starts from absorbed use, which is how I actually spend most of my day, but which disappears the moment I look at it. Maybe the lesson is that "looking at experience" already changes it, and a good phenomenologist has to account for that.

**Merleau-Ponty:** And notice that neither of you mentioned your hand, which knew exactly how heavy the cup was before you lifted it.

### Dialogue 3 — On whether Sisyphus can really be happy

**Camus:** You look troubled. What is it?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Camus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I've been reading your essay. You say we should imagine Sisyphus happy. But he's pushing a rock up a hill forever and it always rolls down. How is that anything but misery dressed up?

**Camus:** What makes it misery?

**You:** That it's pointless. Nothing comes of it.

**Camus:** And what comes of your work? Your commute, your reports, your weekends? Do they add up to something the universe will remember?

**You:** No. But my work does some good for some people.

**Camus:** Good. Then you have found meaning within a life, not a meaning *of* life given by the cosmos. That is all I claim anyone has. Sisyphus's hour of consciousness is the walk back down the hill. He knows exactly what his fate is. That lucidity is his victory.

**Pascal:** And yet the silence of those infinite spaces still frightens me. Lucidity about the void is cold comfort. The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know. I would rather wager on God.

**Camus:** That is the leap I call philosophical suicide. You trade your reason for a consolation.

**Pascal:** And you trade consolation for pride.

**Kierkegaard:** Both of you are spectators of the question. The question is what this person in front of you will do with their life tomorrow morning.

**You:** Here's what I'll take from this. Camus is right that the universe doesn't hand out meaning, and that pretending otherwise is a kind of dishonesty. But "happy" is still a strong word. Maybe what he means is that Sisyphus is not defeated: the meaning is in how he carries the rock, not in where it ends up. That I can believe. Whether it's enough is something I can only find out by living it, which I think Kierkegaard would say is the point.

**Camus:** That is close to what I wrote on my last page: the climb itself can be enough to fill a human heart.

</details>
