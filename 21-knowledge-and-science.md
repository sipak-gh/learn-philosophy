# Chapter 21 — Knowledge and Science

You know your own name, that two plus two is four, that the sun will rise tomorrow and that the Earth goes around the Sun. Or do you? You have never checked the last one yourself; you took it on trust. The third is a prediction about something that hasn't happened yet. And if you were dreaming right now, or plugged into a perfect simulation, everything you take yourself to see would be false. This chapter covers **epistemology**, the theory of knowledge, and its younger sibling, the **philosophy of science**. It asks what knowledge is and why the classic answer, "justified true belief", fails (Plato and Gettier); whether we know anything at all (the skeptics, Descartes, Moore); how experience can justify predictions (Hume's problem of induction); what makes science special (Bacon, Popper, Kuhn, Lakatos, Feyerabend); how to reason with uncertainty (Laplace and the Bayesians); and whether our best theories describe a reality beyond what we can observe (the realism debate). Previous: [Chapter 20 — Philosophy of Mind](20-philosophy-of-mind.md). Next: [Chapter 22 — Political Philosophy](22-political-philosophy.md).

## What is knowledge?

### Plato: true belief tied down

Start with a puzzle from the *Meno* (see [Chapter 5](05-plato.md)). Someone who has never traveled to Larisa, but has a correct belief about the road, will get you there as well as someone who knows the way. So why do we value knowledge more than mere true belief? Socrates' answer compares true beliefs to the statues of Daedalus, which were said to run away unless tied down:

> ...while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding.
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 98a (tr. Jowett)

Knowledge, on this picture, is true belief plus something that anchors it: a grasp of *why* it is true. In the *Theaetetus*, Plato's longest work on knowledge, Socrates and the young mathematician Theaetetus test three definitions. Knowledge is not perception (Protagoras' "man is the measure" makes everyone's perceptions true, including the perception that Protagoras is wrong). Nor is it true belief, as a courtroom shows:

> When, therefore, judges are justly persuaded about matters which you can know only by seeing them, and not in any other way, and when thus judging of them from report they attain a true opinion about them, they judge without knowledge, and yet are rightly persuaded, if they have judged well.
>
> — Plato, *Theaetetus* 201b–c (tr. Jowett)

A clever lawyer can persuade a jury of something true without the jury knowing it. So Theaetetus offers a third definition, that knowledge is "true opinion, combined with reason", or with an *account* (*logos*). The dialogue then tries three interpretations of "account" (putting the belief into words, listing the parts of the thing, stating what distinguishes it from everything else) and rejects all three. The last fails by circularity: to *know* the distinguishing mark, you would already need knowledge. The dialogue ends without an answer, which is typical of Plato and, as we will see, prophetic.

### Justified true belief

Twentieth-century philosophers standardly reconstructed Plato's third definition as the **tripartite** or **JTB analysis**: S knows that p if and only if

1. p is true,
2. S believes that p, and
3. S is justified in believing that p.

Each clause earns its place. You can't know what is false (you can only think you know). You can't know what you don't believe. And, as the jury shows, a lucky true belief isn't knowledge. The justification clause is meant to rule out luck.

It doesn't. Russell saw the difficulty in 1912, though he didn't make much of it:

> If a man believes that the late Prime Minister's last name began with a B, he believes what is true, since the late Prime Minister was Sir Henry Campbell Bannerman. But if he believes that Mr. Balfour was the late Prime Minister, he will still believe that the late Prime Minister's last name began with a B, yet this belief, though true, would not be thought to constitute knowledge.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Chapter XIII

Russell concluded only that a true belief "deduced from a false belief" is not knowledge. Much earlier, around 770 CE, the Buddhist philosopher Dharmottara had described a traveler who sees a shimmering mirage, concludes that there is water ahead, and arrives to find water hidden under a rock. The belief was true and was formed in the ordinary way, but its truth was an accident. (On the Indian theories of *pramāṇa*, the sources of knowledge, see [Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md).)

### Gettier's three pages

In 1963 Edmund Gettier, a young philosopher at Wayne State University who (as colleagues later told the story) was under pressure to publish, wrote one of the shortest famous papers in philosophy. His two cases have the same structure as Russell's and Dharmottara's, but they were aimed precisely at the JTB analysis and made the point unmistakable. Paraphrased:

- **Ten coins.** Smith and Jones have applied for the same job. Smith has strong evidence that Jones will get it (the company president told him so) and that Jones has ten coins in his pocket (he counted them). He infers: *the man who will get the job has ten coins in his pocket.* In fact, Smith gets the job, and, unknown to him, Smith also has ten coins in his pocket. His belief is true and justified. Does he know it? Surely not.
- **Barcelona.** Smith has good evidence that Jones owns a Ford. Picking a place at random, he infers by logic: *either Jones owns a Ford, or Brown is in Barcelona.* Jones doesn't own a Ford, but by coincidence Brown is in Barcelona. Justified, true, believed, not known.

Since then philosophers have produced Gettier cases by the hundred. A modern favorite, adapted from Russell's later work: you glance at a clock in the hall, which reads ten past two, and so you believe it is ten past two. It is. But the clock stopped exactly twelve hours ago. Another, from Alvin Goldman (credited to Carl Ginet): Henry drives through a county where the locals have put up dozens of papier-mâché barn façades. He looks at the one real barn and thinks, "That's a barn." He's right, and his eyes are working fine, but he would have believed the same thing of any of the fakes. Does he know?

The recipe, as Linda Zagzebski pointed out in 1994, is simple. Take any justification that falls short of guaranteeing truth. Construct a case where it would have led to a false belief, then add a second stroke of luck that makes the belief true after all. As long as justification is fallible, there will be Gettier cases.

### After Gettier: what turns true belief into knowledge?

The responses form a map of contemporary epistemology.

- **No false lemmas.** Knowledge must not depend on any false belief (Russell's fix). But Henry in barn county infers nothing false, and he still seems not to know.
- **Defeasibility.** There must be no truth which, if you learned it, would undermine your justification. In barn county, "most barns around here are fake" is such a defeater. The trouble is that misleading defeaters are everywhere, and this condition threatens to rule out too much.
- **Causal and reliabilist theories.** Alvin Goldman proposed first that the fact must cause the belief in the right way, then (1979) that knowledge is true belief produced by a **reliable process**: one that tends to produce true beliefs. Reliabilism is **externalist**: whether you are justified can depend on facts you have no access to, such as how reliable your eyesight is. It handles the clock (consulting a stopped clock is not reliable), but faces the **generality problem**: any belief is produced by a process that can be described more or less broadly ("vision", "vision in barn county", "vision at noon on a Tuesday"), with different reliability.
- **Sensitivity.** Robert Nozick (1981) required that you **track the truth**: if p were false, you wouldn't believe it. Henry fails, since if he were looking at a fake he would still believe it was a barn.
- **Safety.** Ernest Sosa and Timothy Williamson required instead that your belief could not *easily* have been false: in nearby possible situations where you believe it, it is true.
- **Virtue epistemology.** Sosa compares belief to an archer's shot. A shot can be accurate (it hits), adroit (it shows skill) and **apt** (it hits *because of* the skill). A gust of wind that blows a badly aimed arrow onto the target produces accuracy without aptness. Knowledge is apt belief: true *because* of the believer's competence. Zagzebski developed a related view inspired by Aristotle's virtue ethics (see [the Ethics course, Chapter 13](../learn-ethics/13-the-virtue-ethics-revival.md)).
- **Knowledge first.** Williamson's *Knowledge and Its Limits* (2000) turns the project around. After decades of failure, perhaps knowledge can't be analyzed into belief plus extras because it is the more basic notion. Belief is, roughly, what aims at knowledge, and your evidence is simply what you know (his slogan is "E = K").

The lesson most philosophers drew is the one from [Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md): analyses of rich everyday concepts into necessary and sufficient conditions almost never survive. Experimental philosophers have asked whether "Gettier intuitions" are a quirk of Anglophone seminar rooms; large studies have found them remarkably stable from Brazil to Japan to India.

### The structure of justification

A different question concerns how justification is organized. Ask why you believe something, then why you believe your reason, and so on. Ancient skeptics, following Agrippa, said the chain can only do one of three things, a dilemma now called **Agrippa's trilemma** or Münchhausen's trilemma:

1. go on forever (**infinite regress**);
2. loop back on itself (**circularity**); or
3. stop at some unsupported starting point (**dogmatism**).

Each option has become a theory. **Foundationalism** says some beliefs are **basic**, justified without inference by experience or rational insight; Descartes wanted certain foundations ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)), modern foundationalists settle for fallible ones. **Coherentism** says beliefs are justified by fitting into a mutually supporting web, like Quine's ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)); the worry is that a perfectly coherent fairy tale would be justified. **Infinitism** (Peter Klein) accepts the regress: justification is always being able to give a further reason.

Alongside runs the debate between **internalists**, for whom justification depends only on what the believer can access by reflection, and **externalists** like the reliabilists. The test case is the **new evil demon problem**: your exact mental duplicate, deceived by a demon, has thoroughly unreliable beliefs. Are they less *justified* than yours? Internalists say no.

## Skepticism

### The ancient skeptics

Skepticism in the Western tradition began as a way of life ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)). **Pyrrho** of Elis (c. 360–270 BCE), who traveled to India with Alexander's army, is said to have taught that things are indeterminable and that suspending judgment brings **ataraxia**, tranquility. The later Pyrrhonist **Sextus Empiricus** (c. 200 CE) collected *modes*, standard techniques for producing equally strong arguments on both sides of any question, so that the inquirer suspends judgment and, to her surprise, finds peace. The Pyrrhonist does not claim to *know* that nothing can be known. That would be dogmatism. She simply goes on finding that she can't decide.

Montaigne revived Pyrrhonism in the sixteenth century ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)) and adopted as his motto the question "What do I know?" He liked the skeptics' own image for how their arguments undercut even themselves:

> ...when they pronounce, "I know not," or, "I doubt," they say that this proposition carries off itself with the rest, no more nor less than rhubarb, that drives out the ill humours, and carries itself off with them.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, "Apology for Raimond Sebond", *Essays* II.12 (tr. Cotton, rev. Hazlitt)

### Cartesian skepticism and the brain in a vat

Descartes' version is different in aim and effect ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). He doubts in order to find something certain, and his doubts are global: perhaps you are dreaming; perhaps an evil demon deceives you about everything. The modern version is the **brain in a vat**: a mad scientist has removed your brain and feeds it electrical stimulation indistinguishable from normal experience. (*The Matrix* is this thought experiment with a budget.)

The modern argument can be laid out with logical precision:

1. You don't know that you are not a brain in a vat.
2. If you know you have hands, then you know you are not a (handless) brain in a vat.
3. So you don't know you have hands.

Premise 2 rests on a principle called **closure**: if you know p, and you know that p entails q, then you know q (or are in a position to). The argument is valid, the premises are plausible, and the conclusion is absurd. Something must go, and the main responses differ over what.

**Moore: deny premise 1.** G. E. Moore ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)) famously held up his hands before a British Academy audience in 1939 and said, in effect, "Here is one hand, and here is another," proving that external things exist. The lecture is under copyright, but his earlier essay on Hume, now in the public domain, makes the same move with complete candor:

> It is therefore quite impossible for any one to *prove*, in one strict sense of the term, that he does know any external facts. I can only prove that I do, by assuming that in some particular instance, I actually do know one. That is to say, the so-called proof must assume the very thing which it pretends to prove. The only proof that we do know external facts lies in the simple fact that we do know them.
>
> — G. E. Moore, "Hume's Philosophy", in *Philosophical Studies*

Moore's point is about comparative certainty. You are more certain that you have hands than you could ever be of the premises of any philosophical argument to the contrary. So run the argument backwards: you know you have hands, so (by closure) you know you aren't a brain in a vat. One philosopher's modus ponens is another's modus tollens. Many find this unsatisfying (it seems to beg the question against the skeptic); Moore's admirers reply that the skeptic begs the question too.

**Hume: nature is too strong.** Hume, the most skeptical of the great philosophers, also thought radical doubt was psychologically impossible to sustain:

> Nature is always too strong for principle. And though a Pyrrhonian may throw himself or others into a momentary amazement and confusion by his profound reasonings; the first and most trivial event in life will put to flight all his doubts and scruples, and leave him the same, in every point of action and speculation, with the philosophers of every other sect, or with those who never concerned themselves in any philosophical researches.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section XII, Part II

This isn't a refutation, just a description of what happens when you close the book and go to dinner. Hume recommended a "mitigated" skepticism, modest about grand speculation and confident in ordinary life.

**Deny closure.** Fred Dretske and Nozick argued that closure fails. On Nozick's sensitivity condition, you know you have hands (if you didn't, you'd notice), but you don't know you're not a brain in a vat (if you were, you'd still believe you weren't). So you can know the first without knowing the second. The cost is accepting what Keith DeRose called an "abominable conjunction": "I know I have hands, but I don't know I'm not a handless brain in a vat."

**Contextualism.** DeRose, Stewart Cohen and David Lewis argued that "know" shifts its standards with context, like "flat" or "tall". In the kitchen, "I know I have hands" is true by everyday standards. In the seminar room, once the skeptic raises the vat, the standards rise and the same sentence comes out false. Both the skeptic and Moore are right, by different standards. Lewis called knowledge "elusive": it vanishes when you start doing epistemology. Critics object that ordinary speakers don't treat skeptical challenges as changing the subject.

**Semantic externalism.** Hilary Putnam argued (1981) that a brain in a vat's word "vat" would refer to vat-images, not real vats (recall Twin Earth from [Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)), so "I am a brain in a vat" is false even in the vat. Whether this helps is disputed.

## The problem of induction

### Hume's argument

Skepticism about the external world is a philosopher's worry. Hume's problem of induction is more unsettling, because it concerns the reasoning that science and everyday life depend on. We have seen the sun rise every day; we expect it tomorrow. We have seen bread nourish; we expect the next loaf to do the same. But what justifies the step from observed cases to unobserved ones?

> For all inferences from experience suppose, as their foundation, that the future will resemble the past, and that similar powers will be conjoined with similar sensible qualities. If there be any suspicion that the course of nature may change, and that the past may be no rule for the future, all experience becomes useless, and can give rise to no inference or conclusion. It is impossible, therefore, that any arguments from experience can prove this resemblance of the past to the future; since all these arguments are founded on the supposition of that resemblance.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Section IV, Part II

The argument is a dilemma. The principle that nature is uniform could be justified only by **demonstrative** reasoning (logic and mathematics) or by **probable** reasoning (experience). Not by demonstration, because its denial is perfectly conceivable: a world where bread suddenly poisons involves no contradiction. Not by experience, because every argument from experience already assumes the principle. "It has always worked before" is an inductive argument for induction, which is circular.

Hume's positive conclusion is psychological. We do reason inductively, and can't help it, but the source is habit, not reason. "Custom, then, is the great guide of human life."

### Russell's chicken

Russell put the point with a cruelty that every student remembers:

> Domestic animals expect food when they see the person who usually feeds them. We know that all these rather crude expectations of uniformity are liable to be misleading. The man who has fed the chicken every day throughout its life at last wrings its neck instead, showing that more refined views as to the uniformity of nature would have been useful to the chicken.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Chapter VI

What would "more refined views" be? Russell's own answer was that the inductive principle itself, that the more often A has been found with B the more probable it is that they will be found together next time, can neither be proved nor disproved by experience. We must accept it on its "intrinsic evidence" or abandon all justification for our expectations.

### Responses

- **The inductive justification of induction.** Max Black argued that the circularity is not vicious. Critics note that counter-induction ("it has rarely happened, so it probably will next") can be justified in the same circular way.
- **Pragmatic vindication.** Hans Reichenbach argued that if *any* method of prediction works, induction will. If nature is uniform, induction succeeds; if it isn't, nothing does. So induction is the best bet, even if it can't be proved reliable.
- **Dissolution.** P. F. Strawson argued that asking whether induction is reasonable is like asking whether the law is legal. Being reasonable *means*, in part, proportioning belief to inductive evidence. This is a Wittgensteinian move ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)), and many find it too quick.
- **Bayesianism**, discussed below, recasts induction as updating probabilities, though it needs prior probabilities that must come from somewhere.
- **Popper**, also below, gave up on induction altogether.

### Goodman's new riddle

In 1955 Nelson Goodman showed that the problem is even worse than Hume thought. Define a predicate **grue**: an object is grue if it is examined before the year 2100 and is green, or is not examined before 2100 and is blue. Every emerald examined so far is green. So every emerald examined so far is *also* grue. Our evidence supports "all emeralds are green" and "all emeralds are grue" equally well, yet they predict different colors for emeralds first examined in 2100.

Obviously we should project "green" and not "grue". But why? It is tempting to say that "grue" is gerrymandered, defined in terms of a time. But from the point of view of someone who speaks a grue language, "green" is the strange one: green means grue if examined before 2100 and *bleen* otherwise. Goodman's own answer was that "green" is **entrenched**, having been projected successfully many times before. That explains our practice but seems not to justify it. The new riddle shows that induction can't be a purely formal rule. Which generalizations are confirmed by their instances depends on which properties are natural kinds, and nothing in the logic of confirmation tells us what those are.

## What makes science special?

### Bacon and the inductive method

Francis Bacon's *Novum Organum* (1620), the "new instrument" meant to replace Aristotle's logic, proposed that science proceed by careful collection of instances, tables of presence and absence, and gradual ascent to general laws ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)). Bacon's most lasting contribution was his catalog of the mind's distortions, the **idols**. Here is the idol of the tribe, which psychologists now call confirmation bias:

> The human understanding, when any proposition has been once laid down (either from general admission and belief, or from the pleasure it affords), forces everything else to add fresh support and confirmation; and although most cogent and abundant instances may exist to the contrary, yet either does not observe or despises them, or gets rid of and rejects them by some distinction, with violent and injurious prejudice, rather than sacrifice the authority of its first conclusions.
>
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum*, Book I, Aphorism XLVI

Bacon went on to tell of the man shown the votive tablets of sailors who had prayed and survived a shipwreck, who asked where the portraits were of those who had prayed and drowned. That is still the best one-sentence lesson in statistics.

Bacon's picture of the scientist as a patient collector is historically false: without a hypothesis, nobody would know which facts to gather. Poincaré put it memorably:

> Science is built up with facts, as a house is with stones. But a collection of facts is no more a science than a heap of stones is a house.
>
> — Henri Poincaré, *Science and Hypothesis*, Chapter IX, in *The Foundations of Science* (tr. Halsted)

### Popper: conjectures and refutations

Karl Popper (1902–1994) grew up in the intellectual ferment of Vienna after the First World War. As he told the story decades later, he was struck in 1919 by a contrast between two kinds of theory. Marxism, Freud's psychoanalysis and Alfred Adler's individual psychology seemed to explain everything: every event confirmed them. When Popper described a case to Adler that didn't seem to fit, Adler analyzed it without hesitation in terms of his theory of inferiority feelings. Einstein's general relativity, by contrast, made a risky prediction: starlight passing the Sun would bend by a definite amount. Eddington's eclipse expedition of 1919 could have refuted it. It didn't, but it could have.

Popper drew two conclusions, set out in *The Logic of Scientific Discovery* (1934; English 1959) and *Conjectures and Refutations* (1963), whose wording we paraphrase:

1. **The demarcation criterion.** A theory is scientific if and only if it is **falsifiable**: it forbids certain observable outcomes, so that experience could in principle refute it. A theory that is compatible with every possible observation is not thereby strong; it is empty.
2. **The method.** Science doesn't proceed by induction at all. It proceeds by bold **conjectures** and attempted **refutations**. No number of white swans proves "all swans are white", but one black swan refutes it. This asymmetry, a simple point of logic ([Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md)), lets science make progress without induction: we never verify theories, we only eliminate false ones. Theories that survive severe tests are **corroborated**, but corroboration is a report on past performance, not a guarantee.

Working scientists found the picture heroic, and it gave a clear test for pseudoscience such as astrology, whose predictions are vague enough to survive any outcome.

### Troubles for falsificationism

But real science doesn't work quite like that, for three reasons.

**Holism.** As Pierre Duhem showed in 1906, and Quine generalized ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)), hypotheses are never tested alone. A prediction follows from a theory only together with auxiliary assumptions about instruments, initial conditions and other bodies. When a prediction fails, logic tells you that *something* in the package is false, not which part. Consider two episodes from the history of astronomy. In the 1840s, the orbit of Uranus deviated from Newton's predictions. Instead of rejecting Newton, Urbain Le Verrier hypothesized an unseen planet, calculated where it must be, and in 1846 Neptune was found within a degree of the predicted spot. Le Verrier then tried the same move with the anomalous motion of Mercury, postulating a planet "Vulcan" inside Mercury's orbit. Vulcan does not exist. The anomaly was eventually explained by Einstein's general relativity, which is to say that Newton's theory was false all along. At the time, the two cases looked the same. Falsificationism, by itself, gives no rule for when to blame the theory and when to blame an auxiliary.

**Probabilistic theories.** "This coin is fair" forbids no particular sequence of tosses; a hundred heads in a row is merely improbable. Popper had to *decide* to treat very improbable outcomes as refutations.

**The return of induction.** If corroboration tells us nothing about future performance, why should an engineer prefer a well-tested theory when building a bridge? If it does, then it is induction under another name.

### Kuhn: paradigms and revolutions

Thomas Kuhn (1922–1996), a physicist turned historian, published *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* in 1962. It is one of the most cited academic books of the twentieth century and gave the world the phrase "paradigm shift". Kuhn argued that the history of science doesn't look like Popper's endless cycle of conjecture and refutation. It has phases:

- **Normal science.** Most of the time a mature science works within a **paradigm**: a shared framework of theory, exemplary problem solutions, instruments and standards (Ptolemaic astronomy, Newtonian mechanics, the oxygen theory of combustion). Normal science is **puzzle-solving**. Scientists do not try to refute the paradigm. When a result doesn't fit, the scientist, not the paradigm, is usually thought to have failed.
- **Anomaly and crisis.** Anomalies accumulate. Most are shelved; some resist. Eventually confidence erodes and the field enters a **crisis**, with proliferating rival versions of the theory.
- **Revolution.** A new paradigm emerges and wins converts, usually among younger scientists. Kuhn compared the change to a **Gestalt switch**, like suddenly seeing the duck-rabbit figure as a rabbit. After a revolution scientists, in a sense, work in a different world.

The most controversial claim was **incommensurability**. Rival paradigms, Kuhn argued, can't be compared point by point on a neutral scale. They differ over which problems matter, what counts as a solution, and even the meaning of key terms ("mass" in Newton and in Einstein). Observation is **theory-laden**: what you see depends partly on what you already believe. So the choice between paradigms is not settled by logic and experiment alone. Kuhn compared it to a political revolution or a religious conversion.

Critics charged Kuhn with making science a matter of mob psychology. He protested that he had been misread: five shared values guide theory choice (accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity and fruitfulness), but they can conflict and be weighed differently. Theory choice is rational, but it is judgment, not algorithm.

### Lakatos and Feyerabend

Imre Lakatos (1922–1974), Popper's colleague at the London School of Economics, tried to reconcile the two. The unit of appraisal is not a single theory but a **research programme**: a **hard core** of central commitments (Newton's laws) protected by a **protective belt** of auxiliary hypotheses (assumptions about planets, instruments, friction) that absorb refutations. A programme is **progressive** if its changes to the belt predict novel facts that are then confirmed (Neptune) and **degenerating** if it only patches anomalies after the fact (Vulcan, or the endless epicycles of a failing theory). It is rational to abandon a programme that degenerates while a rival progresses, but Lakatos admitted there is no fixed moment when it becomes *irrational* to persist. Comebacks happen: the atomic theory, the wave theory of light and continental drift were all at one time on the losing side.

Paul Feyerabend (1924–1994), Lakatos's friend and sparring partner, drew the radical conclusion. In *Against Method* (1975) he argued, mainly from Galileo's use of rhetoric and ad hoc hypotheses to win acceptance for Copernicanism, that every proposed rule of method has been broken by some great scientist, to science's benefit. The only universal principle left, he concluded, is "anything goes". This "epistemological anarchism" was partly a provocation (he later said the slogan was what a rationalist would be left with after studying history). His serious point was that methods are rules of thumb, and that a free society should grant science no more authority than its specific achievements earn.

Most philosophers now doubt that any single sharp criterion separates science from non-science. Larry Laudan declared the **demarcation problem** dead in 1983; others, such as Massimo Pigliucci, treat "science" as a family-resemblance concept: fields are more or less scientific along several dimensions (testability, coherence, openness to criticism, peer review and replication). Homeopathy and creationism fail on many at once.

## Probability and Bayesian reasoning

### Laplace: common sense reduced to calculus

Science rarely delivers certainty, so a theory of knowledge must also be a theory of degrees of belief. The mathematical theory of probability grew up in the seventeenth century around gambling problems (Pascal and Fermat, 1654; Pascal's wager is in [Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)). Pierre-Simon Laplace's *Philosophical Essay on Probabilities* (1814) presented it as the logic of uncertain reasoning:

> It is seen in this essay that the theory of probabilities is at bottom only common sense reduced to calculus; it makes us appreciate with exactitude that which exact minds feel by a sort of instinct without being able ofttimes to give a reason for it.
>
> — Pierre-Simon Laplace, *A Philosophical Essay on Probabilities*, Conclusion (tr. Truscott and Emory)

For Laplace, probability measures not chance in the world (he was a strict determinist) but our ignorance, "relative, in part to this ignorance, in part to our knowledge". He also applied the calculus to testimony and drew a conclusion already reached informally by Hume's essay on miracles ("A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence"): "the probability of the error or of the falsehood of the witness becomes as much greater as the fact attested is more extraordinary." Carl Sagan's slogan that extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence descends from this line of thought.

### Bayes' theorem

The engine of the modern theory is a theorem published posthumously in 1763 by the Presbyterian minister Thomas Bayes, and developed independently by Laplace. It tells you how to update the probability of a hypothesis H in light of evidence E:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) × P(H) / P(E)

In words: how likely the hypothesis is after the evidence (the **posterior**) depends on how likely it was before (the **prior**) and on how much more likely the evidence is if the hypothesis is true than otherwise (the **likelihood**).

A worked example shows why it matters. A disease affects 1 percent of people. A test for it catches 90 percent of those who have it, but also gives a positive result for 9 percent of those who don't. You test positive. How likely is it that you have the disease? Most people, including, in study after study, most doctors, answer around 90 percent. Take 1,000 people: 10 have the disease, of whom 9 test positive; 990 don't, of whom about 89 test positive. So of the 98 positive results, only 9 are true. The answer is about 9 percent. Neglecting the prior, the **base rate**, is one of the most common and consequential errors in human reasoning.

### Bayesian epistemology

**Bayesianism** in philosophy is the view that rational belief comes in degrees (**credences**), that rational credences obey the probability calculus, and that learning proceeds by **conditionalization**: when you learn E, your new credence in H should be your old credence in H given E. Why obey the calculus? Frank Ramsey (1926) and Bruno de Finetti gave the **Dutch book argument**: if your credences violate the axioms, a bookie can offer you a set of bets, each of which you consider fair, that together guarantee you lose money whatever happens.

Bayesianism illuminates much that the older theories struggled with. It explains why surprising predictions are especially confirming (P(E) is low, so the boost is large: Neptune again). It explains why one failed prediction need not sink a theory with a high prior (blame the auxiliary; Duhem's problem gets a quantitative treatment). It gives a reply to Hempel's **paradox of the ravens**, which notes that "all ravens are black" is logically equivalent to "all non-black things are non-ravens", so that a white shoe seems to confirm that ravens are black. The Bayesian answers that it does, but only by a negligible amount, because there are so many non-black things.

Its difficulties are equally instructive:

- **The problem of the priors.** Where do the starting probabilities come from? **Subjective Bayesians** say any coherent priors are permitted, and point to convergence theorems showing that people with different priors come to agree as evidence accumulates. But convergence can take a long time, and a dogmatist with a prior of zero can never be moved. **Objective Bayesians** look for principles that constrain priors, such as the **principle of indifference** (equal probability to each possibility when you have no reason to favor one), which yields paradoxes when the possibilities can be divided up in different ways.
- **The problem of old evidence.** The anomaly in Mercury's orbit was known for decades before Einstein. Since it was already certain, P(E) = 1, and conditionalizing on it changes nothing. Yet it was among the strongest confirmations of general relativity (Clark Glymour, 1980).

Bayesianism has nonetheless become the default framework in formal epistemology, statistics, cognitive science (the "Bayesian brain") and machine learning. It makes Hume's problem look different without solving it: Bayesian updating tells you how to learn from experience *given* priors, and the priors encode the very assumptions about the uniformity of nature that Hume found unjustifiable.

## Realism and anti-realism

Our best physics describes electrons, quarks and fields that no one has ever seen. Should we believe that they exist, or only that the theories that mention them are useful for predicting what we *can* see?

**Scientific realism** says that mature, successful theories are approximately true, including what they say about unobservables. Its main argument is the **no-miracles argument**, given its classic form by Putnam in 1975: if electrons didn't exist, the success of theories that postulate them, from cathode ray tubes to smartphones, would be an inexplicable coincidence. Realism is the only view on which science's success is not a miracle. This is an **inference to the best explanation**, the form of reasoning that Peirce called abduction ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md)).

**The pessimistic meta-induction.** Larry Laudan replied in 1981 with a list of theories that were empirically successful in their day and are now regarded as false, including about their central entities: the crystalline spheres, the humoral theory of medicine, phlogiston, caloric (heat as a fluid), the luminiferous ether. If past successful theories were false, induction over the history of science suggests that ours probably are too.

**Structural realism.** John Worrall (1989) proposed a compromise, and found it in Poincaré. When theories change, what survives is not their picture of what things are but the mathematical structure of the relations between them. Poincaré had made exactly this point about Augustin-Jean Fresnel's theory of light as vibrations in an ether, replaced by Maxwell's electromagnetic theory:

> ...these equations express relations, and if the equations remain true it is because these relations preserve their reality. They teach us, now as then, that there is such and such a relation between some thing and some other thing; only this something formerly we called *motion*; we now call it *electric current*. But these appellations were only images substituted for the real objects which nature will eternally hide from us. The true relations between these real objects are the only reality we can attain to...
>
> — Henri Poincaré, *Science and Hypothesis*, Chapter X, in *The Foundations of Science* (tr. Halsted)

On this view, we should be realists about structure and agnostics about the nature of what stands in the structure. Poincaré was also famous for **conventionalism**: some apparently factual principles of science are really conventions, chosen for convenience. His example was geometry: "One geometry can not be more true than another; it can only be more convenient." (General relativity, which made the geometry of spacetime an empirical question, later complicated the example.)

**Constructive empiricism.** Bas van Fraassen's *The Scientific Image* (1980) holds that science aims not at truth but at **empirical adequacy**, getting the observable phenomena right; you can use talk of electrons without believing in them. Realists ask where the line falls: is what you see through a microscope observed? Through a window?

Behind the debate lies **underdetermination**: rival theories could fit any body of evidence equally well. **Entity realists** (Ian Hacking, Nancy Cartwright) believe in what we can manipulate in the lab, such as electrons sprayed at targets, while doubting the theories that describe them. Pragmatists ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md)) suspect the whole question is malformed.

## Knowledge as social

Traditional epistemology imagined a solitary knower, but almost everything you know, you know because someone told you. **Testimony** is a source of knowledge in its own right. Hume held that it is justified only by our experience of the reliability of witnesses; his critic Thomas Reid held that we have a default entitlement to believe what we are told, noting that children trust long before they have any track record to go on. Four newer debates build on this.

- **Epistemic injustice.** Miranda Fricker (2007) describes **testimonial injustice**, when prejudice deflates a speaker's credibility, and **hermeneutical injustice**, when a group lacks the shared concepts to make sense of its experience (women who suffered sexual harassment before the term was coined in the 1970s). Feminist **standpoint theorists** argue that marginalized positions can confer epistemic advantages ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)).
- **Peer disagreement.** If someone as able and informed as you reads the same evidence differently, **conciliationists** say you should lower your confidence; **steadfast** views say you may trust reasoning that is in fact correct.
- **Values in science.** Since evidence never makes a hypothesis certain, Richard Rudner argued (1953), deciding how much evidence is enough depends on the cost of being wrong: the argument from **inductive risk**. Helen Longino locates objectivity in communities whose diverse members criticize one another.
- **Trust in experts.** Laypeople rarely can check scientific claims and must judge whom to trust by track records, consensus and institutional checks. The **replication crisis** in psychology and medicine since 2011 has made this urgent.

## Living it

1. **The Gettier diary.** For a week, notice moments when you were right by luck: the guess that came true, the bus you caught because it was late. Write each as a Gettier case. Which response (no false lemmas, reliability, safety, aptness) best explains why you didn't know?
2. **Bacon's tablets.** Pick a belief you hold about what works (a diet, a study habit, a medicine, a sports ritual). List the evidence for it. Then ask where the "portraits of those who drowned" are: the cases that would count against it, which you might never have heard about.
3. **Run the numbers.** Next time you read a headline about a test, a screening or a risk factor, find the base rate and do the 1,000-person calculation above. It takes two minutes and is one of the most useful habits philosophy can teach.
4. **Falsify yourself.** Write down a belief you care about (political, personal or scientific) and then state exactly what observation would make you give it up. If you can't name one, ask whether you hold it as a hypothesis or as something else, and whether that is all right.

## Summary

- **Knowledge** was classically analyzed as **justified true belief**, which goes back to Plato's "true opinion, combined with reason" in the *Theaetetus* and to the tethered statues of the *Meno*.
- **Gettier cases** (anticipated by Dharmottara and Russell) show that justified true belief can be lucky. Responses include no false lemmas, defeasibility, **reliabilism**, **sensitivity**, **safety**, **virtue epistemology** (apt belief) and Williamson's **knowledge first**.
- **Agrippa's trilemma** shapes the debate between **foundationalism**, **coherentism** and **infinitism**; **internalists** and **externalists** disagree over whether justification must be accessible.
- **Skepticism**: Pyrrhonism as a way to tranquility; Descartes' demon and the **brain in a vat**; the closure argument. Responses: **Moore**, Hume's naturalism, denying closure, **contextualism**, Putnam's semantic externalism.
- **Hume's problem of induction**: inductive reasoning can't be justified without circularity. Russell's chicken; responses by Black, Reichenbach and Strawson; Goodman's **grue** shows that confirmation depends on which predicates are natural.
- **Philosophy of science**: Bacon's idols and inductivism; **Popper's falsificationism** and demarcation; problems from **Duhem's holism** (Neptune and Vulcan); **Kuhn's paradigms**, normal science, revolutions and incommensurability; **Lakatos's research programmes**; **Feyerabend's** "anything goes".
- **Bayesianism**: Laplace's "common sense reduced to calculus"; Bayes' theorem, base rates, conditionalization, Dutch books; problems of priors and old evidence.
- **Realism**: the no-miracles argument versus the **pessimistic meta-induction**; **structural realism** (Poincaré, Worrall), **constructive empiricism** (van Fraassen), underdetermination and entity realism.
- **Social epistemology**: testimony (Hume versus Reid), **epistemic injustice** (Fricker), peer disagreement, values and inductive risk, trust in experts.

**You:** No. I was right by luck. If I'd looked five minutes later, I'd have believed something false in exactly the same way.

So I'll say knowledge is true belief that's true *because* of the believer's competence, Sosa's aptness. ### Dialogue 2 — On whether astrology is science

**Popper:** Your horoscope says that this week brings challenges at work and an unexpected connection. Is astrology a science?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Popper in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** No. That prediction can't fail. Every week brings some challenge and some connection. There's no outcome that would count against it.

**Popper:** Good. It is unfalsifiable, and so it is not science, however many believers it confirms.

**Kuhn:** I agree it isn't science, but for a different reason. Historically, astrologers did make predictions that failed, and they had excuses: the birth time was uncertain, the influences too complex. Astronomers had excuses too. The difference is that astrology never had puzzles to solve. Failures didn't generate research; they just got explained away.

**You:** But isn't that what Newtonians did with Uranus? They didn't reject the theory, they explained the anomaly away with another planet.

**Lakatos:** Yes, and they were right to, because the excuse predicted something new, and Neptune was found. When they tried the same move with Mercury, it predicted Vulcan, which wasn't found. The first was progressive, the second degenerating. Astrology's excuses never predict anything new.

**Popper:** Which is my point, put more elaborately.

**Lakatos:** Your point, with a sense of history. A programme can be falsified a hundred times and still be worth pursuing, if its patches keep paying off.

**You:** So none of you uses a single test. Popper asks whether it can fail, Kuhn whether it solves puzzles, Lakatos whether its patches predict anything new.

**Feyerabend:** And I would add that some of what you call pseudoscience has turned out to be right. Traditional medicines led to real drugs. Be careful whom you exclude.

**You:** Here's my verdict. Astrology fails all three tests at once, so it isn't science. But Feyerabend's point stands: whether something is scientific is a question about how it handles failure, not about where it came from. If astrologers produced a bold, specific prediction that kept coming true, I'd have to take it seriously.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On a positive test

**Laplace:** You have just taken a test for a rare disease, and it came back positive. The test is 90 percent accurate. How worried should you be?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Laplace in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I'd want to know how rare the disease is. If it's very rare, most of the positive results will be false alarms, even with a good test.

**Laplace:** Exactly. Say 1 in 100 people have it, and the test wrongly says yes to 9 percent of healthy people. Out of a thousand people, how many positives?

**You:** Ten people have it, and nine test positive. Of the 990 who don't, about 89 test positive. So 98 positives, of which nine are real. About a 9 percent chance.

**Laplace:** Common sense reduced to calculus.

**Hume:** Calculus built on a foundation it cannot justify. You assume that this test will behave on you as it has on the people it was tested on, and that the disease's frequency next year will resemble last year's. All of that is the future resembling the past.

**You:** But you also say we can't help reasoning that way.

**Hume:** Nor should you try. I only ask you to notice that the calculation is no stronger than the custom beneath it.

**Laplace:** And yet, Monsieur Hume, which of the two people does better: the one who reasons as I did, or the one who panics at "90 percent accurate"?

**Hume:** The first, on every occasion I have observed. Which is, of course, an inductive argument.

**You:** Then I'll take both lessons. Laplace shows me how to reason given my assumptions, and the calculation changes what I should do: get a second test before I panic. Hume shows me that the assumptions themselves can't be proved. I can live with that. A chicken who did Bayes' theorem would still be surprised in the end, but it would be surprised less often along the way.

</details>
