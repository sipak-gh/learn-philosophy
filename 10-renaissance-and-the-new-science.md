# Chapter 10 — The Renaissance and the New Science

On April 26, 1336, the poet Francesco Petrarch climbed Mont Ventoux in Provence for no better reason than to see the view. At the top he opened his pocket copy of Augustine's *Confessions* and found a sentence rebuking people who admire mountains and neglect their own souls. He walked down in silence. The scene holds the whole period in miniature: a new curiosity about the world and the self, pulling against an old authority that still had the last word. Over the next three centuries the balance tipped. Humanists recovered the lost texts of antiquity and made the human being, not God's plan, the center of attention. Machiavelli described politics as it is practiced, not as it ought to be. Montaigne turned skepticism on himself and invented the essay. Bacon proposed a new method for wresting secrets from nature, Galileo pointed a telescope at the sky and saw mountains on the moon, and Hobbes rebuilt political philosophy from the ground up on the model of geometry and mechanics. By 1650 the medieval synthesis of [Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md) was broken, and philosophers had to find new foundations for knowledge, which is the project of the next three chapters.

## Humanism: back to the sources

The Renaissance (French for "rebirth"; Italians said *rinascita*) was first a movement of scholars. **Humanists** (*umanisti*) were teachers of the *studia humanitatis*: grammar, rhetoric, poetry, history and moral philosophy. They wanted to write Latin like Cicero rather than like the schoolmen, and their battle cry was *ad fontes*, "to the sources." Manuscript hunters revived Lucretius's atomism (found in 1417; see [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)), Marsilio Ficino translated the whole of Plato into Latin for the Medici, and in 1562 Henri Estienne published a Latin translation of Sextus Empiricus' *Outlines of Pyrrhonism*, and ancient skepticism, almost unknown for a thousand years, became available just when the Reformation had made the question "who decides what is true?" a matter of life and death. Richard Popkin's *The History of Scepticism* argues that this collision drove much of early modern philosophy.

Petrarch (1304–1374), often called the first humanist, shows what changed and what did not. Here is the end of his letter on Mont Ventoux (*Familiares* IV.1):

> Now it chanced that the tenth book presented itself. My brother, waiting to hear something of St. Augustine's from my lips, stood attentively by. I call him, and God too, to witness that where I first fixed my eyes it was written: "And men go about to wonder at the heights of the mountains, and the mighty waves of the sea, and the wide sweep of rivers, and the circuit of the ocean, and the revolution of the stars, but themselves they consider not."
>
> — Petrarch, *Familiares* IV.1, to Dionisio da Borgo San Sepolcro (tr. James Harvey Robinson)

The letter is staged (it imitates Augustine's own conversion scene), and the climb may never have happened as described. But notice the double movement: the curiosity is new, the turn inward old. Petrarch concludes "that nothing is wonderful but the soul."

**Pico della Mirandola** (1463–1494) gave the movement its manifesto. At twenty-three he proposed to defend 900 theses drawn from every tradition he knew in a public disputation in Rome, which the Pope banned. His opening speech, later titled the *Oration on the Dignity of Man*, has God tell Adam that every other creature has a fixed nature and place, while Adam alone has none: he can sink to the level of the beasts or rise toward the angels, as the shaper of his own being. The idea that human beings are defined by their freedom to define themselves will return, stripped of its theology, in Sartre ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)).

Humanism had a satirical edge. In *The Praise of Folly* (1511) by **Erasmus** (c. 1466–1536), Folly herself mocks theologians, monks and philosophers, and she has a point against the Stoics:

> For since according to the definition of the Stoics, wisdom is nothing else than to be governed by reason, and on the contrary Folly, to be given up to the will of our passions, that the life of man might not be altogether disconsolate and hard to away with, of how much more passion than reason has Jupiter composed us?
>
> — Erasmus, *The Praise of Folly* (tr. John Wilson)

His friend **Thomas More** (1478–1535) wrote *Utopia* (1516), coining the word (Greek *ou-topos*, "no place," punning on *eu-topos*, "good place"). Book I is a sharp critique of England, where landowners were enclosing common land to raise sheep for the wool trade:

> 'The increase of pasture,' said I, 'by which your sheep, which are naturally mild, and easily kept in order, may be said now to devour men and unpeople, not only villages, but towns'
>
> — Thomas More, *Utopia*, Book I (tr. Gilbert Burnet)

Book II describes an island without private property, where gold is used for chamber pots. Whether More endorsed this communism or meant it as a provocation is still argued.

## Machiavelli: the effectual truth

In 1513 the Medici returned to power in Florence, and Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527), for fourteen years a senior civil servant and diplomat of the republic, was arrested on suspicion of conspiracy and tortured with the *strappado* (hoisted by the wrists, tied behind the back, and dropped). Released in an amnesty, he retired to his farm at Sant'Andrea in Percussina. In a famous letter to his friend Francesco Vettori (December 10, 1513), he describes his days: trapping thrushes, quarreling at cards in the inn. In the evening he takes off his muddy clothes, puts on the robes of the court, and enters the company of the ancients to question them about their actions. From those conversations, he says, he has composed a little work on principalities. He hoped it would win him a job with the Medici. It did not.

Chapter XV announces the method:

> it appears to me more appropriate to follow up the real truth of the matter than the imagination of it; for many have pictured republics and principalities which in fact have never been known or seen, because how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. XV (tr. W. K. Marriott)

The Italian phrase Marriott renders "the real truth of the matter" is *la verità effettuale della cosa*, "the effectual truth of the thing," the truth about what actually produces effects. The target is the genre of "mirrors for princes," which told rulers that the virtuous course is always also the advantageous one. Machiavelli denies this. The conclusion follows at once:

> Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity.
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. XV (tr. W. K. Marriott)

Chapter XVII asks whether it is better to be loved or feared. Ideally both, but:

> because it is difficult to unite them in one person, it is much safer to be feared than loved, when, of the two, either must be dispensed with. Because this is to be asserted in general of men, that they are ungrateful, fickle, false, cowardly, covetous, and as long as you succeed they are yours entirely
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. XVII (tr. W. K. Marriott)

Fear depends on you; love depends on them. But the prince must avoid being *hated*, which he will be if he touches his subjects' property and women. Chapter XVIII, on keeping faith, gives the most famous image:

> A prince, therefore, being compelled knowingly to adopt the beast, ought to choose the fox and the lion; because the lion cannot defend himself against snares and the fox cannot defend himself against wolves. Therefore, it is necessary to be a fox to discover the snares and a lion to terrify the wolves.
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. XVIII (tr. W. K. Marriott)

The image deliberately reverses Cicero, who in *On Duties* (I.41) had condemned force as the way of the lion and fraud as the way of the fox, both unworthy of a human being. And appearances are what count, because "in the actions of all men, and especially of princes, which it is not prudent to challenge, one judges by the result." The slogan "the end justifies the means" is a later summary, not a line in the book.

### *Virtù* and *fortuna*

Two words organize Machiavelli's thought. *Fortuna* is the play of circumstances that no one controls. *Virtù* is not Christian virtue but the energy, skill and decisiveness that let a person master circumstances: the quality of Cesare Borgia, whom chapter VII holds up as a model. How much is up to us? Chapter XXV offers a figure:

> Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less.
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *The Prince*, ch. XXV (tr. W. K. Marriott)

Fortune is a river in flood: you cannot stop it, but in calm weather you can build dikes. Compare this with Boethius's wheel ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)): for Boethius the wise man stops caring about Fortune's gifts; for Machiavelli the able man builds dikes and seizes her. The chapter ends with an image of Fortune as a woman who must be beaten into submission, which is as ugly as it sounds and has drawn sustained feminist commentary, notably Hanna Pitkin's *Fortune Is a Woman* (1984).

### The republican Machiavelli

The puzzle is that Machiavelli's longer book, the *Discourses on Livy*, is a defense of republics. It praises Rome's mixed constitution and argues, against the received view, that the conflict between nobles and people made Rome great:

> I affirm that those who condemn these dissensions between the nobles and the commons, condemn what was the prime cause of Rome becoming free
>
> — Niccolò Machiavelli, *Discourses on Livy* I.4 (tr. Ninian Hill Thomson)

Chapter I.58 even argues "That a People is wiser and more constant than a Prince." How do the two books fit together? Four main readings:

| Reading | Claim | Representative |
|---|---|---|
| Teacher of evil | Machiavelli deliberately breaks with classical and Christian morality; the break founds modernity | Leo Strauss, *Thoughts on Machiavelli* (1958) |
| Satire or warning | *The Prince* exposes how tyrants work so that free peoples can resist them | Spinoza, Rousseau |
| Two moralities | Machiavelli does not separate politics from morality; he opposes pagan civic virtue to Christian virtue, and shows they cannot be combined | Isaiah Berlin, "The Originality of Machiavelli" (1972) |
| Civic republican | Both books serve one ideal, a free and self-governing state; *The Prince* addresses an emergency | Quentin Skinner, J. G. A. Pocock |

Rousseau put the second reading in one line:

> He professed to teach kings; but it was the people he really taught. His Prince is the book of Republicans.
>
> — Jean-Jacques Rousseau, *The Social Contract* III.6, note (tr. G. D. H. Cole)

Berlin's reading is the most philosophically interesting. If he is right, Machiavelli discovered **value pluralism**: there are genuine goods (the safety and glory of the city, the purity of the individual soul) that cannot all be realized together, and no higher principle to rank them. This is the root of the modern "problem of dirty hands" (Michael Walzer, 1973): can a politician be obliged to do something wrong? You will meet the ethical side of this in [the Ethics course, Chapter 12](../learn-ethics/12-contractualism-and-justice.md).

## Montaigne: what do I know?

Michel de Montaigne (1533–1592) was raised speaking only Latin until he was six and became a magistrate in Bordeaux. In 1571, at thirty-eight, he retired to the tower of his château, had Latin and Greek sayings painted on the ceiling beams, and began to write. He called his pieces *essais*: "attempts" or "trials." The form he invented is still with us. His preface warns the reader off:

> Thus, reader, myself am the matter of my book: there's no reason thou shouldst employ thy leisure about so frivolous and vain a subject.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays*, "To the Reader" (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

The modesty is a joke and a program. Montaigne is the first major philosopher to make his own ordinary self (his kidney stones, his bad memory, his preference for white wine) the object of sustained inquiry, on the ground that "every man carries the entire form of human condition" (III.2).

The death of his friend Étienne de La Boétie haunted him; asked why he loved him, Montaigne could only answer, "because it was he, because it was I" (I.28).

### From Stoic to skeptic

The early essays are full of Stoic maxims. "That to study philosophy is to learn to die" (I.20) takes its title from Cicero and its spirit from Seneca:

> The premeditation of death is the premeditation of liberty; he who has learned to die has unlearned to serve.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* I.20 (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

(For Montaigne's place in the Stoic tradition, see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 14](../learn-stoicism/14-stoicism-through-the-ages.md).) The longest essay, the "Apology for Raymond Sebond" (II.12), is something else: a skeptical demolition of human pretension. Sebond was a fifteenth-century theologian who claimed to prove the Christian faith by natural reason. Montaigne's "defense" of him attacks reason itself:

> Presumption is our natural and original disease. The most wretched and frail of all creatures is man, and withal the proudest.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* II.12 (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

He piles up evidence that animals reason, communicate and feel, and asks how we can be sure we know them:

> When I play with my cat who knows whether I do not make her more sport than she makes me?
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* II.12 (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

The philosophical core is Sextus Empiricus's **problem of the criterion**. To decide which appearances are true, we need a criterion; to know the criterion is reliable, we need a proof; to trust the proof, we need a criterion:

> to verify this instrument we must have demonstration; to verify this demonstration an instrument; and here we are round again upon the wheel, and no further advanced.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* II.12 (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

Montaigne had a medal struck with a pair of balanced scales and his motto *Que sais-je?*, which Cotton renders as a question to live by: "What do I know?" Note the form. A dogmatic skeptic says "I know nothing," which refutes itself; a Pyrrhonist asks a question and suspends judgment (*epochē*, [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)).

What follows from this for religion? Montaigne concludes that since reason cannot settle anything, we should hold to the faith and customs we were born into. This is **skeptical fideism**: doubt clears the ground for faith. Popkin reads Montaigne this way; others suspect the piety is a shield. The same skepticism had a sharp political edge in "Of Cannibals" (I.31 in modern editions), written after Montaigne met three Tupinambá men brought from Brazil to Rouen:

> I find that there is nothing barbarous and savage in this nation, by anything that I can gather, excepting, that every one gives the title of barbarism to everything that is not in use in his own country.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* I.30 [modern I.31], "Of Cannibals" (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

He notes that Europeans who torture people to death over theology are in no position to sneer at people who eat their enemies after they are dead. This is cultural relativism used as a weapon of moral criticism, which raises a puzzle you should notice: if all standards are local, from where does Montaigne condemn European cruelty?

### The self in motion

The late essays find a calmer voice. "Of Repentance" opens:

> Others form man; I only report him
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* III.2, "Of Repentance" (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

He cannot pin his subject down, because it keeps changing: "I do not paint its being, I paint its passage." And the last essay, "Of Experience," ends with a defense of ordinary life against heroic philosophy:

> and when seated upon the most elevated throne in the world, we are but seated upon our breech. The fairest lives, in my opinion, are those which regularly accommodate themselves to the common and human model without miracle, without extravagance.
>
> — Michel de Montaigne, *Essays* III.13, "Of Experience" (tr. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt)

Pierre Villey argued (1908) that the *Essays* show a development from Stoicism to skepticism to a natural, Epicurean wisdom. Later scholars, among them Hugo Friedrich and Ann Hartle (*Michel de Montaigne: Accidental Philosopher*, 2003), think the stages too tidy, since Montaigne kept revising the early essays until his death. Either way, he gave modern thought two of its themes: doubt, which Descartes would try to conquer ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)), and the changing, embodied, particular self.

## Bacon: the new instrument

Francis Bacon (1561–1626) rose to be Lord Chancellor of England and fell in 1621, convicted of taking bribes from litigants. (He did not deny taking them; he said they had never changed his verdicts.) His intellectual ambition was larger than his career. He planned a *Great Instauration*, a total renewal of the sciences, of which the *Novum Organum* (1620) was the method. The title challenges Aristotle, whose logical works were known as the *Organon*, "the instrument." Bacon offers a new one.

The opening aphorisms set the goal:

> Knowledge and human power are synonymous, since the ignorance of the cause frustrates the effect; for nature is only subdued by submission
>
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* I.3 (ed. Joseph Devey)

The slogan "knowledge is power" compresses this; the Latin *ipsa scientia potestas est* appears in his earlier *Meditationes Sacrae* (1597). The point is a reorientation. Medieval and ancient science aimed at contemplation, understanding why things are as they are; Bacon wants the "relief of man's estate," science as technology. His utopia, *New Atlantis* (1627), imagines a state-funded research institute, Salomon's House, which inspired the founders of the Royal Society.

### The idols

Before the mind can learn, it must be cleared of false images, which Bacon calls **idols** (Greek *eidōla*, "phantoms"):

> Four species of idols beset the human mind, to which (for distinction's sake) we have assigned names, calling the first Idols of the Tribe, the second Idols of the Den, the third Idols of the Market, the fourth Idols of the Theatre.
>
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* I.39 (ed. Joseph Devey)

1. **Idols of the Tribe** are errors common to the human species: "the human mind resembles those uneven mirrors which impart their own properties to different objects" (I.41). We see patterns and purposes that are not there.
2. **Idols of the Den** (or Cave, after Plato) are individual: each person's temperament, education and favorite authors.
3. **Idols of the Market** come from language, from words "formed at the will of the generality" that name things that do not exist or blur things that do.
4. **Idols of the Theatre** are philosophical systems, "so many plays brought out and performed, creating fictitious and theatrical worlds."

Aphorism 46 gives the best pre-modern description of what psychologists now call **confirmation bias**:

> The human understanding, when any proposition has been once laid down (either from general admission and belief, or from the pleasure it affords), forces everything else to add fresh support and confirmation; and although most cogent and abundant instances may exist to the contrary, yet either does not observe or despises them, or gets rid of and rejects them by some distinction
>
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* I.46 (ed. Joseph Devey)

He illustrates it with a story from Cicero. A skeptic was shown votive pictures painted by sailors who had prayed and survived shipwrecks, and asked whether this did not prove the gods' power. He replied: "But where are the portraits of those who have perished in spite of their vows?" Statisticians today call this **survivorship bias**.

### Ants, spiders and bees

Bacon's positive method is **eliminative induction**. Medieval induction, he complained, jumped from a few cases to the most general principles. Instead, compile tables: a Table of Presence (all cases where heat occurs), a Table of Absence (similar cases where it does not), and a Table of Degrees (where it varies). Then exclude every candidate nature that is not present in all positive cases. One negative instance can refute a generalization that a thousand positive ones cannot prove, which anticipates Popper's falsificationism by three centuries ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)). From his tables on heat, Bacon concluded that heat is a kind of motion, which was close to right. He sums up the ideal in an image:

> Those who have treated of the sciences have been either empirics or dogmatical. The former like ants only heap up and use their store, the latter like spiders spin out their own webs. The bee, a mean between both, extracts matter from the flowers of the garden and the field, but works and fashions it by its own efforts.
>
> — Francis Bacon, *Novum Organum* I.95 (ed. Joseph Devey)

The standard criticism is that Bacon underrated mathematics and bold hypotheses. Galileo did not collect tables; he imagined frictionless planes. Whewell and Popper argued that discovery needs conjecture, not just sorting. But Bacon's picture of science as public, cumulative, experimental and useful became the self-image of the Royal Society. According to John Aubrey, he died in 1626 of a chill caught while stuffing a chicken with snow to see whether cold would preserve meat. The story may be embroidered, but it is the right kind of story.

## Galileo: the book of nature

In 1609 Galileo Galilei (1564–1642), professor of mathematics at Padua, heard of a Dutch spyglass and built his own, eventually grinding lenses that magnified about twenty times. He turned it on the sky, and in March 1610 published *Sidereus Nuncius*, *The Sidereal Messenger* (or *Starry Message*). The moon, which Aristotelian cosmology held to be a perfect sphere of incorruptible celestial matter, turned out to be nothing of the kind. Anyone with a good telescope, Galileo wrote, can know:

> that the Moon certainly does not possess a smooth and polished surface, but one rough and uneven, and, just like the face of the Earth itself, is everywhere full of vast protuberances, deep chasms, and sinuosities.
>
> — Galileo Galilei, *The Sidereal Messenger* (tr. Edward Stafford Carlos)

The Milky Way dissolved into countless stars. Most important, Jupiter had four moons, which Galileo shrewdly named the "Medicean Stars" and thereby earned a court appointment in Florence. The moons mattered for an argument. One objection to Copernicus was that if the earth moved around the sun, it would leave its moon behind. Jupiter answered it:

> for now we have not one planet only revolving about another, while both traverse a vast orbit about the Sun, but our sense of sight presents to us four satellites circling about Jupiter, like the Moon about the Earth, while the whole system travels over a mighty orbit about the Sun in the space of twelve years.
>
> — Galileo Galilei, *The Sidereal Messenger* (tr. Edward Stafford Carlos)

Notice what kind of argument this is. It does not prove Copernicus right. It removes an objection by showing that what was thought impossible actually happens.

### Mathematics and the two kinds of qualities

Galileo's philosophical legacy lies in two claims from *The Assayer* (1623), a polemic about comets. First, the universe is a great book that cannot be read until we learn its language, and that language is mathematics: its characters are triangles, circles and other geometrical figures, without which we wander in a dark labyrinth. Second, the qualities we perceive come in two kinds. Shape, size, number, position and motion belong to bodies themselves; tastes, odors and colors exist only in the perceiver. Galileo's example is tickling: a feather drawn across the sole of the foot produces a tickle, but no one thinks the tickle is in the feather. Remove the living animal and these qualities vanish.

This distinction, later named **primary and secondary qualities** by Boyle and Locke ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)), split the world in two: a colorless world of matter in motion described by mathematics, and a world of colors, sounds and feelings that exists only "in the mind." Where, then, is the mind? That question is the **mind–body problem**, and Descartes inherited it in this form.

### The trial

In his *Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina* (1615), Galileo argued, citing Augustine, that scripture accommodates itself to ordinary understanding and is not a textbook of astronomy. He quoted Cardinal Baronius's quip that the Holy Spirit intends to teach us how one goes to heaven, not how the heavens go. In 1616 the Church declared Copernicanism "formally heretical" as a claim about reality, and Cardinal Bellarmine warned Galileo not to hold or defend it. Bellarmine's position was not stupid: in a letter to Foscarini (1615) he said that if a true demonstration of the earth's motion were found, scripture would have to be reinterpreted, but none had been found. He was right that Galileo lacked one; Galileo's favorite proof, from the tides, was wrong.

In 1632 Galileo's *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* put the Pope's favorite argument into the mouth of a character named Simplicio. In 1633 he was tried, forced to abjure, and confined to his villa, where he wrote *Two New Sciences* (1638), founding the mathematical study of motion. The story that he muttered *Eppur si muove* ("and yet it moves") first appears in print in 1757 and is almost certainly legend.

## Hobbes: the mortal god

Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679) was born, he later wrote in a Latin verse autobiography, when his mother went into premature labor at the news of the Spanish Armada, so that she gave birth to twins: himself and fear. He spent most of his life as tutor and secretary to the Cavendish family, met Galileo in Florence, and fled to France in 1640 as England slid into civil war. He lived to ninety-one.

Aubrey tells how Hobbes, at about forty, found Euclid open at Book I, Proposition 47 in a gentleman's library, swore the theorem was impossible, and followed the proof back proposition by proposition until he was convinced. That is Hobbes's method: start from definitions and build up, step by step, to conclusions no one can deny. He wanted to do for politics what Euclid did for space and Galileo for motion.

### Mechanism

*Leviathan* (1651) opens by comparing living things to machines:

> For seeing life is but a motion of Limbs, the begining whereof is in some principall part within; why may we not say, that all Automata (Engines that move themselves by springs and wheeles as doth a watch) have an artificiall life? For what is the Heart, but a Spring; and the Nerves, but so many Strings; and the Joynts, but so many Wheeles, giving motion to the whole Body
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, Introduction

Hobbes is a thoroughgoing **materialist**: everything that exists is body in motion, including thought. Sensation is motion in the organs; imagination is "decaying sense"; desire is motion toward and aversion motion away. Good and evil are names for the objects of our desires and aversions. And human beings are restless:

> So that in the first place, I put for a generall inclination of all mankind, a perpetuall and restlesse desire of Power after power, that ceaseth onely in Death.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 11

Read the next sentence before you call Hobbes a cynic: the cause is not always that we want more pleasure, but that a person "cannot assure the power and means to live well, which he hath present, without the acquisition of more." The drive for power is a rational response to insecurity.

### The state of nature

Chapter 13 asks what life would be like without government: the **state of nature**. Hobbes's argument can be set out like this:

1. People are roughly equal: "the weakest has strength enough to kill the strongest, either by secret machination, or by confederacy with others."
2. Equality of ability breeds equality of hope: each expects to get what he wants. When two want the same thing that both cannot have, they become enemies.
3. There are three causes of conflict: **competition** (for gain), **diffidence**, meaning distrust (for safety), and **glory** (for reputation).
4. Distrust is decisive. Even if most people are peaceful, you cannot tell who is not, so the reasonable policy is to strike first.
5. So without a common power to keep them all in awe, people are in a condition of war, "and such a warre, as is of every man, against every man."

War here means not constant fighting but a known disposition to fight, as foul weather means not one shower but a tendency to rain. The consequences:

> In such condition, there is no place for Industry; because the fruit thereof is uncertain; and consequently no Culture of the Earth; no Navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by Sea; no commodious Building; no Instruments of moving, and removing such things as require much force; no Knowledge of the face of the Earth; no account of Time; no Arts; no Letters; no Society; and which is worst of all, continuall feare, and danger of violent death; And the life of man, solitary, poore, nasty, brutish, and short.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 13

Hobbes anticipates the objection that this is an insult to human nature, and answers with evidence from your own behavior:

> Let him therefore consider with himselfe, when taking a journey, he armes himselfe, and seeks to go well accompanied; when going to sleep, he locks his dores; when even in his house he locks his chests; and this when he knows there bee Lawes, and publike Officers, armed, to revenge all injuries shall bee done him
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 13

Was there ever a state of nature? Hobbes points to civil wars, to relations between sovereign states, and (with the prejudice of his age) to Native Americans. But the argument needs no historical state of nature. It is a thought experiment about removing the state, and an argument that anything is better than that.

### The way out

Reason finds the exit. The first law of nature is "To seek Peace, and follow it." The second is that a person should be willing, when others are too, to lay down the right to all things and be content with as much liberty against others as he allows them against himself. But promises alone do not help, because the first to perform exposes himself to the second's betrayal:

> And Covenants, without the Sword, are but Words, and of no strength to secure a man at all.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 17

So everyone covenants with everyone else to authorize one person or assembly to act for all, with enough power to enforce the peace:

> This is the Generation of that great LEVIATHAN, or rather (to speake more reverently) of that Mortall God, to which wee owe under the Immortall God, our peace and defence.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, *Leviathan*, ch. 17

The sovereign's power must be **absolute** and undivided, Hobbes argues, because divided sovereignty (king against parliament) is exactly what produced the civil war. Subjects keep only the right to defend their own lives, which no covenant can give away.

### Is it rational to keep the covenant?

Chapter 15 confronts "the Foole," who "hath sayd in his heart, there is no such thing as Justice" and argues that breaking a covenant is reasonable whenever it pays. Hobbes's reply is that the fool may get lucky, but no one will admit him to any alliance if his policy is known, so he gambles on others' errors. Game theorists recast the state of nature as a **prisoner's dilemma**: each does better by defecting whatever the other does, yet both do worse than if both cooperated. David Gauthier (*Morals by Agreement*, 1986), Gregory Kavka and Jean Hampton developed Hobbesian contract theory in these terms; see [the Ethics course, Chapter 12](../learn-ethics/12-contractualism-and-justice.md).

The deepest objection came from Locke ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)). If people are so dangerous that they need an absolute ruler, why trust the ruler, who is also a person, and now an armed one?

> This is to think, that men are so foolish, that they take care to avoid what mischiefs may be done them by pole-cats, or foxes; but are content, nay, think it safety, to be devoured by lions.
>
> — John Locke, *Second Treatise of Government* §93

The argument between Hobbes, Locke and Rousseau is taken up again in [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md).

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Was there a Renaissance?** Jacob Burckhardt's *The Civilization of the Renaissance in Italy* (1860) presented it as the birth of modern individualism. Charles Homer Haskins (*The Renaissance of the Twelfth Century*, 1927) replied that there had been earlier renaissances. Paul Oskar Kristeller showed that humanism was a curriculum rather than a philosophy, and that scholastic Aristotelianism flourished in the universities until the seventeenth century.

**Was there a Scientific Revolution?** Alexandre Koyré and Herbert Butterfield made "the Scientific Revolution" a standard category; Thomas Kuhn generalized it. Steven Shapin's *The Scientific Revolution* (1996) opens with the line that there was no such thing, and that he is writing a book about it. The debate concerns continuity (Pierre Duhem traced Galilean mechanics to fourteenth-century Paris) and the role of non-rational factors: Frances Yates argued that Hermetic magic shaped figures like Giordano Bruno, burned in Rome in 1600.

**Machiavelli** and **Hobbes** each have rival schools, sketched above. On Hobbes, the main dispute is whether his laws of nature are merely prudential (David Gauthier) or genuinely moral obligations grounded in God's command (A. E. Taylor and Howard Warrender), with S. A. Lloyd and others offering reciprocity-based readings.

## Living it

**Audit your idols.** Pick a belief you hold about something empirical: whether a diet works, whether a colleague is competent, whether a policy succeeded. Run Bacon's four idols over it. Tribe: are you seeing a pattern in noise? Den: would you believe it if you had a different temperament or upbringing? Market: is a loaded word doing the work ("woke," "natural," "toxic")? Theatre: is a system you admire supplying the conclusion? Then ask Bacon's question from aphorism 46: where are the portraits of the drowned? Name the evidence that would count against your belief and look for it.

**Find the effectual truth.** Take an institution you belong to and write two short descriptions: the official account of how decisions are made, and Machiavelli's account of how they are actually made. Then ask Berlin's question. Where the two differ, is that corruption, or are there goods (getting things done, protecting people) that the official morality cannot accommodate? Decide what you would do in the gap.

**Essay yourself.** Write a page in Montaigne's manner on one ordinary topic (sleep, anger, a habit, a food) using only your own experience, and then note one point on which you contradict yourself. Montaigne thought that such contradictions are not failures of the method but its findings.

## Summary

- Humanism recovered ancient texts (*ad fontes*), including Plato, Lucretius and Sextus Empiricus; Petrarch, Pico, Erasmus and More turned attention to human dignity, freedom and social criticism.
- Machiavelli's "effectual truth": politics as practiced, not as preached; a prince must learn "how to do wrong"; fear is safer than love; the fox and the lion; *virtù* against *fortuna*. The *Discourses* defend republics and civic conflict. Rival readings: teacher of evil (Strauss), satirist (Rousseau), two incompatible moralities (Berlin), civic republican (Skinner).
- Montaigne invented the essay and made his changing self its subject; his skepticism ("What do I know?", the problem of the criterion, the cat) leads to fideism and to cultural self-criticism ("Of Cannibals").
- Bacon: knowledge as power over nature; the four idols (tribe, den, market, theatre); confirmation and survivorship bias; eliminative induction; the bee between ant and spider. Critics: he underrated mathematics and hypothesis.
- Galileo: the telescope's evidence against Aristotelian cosmology; the book of nature written in mathematics; primary versus secondary qualities, which create the modern mind–body problem; the 1616 decree and the 1633 trial.
- Hobbes: materialism and mechanism; the restless desire for power; the state of nature as a war of all against all; laws of nature; the covenant and the absolute sovereign; the Fool; game-theoretic readings; Locke's polecats and lions.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether a good manager must learn how not to be good

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** You have just been promoted to run a team of twelve. Congratulations. Tell me how you intend to lead.

**You:** Honestly and fairly. I will be open about decisions, give credit where it is due, and treat everyone the same.

*Your turn: Machiavelli will now ask what happens when honesty and the team's survival come apart. Can you think of a case? Write three or four sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I suppose there could be a reorganization coming that I cannot announce yet. But I would still not lie about it.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** Suppose a member of your team asks you directly whether her job is safe, and it is not, and you have been told that if word gets out before the announcement, the whole team will be cut instead of two people. What do you say?

**You:** I would say I cannot discuss it.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** And she, being no fool, hears "no." Word spreads. You have been honest and twelve people lose their jobs instead of two. Did you act well?

**You:** I did not lie. What others infer is not my responsibility.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** A convenient conscience. Which do your people care about more: your clean hands or their salaries? The man who acts by the book among people who do not "soon meets with what destroys him."

**You:** But if I lie once, I lose their trust. Your own book says the prince must avoid being hated, and a manager caught lying will be.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** Good: you are already thinking like a prince. You do not object that lying is wrong; you object that it is imprudent. That is my ground.

**You:** No. I also think it is wrong. But I admit I cannot show that the wrongness outweighs ten people's jobs.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** Then you have found what my readers usually miss. I never said that cruelty and deceit are good. I said that a person responsible for others will sometimes have to choose between their good and his own innocence. Which do you choose?

**You:** If those really are the options, then their good. But you make it too easy. In real offices the choice is rarely that stark, and the man who thinks every situation is an emergency becomes a liar by habit.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** Agreed. That is why I said the prince must know how to do wrong "and to make use of it or not according to necessity." The art lies in the "not." A manager who deceives when it is unnecessary is merely a bad manager.

**You:** So my refined position is this. Honesty is my default and my reputation depends on it. There may be rare cases where protecting the people in my charge requires deceiving them, and in those cases I should do it, and also not pretend it was clean. Berlin would say I am caught between two moralities.

**Niccolò Machiavelli:** Then you have conceded that the good leader and the good person are not always the same. Most of your colleagues will never admit that, even to themselves.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether you would consent to Leviathan

**Thomas Hobbes:** Did you lock your door last night?

**You:** Yes, of course.

**Thomas Hobbes:** Why? You live in a peaceful town with police.

*Your turn: Hobbes is about to use your answer as evidence for his view of human nature. What does your locked door show, and what does it not show? Write three or four sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Because some people steal. Not most, but some.

**Thomas Hobbes:** And since you cannot tell which ones, you treat all as possible thieves. That is my whole state of nature in a single lock. Now take away the police and the courts. What would you do?

**You:** Lock more doors. Maybe buy a weapon. Join with my neighbors for protection.

**Thomas Hobbes:** And your neighbors, seeing you armed and allied, would they feel safer?

**You:** Probably not. They might form their own alliance. I see where this goes: each defensive step looks like a threat to someone else.

**Thomas Hobbes:** Precisely. No one need be wicked. Distrust alone produces war. So would you not agree, in that condition, with everyone else, to give up your right to do whatever you judge necessary, provided they do the same, and to set up one power to enforce the agreement?

**You:** Yes, I would agree to some power. But not an absolute one. I would want a constitution, courts, elections, the right to remove the ruler.

**Thomas Hobbes:** And who decides whether the ruler has broken the constitution?

**You:** The courts.

**Thomas Hobbes:** And if the ruler disagrees with the courts? Then you have two sovereigns, and the question of which prevails is settled by force. I watched that happen between King and Parliament. Divided sovereignty is the state of nature with better uniforms.

**You:** But modern democracies divide power all the time and do not collapse into civil war.

**Thomas Hobbes:** Because in the end everyone accepts some final arbiter that the armed forces obey. Call that the sovereign. My point is about logic, not kings.

**You:** Then I accept a weaker version: there must be a final authority whose decisions are accepted. But Locke's question still stands. If people are dangerous, the person or body holding final power is dangerous too. You protect me from polecats and leave me to the lion.

**Thomas Hobbes:** The lion has reasons to keep his subjects alive and productive, since his strength is theirs. And even a bad sovereign is better than war.

**You:** History has plenty of lions who were not so rational. I think the honest result is this: your argument shows that I need a state, and that the state needs a final authority. It does not show that the authority must be unchecked, because institutions can make the lion answerable without dividing sovereignty in your sense.

**Thomas Hobbes:** Then you have become a constitutionalist by way of my argument, which is how many of them began. Go and read Locke, and see whether he solves the problem or only hides it.

</details>
