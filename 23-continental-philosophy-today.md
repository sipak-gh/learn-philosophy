# Chapter 23 — Continental Philosophy Today

In 1992 the University of Cambridge proposed to give Jacques Derrida an honorary degree. Nearly twenty philosophers from around the world, Quine among them, wrote to *The Times* to protest that his work did not meet accepted standards of clarity and rigor. The university's Senate voted, for the first time in decades, and the degree went through, 336 votes to 204. The episode was the most public battle in a long cold war between "analytic" philosophy ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)) and "continental" philosophy, the tradition of Hegel, Marx, Nietzsche, Husserl and Heidegger as it developed in Germany and France after 1930. This chapter maps that second tradition from the **Frankfurt School's** critical theory through **structuralism** and **post-structuralism** (Foucault, Derrida, Deleuze) to **Habermas** and the movements of the last thirty years. The texts are difficult and often deliberately so. The aim here is to state their central ideas as plainly as possible, so you can judge for yourself whether the difficulty is earned. Previous: [Chapter 22 — Political Philosophy](22-political-philosophy.md). Next: [Chapter 24 — Building a Philosophy of Life](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md).

## What is continental philosophy?

The label is a bad one. It contrasts a style with a place, as if one divided cuisine into "Chinese" and "spicy". Frege, Wittgenstein, Carnap and the Vienna Circle were as continental as could be; many of today's leading "continental" philosophers teach in the United States. The label was popularized by English-speaking philosophers in the mid-twentieth century to mark off what they were not doing. A famous 1958 conference at Royaumont, near Paris, meant to bring the two sides together, produced mostly mutual incomprehension: Gilbert Ryle predicted that phenomenology was headed for collapse, and the French participants wondered aloud whether the British had a philosophy at all.

Still, the traditions differ in recognizable ways, as tendencies rather than rules.

- **History.** Continental philosophers tend to think that concepts have histories and that you can't understand a problem without understanding how it arose. Analytic philosophers tend to treat problems as timeless puzzles, to be solved with the best current tools.
- **Models.** Analytic philosophy took logic and the natural sciences as models; continental philosophy took literature, history, psychoanalysis and political theory.
- **Aims.** Continental philosophy is more often **critical** in a political sense, asking whose interests a way of thinking serves, and less often content with clarifying concepts.
- **Ancestors.** Continental philosophy descends from Kant through Hegel, Marx, Nietzsche, Husserl and Heidegger ([Chapter 13](13-kant.md), [Chapter 14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md), [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md), [Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)). Analytic philosophy descends from Kant through Frege, Russell and the logical positivists.

### The masters of suspicion

The French philosopher Paul Ricoeur, in *Freud and Philosophy* (1965), called Marx, Nietzsche and Freud the three "masters of suspicion." Each taught that our conscious beliefs about ourselves are not to be taken at face value, because they are produced by forces we don't see: class interest for Marx, the will to power for Nietzsche, unconscious desire for Freud. Most of this chapter's thinkers inherit their method of reading against the grain.

Marx's eleventh thesis on Feuerbach, carved on his tombstone in Highgate Cemetery, sets the agenda for critical theory: "Philosophers have only interpreted the world differently, but the point is to change it." His concept of **ideology** names systems of ideas that present the interests of a ruling class as natural and universal.

Nietzsche ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md); in depth in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 6](../learn-nihilism/06-nietzsche-the-death-of-god.md)) supplied the method of **genealogy**: tracing a value or institution back through its history to show that it has no single fixed meaning or origin. His central example was punishment:

> The origin of the existence of a thing and its final utility, its practical application and incorporation in a system of ends, are *toto cœlo* opposed to each other--everything, anything, which exists and which prevails anywhere, will always be put to new purposes by a force superior to itself, will be commandeered afresh, will be turned and transformed to new uses...
>
> — Friedrich Nietzsche, *The Genealogy of Morals*, Second Essay, §12 (tr. Samuel)

A few pages later Nietzsche concludes that it is no longer possible to say what punishment is *for*: "it is only that which has no history, which can be defined." Foucault would take this sentence and build a career on it.

Freud described his discovery of the unconscious as the third great blow to human self-love, after Copernicus displaced the Earth and Darwin displaced humanity from special creation:

> But the third and most irritating insult is flung at the human mania of greatness by present-day psychological research, which wants to prove to the "I" that it is not even master in its own home, but is dependent upon the most scanty information concerning all that goes on unconsciously in its psychic life.
>
> — Sigmund Freud, *A General Introduction to Psychoanalysis*, Lecture XVIII (tr. Hall)

In his late *Civilization and Its Discontents* (1930), Freud turned to society. Civilization makes us secure but makes us unhappy, because it is built on the suppression of our instincts, sexual and aggressive:

> ...it is impossible to ignore the extent to which civilization is built up on renunciation of instinctual gratifications, the degree to which the existence of civilization presupposes the non-gratification (suppression, repression or something else?) of powerful instinctual urgencies.
>
> — Sigmund Freud, *Civilization and Its Discontents*, Chapter III (tr. Riviere)

The aggression doesn't disappear; it is turned inward as the **superego**, the harsh internal judge we experience as guilt. The Frankfurt School would try to combine Freud's diagnosis with Marx's.

## The Frankfurt School and critical theory

### The Institute

The Institute for Social Research was founded in Frankfurt in 1923 with money from a grain merchant's son, Felix Weil, as a center for Marxist research independent of any party. Under **Max Horkheimer**, director from 1930, it became the home of a group that included **Theodor W. Adorno**, **Herbert Marcuse**, **Erich Fromm**, and, more loosely, **Walter Benjamin**. Nearly all were Jewish, and in 1933 they fled the Nazis, first to Geneva, then to Columbia University in New York and to Los Angeles. Benjamin, trying to escape occupied France over the Pyrenees in 1940, took his own life at the Spanish border when told he would be returned to the Gestapo.

Their problem was the failure of Marx's predictions. The workers of the advanced capitalist countries had not made a revolution. Instead, many had supported fascism; in the Soviet Union, the revolution had produced Stalin; and in America, a prosperous working class seemed content with consumer goods and mass entertainment. Why? Marx's economics could not answer. The Frankfurt theorists turned to Freud's psychology, Weber's sociology of rationalization, and the analysis of culture.

In "Traditional and Critical Theory" (1937), Horkheimer distinguished two kinds of social theory. **Traditional theory**, modeled on natural science, describes society as it is and treats its own standpoint as neutral. **Critical theory** recognizes that the theorist is part of the society she studies, and aims not only to understand society but to emancipate people from the forms of domination it contains. Critical theory, in this technical sense, is any theory with that double aim.

### Dialectic of Enlightenment

Written in California exile during the war, Horkheimer and Adorno's *Dialectic of Enlightenment* asks how the most scientifically advanced civilization in history had produced Auschwitz. Their answer, put plainly, is that **enlightenment reverts to myth**. Reason began as a tool for freeing humanity from fear of nature, by understanding and controlling it. But reason narrowed into **instrumental reason**: calculating the most efficient means to given ends, without asking whether the ends are good. Everything, nature and people alike, becomes material to be measured, managed and used. Domination of nature turns into domination of human beings, and in the end, of our own inner nature. The bureaucratic efficiency of the death camps was not a lapse from modern rationality but one of its possibilities. Their reading of Homer's Odysseus, who has himself bound to the mast so he can hear the Sirens while his oarsmen, ears stopped with wax, row on, is a parable of the bourgeois self: enjoyment for the master, labor for the workers, and self-denial for both.

Their chapter on **the culture industry** argues that mass-produced entertainment (Hollywood films, radio, popular music) is not a democratization of art but a form of social control. Its products are standardized, their differences superficial, and they train audiences in passive consumption and conformity. Leisure becomes a copy of work. Critics have called this snobbery: Adorno's dismissal of jazz is notorious. But the analysis of how media industries shape desire has seemed more prescient with every decade, and it underlies much current criticism of algorithmic social media.

Adorno's later work, *Minima Moralia* (1951) and *Negative Dialectics* (1966), is fragmentary and bleak. His most quoted remark, from a 1949 essay, is that "to write poetry after Auschwitz is barbaric". He later qualified it, but the question behind it, what culture can mean after such a catastrophe, became a central question for postwar European thought.

### Marcuse and the New Left

Herbert Marcuse, who stayed in America, became the philosopher of the 1960s student movement. *Eros and Civilization* (1955) argued, against Freud, that much of the repression civilization demands is **surplus repression**, required not by civilization itself but by a particular system of domination, and that a less repressive society was possible. *One-Dimensional Man* (1964) argued that advanced industrial society absorbs all opposition: by satisfying "false needs" for consumer goods, it produces people who can no longer imagine an alternative. His essay "Repressive Tolerance" (1965) controversially argued that tolerance in an unequal society protects the powerful, and that tolerance of movements from the right might legitimately be withdrawn. Critics on both left and right found this alarming, and it remains so.

Benjamin, meanwhile, had argued in "The Work of Art in the Age of Its Technological Reproducibility" (1935) that photography and film destroy the **aura** of the unique artwork, its presence in a particular time and place. That could be emancipatory, since art comes off its pedestal and becomes available to the masses. But it could also be dangerous, as in the fascist aestheticizing of politics through mass rallies and newsreels.

## Structuralism

### Saussure and the sign

While critical theory developed in Germany, a different movement grew in France from an unlikely source: a course on linguistics given in Geneva between 1907 and 1911 by **Ferdinand de Saussure** and published by his students after his death as the *Course in General Linguistics* (1916). Saussure's ideas, paraphrased:

- **Langue and parole.** Linguistics should study not individual acts of speaking (*parole*) but the underlying system that makes them possible (*langue*), as a chess theorist studies the rules, not particular games.
- **The sign.** A linguistic sign unites a **signifier** (a sound-pattern, such as the sound *tree*) with a **signified** (a concept). The connection between them is **arbitrary**: nothing about the sound *tree* resembles trees, as the French *arbre* shows.
- **Difference.** Signs get their value not from any positive content but from their differences from other signs in the system. "Cat" means what it does because it isn't "bat", "cap" or "dog". In language, Saussure argued, there are only differences, with no positive terms.

The last idea was explosive. If meaning is a product of a system of differences, then perhaps every cultural phenomenon (myth, kinship, fashion, the unconscious) is a sign system that can be analyzed the same way, independently of what individuals think they are doing.

### The structuralist movement

In the 1950s and 1960s **structuralism** swept French intellectual life, displacing Sartre's existentialism, with its emphasis on individual freedom and choice ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)). The structuralists' slogan might have been that we don't speak language, language speaks us.

- **Claude Lévi-Strauss** applied structural analysis to anthropology. Kinship systems across cultures were structured by the exchange of women between groups, and myths were organized by **binary oppositions** (raw and cooked, nature and culture, life and death) that the stories attempt to mediate. The "savage mind", he argued, is as logical as the scientist's; it simply works with concrete materials.
- **Roland Barthes** analyzed modern culture as a system of myths. In *Mythologies* (1957), short essays decode professional wrestling, steak and chips, a soap-powder advertisement, and, most famously, a *Paris Match* cover showing a young Black soldier saluting the French flag, which silently says that France is a great empire whose subjects of all colors serve it loyally. Myth, for Barthes, turns history into nature: it makes what is contingent and political look obvious and eternal.
- **Jacques Lacan** reread Freud through Saussure, arguing that the unconscious is structured like a language. His **mirror stage** describes the infant who first recognizes itself in a mirror, forming its sense of self through an external image: the ego is from the start an imaginary construction.
- **Louis Althusser** reread Marx structurally. His account of **interpellation** (1970) describes how ideology works by "hailing" individuals. A police officer calls "Hey, you there!" and you turn around, recognizing yourself as the one addressed, and so as a subject. Schools, churches, families and media, his "ideological state apparatuses", constitute us as subjects who freely accept our place.

Structuralism's ambition was to make the human sciences as rigorous as linguistics. Its cost was the disappearance of the free, conscious subject who had been the hero of philosophy since Descartes. The phrase "the death of man", from the last page of Foucault's *The Order of Things* (1966), summarized the mood: "man" was a recent invention, which might soon be erased like a face drawn in the sand where the sea comes in.

## Post-structuralism

By the late 1960s, the most important French thinkers had turned against structuralism's own pretensions to scientific objectivity, though they kept its suspicion of the subject. This loose group, never a school, is called **post-structuralism**. The events of May 1968, when student revolts and a general strike nearly brought down the French government, gave their work a political edge.

### Foucault: power and knowledge

**Michel Foucault** (1926–1984) was a historian of systems of thought, as his chair at the Collège de France was titled. His histories of madness, medicine, the prison and sexuality share a method he called first **archaeology** and later, following Nietzsche, **genealogy**. Each shows that something we take to be natural and timeless has a history, recent and contingent, and is bound up with forms of power.

*Discipline and Punish* (1975) opens with a scene that readers never forget. In 1757, Robert-François Damiens, who had attempted to kill Louis XV, was publicly tortured and dismembered in Paris in front of a crowd. Foucault sets beside this a timetable for a house of young prisoners in Paris eighty years later: rise at the first drum roll, dress in silence, prayer, work, meals, school, all scheduled to the minute. In less than a century, punishment changed from a spectacle inflicted on the body to a discipline aimed at the soul. The standard story is that punishment became more humane. Foucault's argument is that it became more efficient: power stopped being something that crushed people occasionally and became something that shaped them continually.

The emblem of this **disciplinary power** is the **Panopticon**, a prison designed by Jeremy Bentham in 1791. Cells are arranged in a ring around a central tower, from which an inspector can see into every cell without being seen. Since prisoners never know when they are being watched, they must behave as if they always are. They come to watch themselves. Foucault argued that the same techniques (**surveillance**, examination, ranking against norms, detailed timetables) spread through schools, barracks, factories and hospitals, producing **docile bodies**: individuals who are useful, productive and obedient, and who experience their conformity as their own nature.

Foucault's central and most debated thesis is that **power and knowledge** are inseparable. The human sciences (psychiatry, criminology, pedagogy) did not simply discover truths about madness, criminality and childhood; they arose together with the institutions that confined, reformed and examined people, and they produced the categories ("the delinquent", "the homosexual") they claimed to describe. Power, on this view, is not only repressive, a matter of saying no. It is **productive**: it creates identities, desires and forms of knowledge. And it is not possessed by a sovereign at the top but circulates through a network of relations, "capillary", in the institutions of daily life.

In the first volume of *The History of Sexuality* (1976), Foucault applied this to sex. The standard story, the **repressive hypothesis**, says the Victorians repressed sexuality and the twentieth century liberated it. Foucault argued that the nineteenth century in fact produced an explosion of talk about sex, in confessionals, medicine, psychiatry and pedagogy, and that this talk was a means of control. He also described **biopower**: modern states' concern with managing the life of populations, through statistics on birth rates, health and hygiene. The concept has become central to debates about public health, pandemics and data.

In his last years, Foucault turned to ancient ethics, which he read through the French historian Pierre Hadot, as a set of **technologies of the self**: practices like the Stoics' evening review and meditation by which people shaped themselves into ethical subjects. He hoped such practices might offer an ethics not grounded in universal law or human nature. The connection with Stoicism is direct; see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 15](../learn-stoicism/15-modern-stoicism.md).

**Objections.** If all knowledge is entangled with power, what about Foucault's own histories? And if there is no standpoint outside power, from what standpoint does he criticize it? Habermas and Nancy Fraser pressed this point: Foucault's work is full of indignation, but he refused to say what makes some forms of power worse than others. Historians have also challenged his evidence. Foucault replied that he aimed not to judge but to show that things could be otherwise, opening a space for freedom.

### Derrida: deconstruction

**Jacques Derrida** (1930–2004), born into a Jewish family in French Algeria, burst onto the scene in 1966 with a lecture at Johns Hopkins University, "Structure, Sign, and Play", and published three books in 1967, including *Of Grammatology*. His method, **deconstruction**, is a way of reading philosophical and literary texts so as to expose the tensions on which they rely.

Derrida took Saussure's insight, that meaning arises from differences, more seriously than Saussure did. If a sign's meaning depends on its differences from all other signs, then meaning is never fully present in any one sign. It is always deferred along a chain of other signs. Derrida coined the word **différance**, spelled with an *a* that sounds the same in French as *différence*, to combine "differ" and "defer" (and to show, with a difference you can only *see*, that writing is not just a record of speech).

The target was what Derrida called the **metaphysics of presence** or **logocentrism**: the assumption, running from Plato to Husserl, that meaning is ultimately grounded in something fully present to consciousness: a Form, God, the self-present thought, the speaker's intention. A symptom is **phonocentrism**, the privilege of speech over writing. From Plato's *Phaedrus* to Rousseau and Saussure, writing has been treated as a secondary, dangerous supplement to the living voice, a mere copy of speech that can circulate without its author and be misunderstood. Derrida showed that the features attributed to writing (dependence on a system of differences, possibility of repetition in the author's absence) belong to speech as well, indeed to all signification.

Deconstruction typically proceeds by identifying a **binary opposition** in a text (speech and writing, nature and culture, presence and absence, male and female), showing that the text privileges one term, and then showing that the text itself, in its details, undermines that hierarchy. The privileged term turns out to depend on the one it excludes. The point is not to reverse the hierarchy but to unsettle the system of oppositions.

His most notorious sentence, from *Of Grammatology*, is usually translated "There is nothing outside the text." It has been read as a claim that the world doesn't exist, only words. Derrida later explained that he meant something closer to "there is no outside-context": nothing is given to us independently of a system of differences and interpretation, which is a claim most pragmatists ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md)) and the later Wittgenstein ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)) would accept.

His famous exchange with John Searle (1977) over J. L. Austin's theory of speech acts ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)) became a test case of mutual incomprehension. Derrida argued that Austin's exclusion of "non-serious" utterances (an actor's promise on stage) as parasitic on ordinary ones couldn't be maintained, since the possibility of being quoted, repeated or performed belongs to every utterance; Searle replied that Derrida had misunderstood Austin; Derrida replied at great length and with mockery. Readers still disagree about who won.

In his later work Derrida turned explicitly to ethics and politics: hospitality, forgiveness, the gift, friendship, and justice, which he argued is "undeconstructible", an infinite demand never satisfied by any law. Influenced by Emmanuel Levinas, for whom ethics begins in the face-to-face encounter with the Other who calls me to responsibility, he wrote on the ethics of welcoming refugees and on the death penalty.

### Deleuze: philosophy as the creation of concepts

**Gilles Deleuze** (1925–1995) is the most metaphysical of the three. Where Derrida found philosophy's tradition of identity and presence everywhere, Deleuze sought out the tradition's outliers (Lucretius, Spinoza, Hume, Nietzsche, Bergson) and built from them a **philosophy of difference**. In *Difference and Repetition* (1968), he argued that Western thought subordinated difference to identity, treating differences as differences *between* things already defined. For Deleuze, difference comes first: reality is a process of becoming, of differentiation, out of which stable identities are temporary products. This is a metaphysics in the spirit of Heraclitus ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)) and Bergson ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)).

With the psychoanalyst Félix Guattari, Deleuze wrote *Anti-Oedipus* (1972) and *A Thousand Plateaus* (1980). *Anti-Oedipus* attacked psychoanalysis for reducing desire to the family drama (daddy-mommy-me) and argued that desire is not a lack but a productive force, which capitalism both unleashes and recaptures. *A Thousand Plateaus* contrasts **arborescent** thought, organized like a tree with a root and a hierarchy of branches, with **rhizomatic** thought, like the root system of grass, spreading without a center, any point connecting with any other. The internet was not yet invented, but the image fits it.

In their last joint book, *What Is Philosophy?* (1991), they define philosophy as the creation of concepts, as distinct from science (which creates functions) and art (which creates sensations). And Deleuze's two-page "Postscript on the Societies of Control" (1990) argued that Foucault's disciplinary societies, with their enclosed institutions, were giving way to **societies of control**: continuous, modulating, networked forms of power, in which you are never finished with anything (education, work, debt) and are tracked by codes and passwords rather than confined in buildings. Read in the age of the smartphone, it seems uncanny.

### The backlash

Post-structuralism became enormously influential in literature departments, especially in the United States, from the 1970s. In philosophy departments it met resistance. Critics charged it with obscurity, with relativism (if all truth is an effect of power or text, what about the truth that the Holocaust happened?) and with political quietism (if every position is complicit with power, why act?).

In 1996 the physicist Alan Sokal submitted a parody article, "Transgressing the Boundaries: Towards a Transformative Hermeneutics of Quantum Gravity", to the cultural studies journal *Social Text*, which published it. He revealed the hoax soon after, in the magazine *Lingua Franca*, and he and Jean Bricmont followed with *Impostures intellectuelles* (1997; in English *Fashionable Nonsense*, 1998), cataloging misuses of science by French theorists, Lacan and Deleuze among them. Defenders replied that the hoax showed lax editing, not the bankruptcy of a field, and that the misuses were peripheral. Martha Nussbaum's essay "The Professor of Parody" (1999) attacked Judith Butler's style and politics from within the left, arguing that obscurity encouraged a retreat from practical political work.

There is a fair verdict somewhere in between. Some of this writing is needlessly obscure, and some of it uses science and mathematics as decoration. But the central ideas (that meanings depend on systems of difference, that institutions shape what counts as knowledge, that the categories of human sciences have political histories, that the self is not transparent to itself) can be stated clearly, and once stated they are hard to dismiss.

## Habermas: reason as communication

**Jürgen Habermas** (born 1929), Adorno's assistant and the leading figure of the Frankfurt School's second generation, has spent a long career trying to rescue the Enlightenment from its critics, both from his teachers' pessimism and from the post-structuralists.

His first major book, *The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere* (1962), traced the rise in eighteenth-century coffeehouses, salons and newspapers of a **public sphere** where private individuals met to debate matters of common concern as equals, judging arguments by their merits rather than the speakers' rank. It also traced its decline, as mass media and public relations turned citizens back into consumers. The book has become indispensable in debates about social media.

In *The Theory of Communicative Action* (1981), Habermas argued that Adorno and Horkheimer had mistaken one form of reason for reason itself. Alongside **strategic** or instrumental action, aimed at success, there is **communicative action**, aimed at reaching understanding. A speaker who makes a claim implicitly raises **validity claims**: that what she says is true, that it is appropriate, and that she is sincere. She commits herself to defending them with reasons if challenged. Rationality is built into the structure of language use itself, and it is not only instrumental. It is the shared practice of giving and asking for reasons. (Compare the pragmatists in [Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md) and Robert Brandom's analytic version of the same idea.)

Modern societies, he argued, have two spheres. The **lifeworld** of family, culture and civil society is coordinated by communicative action, and the **systems** of the market and the state bureaucracy by money and power. The pathology of modernity is the **colonization of the lifeworld**, when money and administrative power invade domains, such as education, family and culture, that should be governed by mutual understanding.

**Discourse ethics** follows. Valid moral norms are those that all affected persons could agree to as participants in a practical discourse. Such a discourse would approximate an **ideal speech situation**: everyone affected may participate and raise any question, no one is coerced, and the only force at work is what Habermas calls "the unforced force of the better argument". This is not a description of any actual debate. It is a standard implicit in our practice of argument, against which actual debates, distorted by power, can be criticized. In politics, it underwrites **deliberative democracy** ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)), in which legitimacy flows from inclusive public deliberation, not just from voting. Habermas debated Rawls in 1995; their views are close cousins, both rooted in Kant, and the exchange was civil.

Here Habermas confronts Foucault directly. In *The Philosophical Discourse of Modernity* (1985), he argued that the post-structuralists' critiques of reason are **performatively contradictory**: they use reason, argument and evidence to attack reason, and they depend on unacknowledged norms of freedom and justice to criticize power. Foucault's defenders reply that Habermas's ideal speech situation is itself a fiction that conceals real power, and that consensus is often how exclusion operates. Axel Honneth, of the third generation, has developed a theory of social struggles for **recognition**, building on Hegel's master-slave dialectic.

## Recent directions

Since 1990 the continental tradition has fragmented into many lively projects. A few worth knowing:

- **Gender and queer theory.** Judith Butler's *Gender Trouble* (1990) argued, drawing on Foucault, Derrida and Beauvoir ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)), that gender is **performative**: not an inner essence that behavior expresses but something constituted by the repeated performance of norms. Even "sex" may not be the natural bedrock on which gender is built. The book is central to queer theory and to current debates about gender.
- **Postcolonial theory.** Frantz Fanon's *The Wretched of the Earth* (1961) analyzed the psychology of colonial violence and decolonization. Edward Said's *Orientalism* (1978), using Foucault's power-knowledge, showed how Western scholarship and art constructed "the Orient" as exotic, irrational and in need of rule. Gayatri Chakravorty Spivak's "Can the Subaltern Speak?" (1988) asked whether the voices of the most marginalized can be heard at all when even sympathetic intellectuals speak for them. Achille Mbembe has extended Foucault's biopower to **necropolitics**: power over who must die.
- **The return of metaphysics.** **Speculative realism**, launched at a 2007 London workshop, rejects what Quentin Meillassoux called **correlationism**, the post-Kantian assumption that we can only ever know the correlation between thought and being, never being by itself. In *After Finitude* (2006), Meillassoux asks how a correlationist can make sense of scientific statements about events before any life existed, such as the formation of the Earth. Graham Harman's **object-oriented ontology** argues that all objects, not just human minds, withdraw from full relation. **New materialists** (Jane Bennett, Karen Barad) attribute agency to matter itself.
- **Radical politics.** Alain Badiou's philosophy of the **event**, a rupture that creates new truths to which subjects can be faithful (a revolution, a scientific discovery, falling in love); Slavoj Žižek's Lacanian Marxist cultural criticism; Giorgio Agamben's **bare life** and the state of exception, invoked in debates about camps, detention and emergency powers; Jacques Rancière on equality and the politics of aesthetics.
- **Technology and the self.** Bernard Stiegler on technics and memory; Byung-Chul Han's short, popular books, such as *The Burnout Society* (2010), arguing that the old disciplinary society of prohibition has become an achievement society of self-exploitation, where you are your own overseer. The analysis extends Foucault and Deleuze to the age of the productivity app.
- **Phenomenology renewed.** Dan Zahavi and Shaun Gallagher have brought phenomenology into dialogue with cognitive science; **critical phenomenology** (Lisa Guenther, Gayle Salamon) studies the lived experience of solitary confinement, disability, race and gender.

Meanwhile the analytic–continental divide has weakened. Analytic philosophers such as Robert Brandom and John McDowell read Hegel; continental philosophers write on cognitive science; and much of the best current work in ethics and political philosophy draws on both. Younger philosophers increasingly treat the distinction as a matter of history rather than of loyalty.

## Living it

1. **Spot the Panopticon.** Make a list of every way your behavior is monitored in one day: phone location, workplace software, cameras, grades, fitness apps, social media metrics. For each, ask Foucault's question: how has it changed what you do even when no one is actually watching?
2. **A mythology.** Pick an advertisement, a sports broadcast or a viral video and analyze it as Barthes would. What does it say explicitly, and what does it say silently? What does it present as natural that is actually historical or political?
3. **Deconstruct an opposition.** Find a binary opposition in something you read this week (work/leisure, reason/emotion, nature/technology, normal/abnormal). Which term is privileged? Find a place where the text depends on the term it excludes.
4. **The better argument.** Next time you're in a disagreement, try Habermas's standard: are you trying to persuade by the force of the better argument, or by strategic means (status, pressure, silence, rhetoric)? What would it take to make the conversation more like an ideal speech situation?

## Summary

- "Continental philosophy" names a tradition, not a place: historically minded, critical, descending from Hegel, Marx, Nietzsche, Husserl and Heidegger. The **masters of suspicion** (Marx, Nietzsche, Freud) taught it to read beneath consciousness: ideology, genealogy, the unconscious.
- The **Frankfurt School** (Horkheimer, Adorno, Marcuse, Benjamin): **critical theory** aims at emancipation; *Dialectic of Enlightenment* argues that **instrumental reason** reverts to myth and domination; the **culture industry**; Marcuse's surplus repression and one-dimensional society; Benjamin's aura.
- **Structuralism**: Saussure's arbitrary sign and meaning as difference; Lévi-Strauss's binary oppositions; Barthes's myths; Lacan's unconscious structured like a language; Althusser's interpellation; the "death of man".
- **Foucault**: genealogy; the shift from spectacle to **discipline**; the **Panopticon** and docile bodies; **power-knowledge**; the repressive hypothesis and **biopower**; technologies of the self.
- **Derrida**: **deconstruction** of binary oppositions; **différance**; critique of the metaphysics of presence and phonocentrism; "nothing outside the text"; the Searle debate; later ethics of hospitality and justice.
- **Deleuze** (with Guattari): philosophy of difference and becoming; desire as productive; the rhizome; **societies of control**.
- The **backlash**: charges of obscurity and relativism; the **Sokal hoax**; Nussbaum on Butler.
- **Habermas**: the **public sphere**; **communicative action** and validity claims; lifeworld and system; **discourse ethics** and the ideal speech situation; deliberative democracy; the charge of performative contradiction against post-structuralism; Honneth's recognition.
- **Recent directions**: Butler's gender performativity; postcolonial theory (Fanon, Said, Spivak, Mbembe); speculative realism and new materialism; Badiou, Žižek, Agamben; Han's burnout society; renewed phenomenology; a weakening divide.

### Dialogue 1 — On the fitness tracker

**Foucault:** You wear a device that counts your steps, monitors your sleep and tells you when to stand up. You chose to buy it. Are you more free or less?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Foucault in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** More free, I think. I bought it to help me do something I wanted to do anyway, be healthier. No one forces me to wear it, and I can take it off whenever I want.

**Foucault:** And what counts as "healthy"? Ten thousand steps, eight hours of sleep, a resting heart rate below a certain number. Where did those norms come from?

**You:** From research, I suppose. Or from the company. Actually, I've read that ten thousand steps came from a Japanese marketing campaign in the 1960s.

**Foucault:** So a norm with a history, presented as a fact about your body. You measure yourself against it daily. You feel a small pang of guilt when you fall short. Who is watching you?

**You:** No one. Me.

**Foucault:** That is the Panopticon's achievement. The inspector in the tower no longer needs to be there. You have become your own inspector, and you experience it as freedom.

**Habermas:** But surely it matters whether the norm is a good one. Walking more is better for most people than walking less. If you were persuaded by good reasons to walk more, why call that discipline rather than learning?

**Foucault:** I don't say that walking is bad. I say that you should see the mechanism, so that you can decide what to do with it.

**You:** Here's where I come out. Habermas is right that some norms are well-supported and following them isn't oppression. But Foucault shows me something I hadn't noticed: the device doesn't just help me reach my goals, it shapes what I count as a goal, and I've taken its numbers as facts about me. So I'll keep it, but I'll check the reasons behind its norms, and I'll take it off sometimes, just to find out whether I can.

</details>

### Dialogue 2 — On what a text means

**Searle:** When I write "Close the window," I mean that you should close the window. That's what the sentence means. Isn't it obvious that a text means what its author intended?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Searle in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** For a note like that, yes. But with a poem or a constitution, people read things into it the author never intended, and sometimes the readings seem right. And authors are often dead, so we can't ask them.

**Derrida:** More than that. For your note to function as writing at all, it must be readable in your absence: by someone who finds it tomorrow, who doesn't know who wrote it, who might be an actor reading it in a play. That possibility isn't an accident that sometimes befalls a text. It is what makes a text a text.

**Searle:** That's a confusion. The possibility of being misused doesn't change what the sentence means in a normal context. Austin excluded the actor's promise because it's parasitic on real promises. There's nothing mysterious about it.

**Derrida:** And who decides which context is normal? The exclusion you call obvious is precisely what I am asking about.

**You:** But you're not saying that "Close the window" could mean anything at all?

**Derrida:** No. I never said anything goes. I said that meaning depends on contexts, and that no context can be fully closed off. There is always another reading to consider.

**Searle:** Which, if true, applies equally to what you just said, and yet you expect me to understand you.

**Derrida:** And you do. Partly.

**You:** Here's my take. Searle is right that in ordinary communication, intentions and shared context usually settle meaning well enough, and that's why language works. Derrida is right that this settling is never guaranteed, and that the cases where it fails (law, scripture, poetry, contracts) are exactly the ones where the stakes are highest. So I'll read for the author's intention first, but I won't pretend that's the end of interpretation.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether argument can be free of power

**Habermas:** In a genuine argument, the only force that should move us is the force of the better argument. Isn't that the standard by which every debate should be judged?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Habermas in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It's a good standard. But I've been in plenty of meetings where the "better argument" was whatever the senior person said, and anyone who disagreed was called emotional or unreasonable.

**Habermas:** Exactly, and you can criticize that meeting only because you have a standard it failed. Without the ideal, your complaint would make no sense.

**Foucault:** Or the standard itself is part of the problem. Who decides what counts as a reason, what counts as calm and rational speech? Those norms have a history. They have often been used to exclude women, the colonized, the mad, from being heard at all.

**Habermas:** And how do you know they were wrongly excluded? You appeal to a norm of inclusion, to a right to be heard. You use the ideal while denying it.

**Foucault:** I use whatever tools are at hand. I don't claim they are free of history.

**Adorno:** Allow me to be pessimistic. Even the "unforced" agreement you hope for may be produced by a culture industry that has shaped everyone's desires in advance. Consensus is no proof of freedom.

**You:** So the ideal can't guarantee anything, and the critique of the ideal still needs something like it.

**Habermas:** Yes. That is my point.

**Foucault:** And mine is that you must never stop examining who is being excluded in its name.

**You:** Then I'll hold both. Habermas gives me the standard: in a conversation that matters, everyone affected should be able to speak and only reasons should count. Foucault and Adorno give me the warning: what passes for "reason" in any given room may carry hidden exclusions, and agreement may be manufactured. So the ideal is something to aim at and use for criticism, not something I can assume any debate already meets, including the ones where I'm the one with the power.

</details>
