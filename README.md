# Learn Philosophy: From Thales to Today

Philosophy is the habit of asking what everyone else takes for granted. What is real? How do you know? What do you owe other people? Is the self you call "I" one thing or many? Can a life go well or badly in a way that has nothing to do with how it feels? You already hold answers to these questions, picked up from parents, schools, religions, films and friends. Philosophy is the twenty-six-century conversation in which people have tested such answers, broken them and built better ones, and this course invites you into it. Its other promise is practical: philosophy is a toolkit for building your self and your values on purpose instead of by accident.

It is also a lot of fun. The conversation is full of characters and good arguments. Thales falls into a well while staring at the stars, and a servant girl laughs. Zeno proves that a runner can never finish a race. Socrates is condemned to death for asking questions and spends his last day arguing that death is nothing to fear. Descartes doubts everything until he finds the one thing he cannot doubt; Hume wakes Kant from his "dogmatic slumber"; Nietzsche announces that God is dead and asks what we will do now. In the twentieth century a Viennese club tries to abolish metaphysics, a bat becomes the hardest problem in the philosophy of mind, and a three-page paper by Edmund Gettier breaks a definition of knowledge that had stood since Plato. Along the way you meet the classical traditions of India and China, the philosophers of Islam and medieval Judaism, the pragmatists, the existentialists and the critics of race, gender and power. Each chapter asks you to try an idea on, test it against your own life and argue back. In the last chapter you write down your own philosophy of life and know where every piece of it came from.

## Who it's for

Anyone who has caught themselves asking "but why?" one step further than the conversation allowed. No background in philosophy is assumed: the first chapters teach the basic tools (what an argument is, how to read a hard text, how to test an idea with a thought experiment) and define every term as it appears. The course then ramps up steadily. Chapters 1–8 assume nothing; by Chapters 20–24 you will be reading about the current debates in philosophy of mind, epistemology, political philosophy and continental thought at roughly a graduate-seminar level, with the key papers, books and positions named so you can go further.

## What you need

- **No software** beyond an EPUB reader (Apple Books, Calibre, KOReader, Thorium, Foliate, or any e-reader) and a PDF viewer for the *Tractatus*.
- The **bundled readings**: 148 public-domain books from Project Gutenberg in [readings/](readings/README.md), from the Upanishads, Confucius, Plato and Aristotle to Kant, Mill, Nietzsche, William James and Russell.
- Some **procure-yourself books and articles**: most twentieth-century philosophy is still in copyright, so you will need to buy, borrow or find these through a library. The essentials are Thomas Nagel's *What Does It All Mean?*, Kirk, Raven and Schofield's *The Presocratic Philosophers*, Wittgenstein's *Philosophical Investigations*, Sartre's *Existentialism Is a Humanism*, Beauvoir's *The Second Sex*, Nagel's "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", Gettier's "Is Justified True Belief Knowledge?" and Rawls's *A Theory of Justice*. Each chapter's Required reading block names the edition; [Appendix C](appendix-c-reading-list.md) collects them all.
- A notebook. Many exercises ask you to write, and the last chapter asks you to draft your own philosophy of life.

## How to use this course

1. **Read the chapters in order.** Each builds on the vocabulary and arguments of the ones before, and the course has an arc: from the tools of argument, through the history of Western philosophy with the classical traditions of India and China alongside it, to the main problems of contemporary philosophy, and finally to your own synthesis.
2. **Do the Required reading.** Each chapter opens with a short list: sections of the bundled books (linked directly) and, where needed, procure-yourself books and articles with full details. The chapter quotes and explains the key passages, but nothing replaces reading the originals. Optional "Further reading" follows.
3. **Try the Living it section.** Near the end of every chapter is a concrete practice: a way to test the idea against your own life, or to use it in building your own values. These are tied to the texts, not generic self-help.
4. **Use the Summary** to review before moving on.
5. **Work through the Dialogues.** Each chapter closes with two or three imagined conversations in the tradition of Plato's dialogues. A questioner probes you, or someone holding a view from the chapter; the questioner may be Socrates or another philosopher speaking in character (Confucius, Descartes, Hume, Kant, Nietzsche, Beauvoir, Wittgenstein). The dialogues are invented teaching exercises, not quotations, though they stay true to each thinker's positions. Each one stops at a *Your turn* prompt: write your answer, then open **Continue the dialogue** to see how the conversation might go on.

## How the four courses fit together

This course is the survey in a series of four. The other three are deep dives into territory the survey can only map:

- **[Learn Stoicism](../learn-stoicism/README.md)**: one complete ancient way of life, its physics, logic and ethics, and its afterlife up to modern cognitive therapy.
- **[Learn Ethics](../learn-ethics/README.md)**: the whole field of moral philosophy, from Socrates and Confucius through Kant, Mill and the virtue revival to metaethics, bioethics and AI ethics.
- **[Learn Nihilism](../learn-nihilism/README.md)**: the challenge that nothing matters, from Ecclesiastes and Schopenhauer through Nietzsche, Heidegger and Camus to error theory and antinatalism.

**Suggested reading order.** Start here: the survey gives you the vocabulary, the history and the map. Then take the deep dives in the order **Stoicism, Ethics, Nihilism**. Stoicism shows what a lived philosophy looks like; Ethics gives you the theories and methods for building your own values; Nihilism tests whatever you have built against its strongest objection. Each deep dive also stands on its own.

**Where to branch.** If you would rather branch off as you go:

- after [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md) (Hellenistic philosophy), take the Stoicism course, which expands that chapter's short section on the Stoics into sixteen chapters;
- after [Chapter 16](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md) (utilitarianism and liberalism), take the Ethics course, by which point the survey has introduced Socratic, Aristotelian, Kantian and utilitarian ethics in Chapters 4–6, 13 and 16; Chapter 22 on political philosophy pairs with its chapter on contractualism and justice;
- after [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md) (Nietzsche) or [Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) (phenomenology and existentialism), take the Nihilism course, which treats Schopenhauer, Kierkegaard ([Chapter 14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md) here), Nietzsche, Heidegger, Sartre, Beauvoir and Camus in depth.

Then come back for the rest of the survey. [Chapter 24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md) draws the four courses together.
