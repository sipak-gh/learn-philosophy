# Chapter 9 — Faith and Reason

For roughly a thousand years, from the last days of the Roman Empire to the eve of the Reformation, the most brilliant minds of Europe, North Africa and the Near East worked on one problem from many angles: what can human reason know on its own, what must be received from revelation, and what happens when the two seem to disagree? Christians, Muslims and Jews inherited the same Greek library (Plato through the Neoplatonists, Aristotle through his commentators) and the same scriptural God who creates freely, knows everything and judges. Fitting the two together produced some of the sharpest arguments in the history of philosophy: Augustine's analysis of evil as a lack, Boethius on foreknowledge and eternity, Anselm's ontological argument, Avicenna's distinction between essence and existence, Averroes' defense of philosophy before a religious court, Maimonides' negative theology, Aquinas's Five Ways, and Ockham's razor. You do not need to be religious to profit from this chapter. These thinkers were the first to ask, with full rigor, how a rational person should handle claims that rest on authority and testimony, and how far argument can go before it runs out. Previous: [Chapter 8 — Classical India and China](08-classical-india-and-china.md). Next: [Chapter 10 — The Renaissance and the New Science](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md).

## Athens and Jerusalem: the shape of the problem

Around the year 200 the North African Christian writer Tertullian asked, in effect, what Athens had to do with Jerusalem: what the Academy had to do with the Church (*On the Prescription of Heretics* 7). His implied answer was: nothing at all. Philosophy breeds heresy; the gospel is enough. That is one pole of a spectrum, usually called **fideism** (from *fides*, faith): faith does not need, and may be harmed by, rational support.

The interesting positions lie between that pole and its opposite, reason as sole judge. A map before the tour:

| Position | Slogan | Main representatives |
|----------|--------|----------------------|
| Fideism | Faith needs no reasons | Tertullian (in rhetoric), later Kierkegaard (Chapter 14) |
| Faith seeking understanding | Believe in order to understand | Augustine, Anselm |
| Harmony with a division of labor | Some truths are provable, others revealed; they cannot conflict | Avicenna, Averroes, Maimonides, Aquinas |
| Philosophy for the few | Scripture speaks to everyone in images; demonstration is for the trained | al-Farabi, Averroes, Maimonides (on one reading) |
| Narrowing of natural theology | Reason proves much less than we thought; God's freedom exceeds our logic | al-Ghazali (in one way), Ockham (in another) |

Keep two distinctions in view throughout. First, the **content** question: which particular claims (God exists, the world had a beginning, the soul survives death) can be demonstrated? Second, the **authority** question: when a demonstration and a scriptural text clash, which gives way? Averroes was generous on the first and gave demonstration the upper hand on the second; Ockham thought very little could be demonstrated, so faith carries most of the weight.

## Augustine: the restless heart

Aurelius Augustinus was born in 354 in Thagaste, a small town in what is now Algeria, to a pagan father and a fiercely Christian mother, Monica. A brilliant teacher of rhetoric, he climbed from Carthage to Rome to the imperial court at Milan, spent nine years among the Manichaeans (a dualist sect for which the world is a battle between a good principle of light and an evil principle of darkness), kept a concubine for over a decade, and in 386, in a garden in Milan, converted. He became bishop of Hippo, wrote more than five million words, and died in 430 while the Vandals besieged his city.

The *Confessions* (written about 397–400) is the first great autobiography in the West, and it is a philosophical work disguised as a prayer. It opens with its thesis:

> Thou awakest us to delight in Thy praise; for Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee.
>
> — Augustine, *Confessions* I.1.1 (tr. E. B. Pusey)

The thesis is about desire: we want an unchanging good, so every finite thing we chase disappoints, being the wrong size for the want. When his closest friend died young, Augustine fell apart:

> I became a great riddle to myself, and I asked my soul, why she was so sad, and why she disquieted me sorely: but she knew not what to answer me.
>
> — Augustine, *Confessions* IV.4.9 (tr. E. B. Pusey)

That phrase, "a great riddle to myself" (*mihi magna quaestio*), announces a new philosophical subject: the inner self as a problem to be investigated. Charles Taylor, in *Sources of the Self* (1989), credits Augustine with the "inward turn" that runs through Descartes to modern ideas of selfhood.

### The pears

In Book II Augustine tells a story that seems trivial: at sixteen he and some friends stole pears from a neighbor's tree at night, "not for our eating, but to fling to the very hogs" (II.4.9). Why spend a chapter on it? Because it is a philosophical test case. Greek ethics, from Socrates on, held that people always act for an apparent good: nobody does wrong knowingly (see [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md)). Augustine's theft had no apparent good. He was not hungry; the pears were bad; he threw them away. What he loved, he says, was the wrongness itself:

> It was foul, and I loved it; I loved to perish, I loved mine own fault, not that for which I was faulty, but my fault itself.
>
> — Augustine, *Confessions* II.4.9 (tr. E. B. Pusey)

He then tests hypotheses like a detective (the company? a perverse imitation of God's freedom?). The episode challenges the intellectualist picture of action and makes room for a **will** (*voluntas*) that can turn against what it knows. Scholars such as Albrecht Dihle (*The Theory of Will in Classical Antiquity*, 1982) argue that Augustine invented the concept of the will as a faculty distinct from reason and desire; others, such as Michael Frede (*A Free Will*, 2011), trace it back to the Stoic Epictetus. Either way, Augustine made it central.

### Evil as privation

As a Manichaean, Augustine had thought evil was a substance, a dark stuff opposed to God. From the "books of the Platonists" he read in Milan (VII.9.13; probably Plotinus and Porphyry in Latin) he took the doctrine that evil is not a thing but a lack, a **privation of good** (*privatio boni*). The argument in Book VII can be laid out like this:

1. Everything that exists was made by God, and God makes only good things.
2. Whatever can be corrupted (made worse) has some good in it to lose.
3. If a thing lost all its good, it would cease to exist.
4. So, as long as a thing exists, it is good to some degree.
5. Therefore evil is not a substance; it is a corruption or lack of good in a good thing.

> So long therefore as they are, they are good: therefore whatsoever is, is good. That evil then which I sought, whence it is, is not any substance: for were it a substance, it should be good.
>
> — Augustine, *Confessions* VII.12.18 (tr. E. B. Pusey)

Blindness is not a thing in the eye but the absence of sight where sight should be. The view blocks dualism (there is no rival evil principle) and explains why evil is parasitic: lies need truth, cruelty needs a living victim.

The strongest objection is that it seems to explain away the reality of pain. Torture does not feel like an absence. Defenders reply that the theory concerns metaphysical structure, not how things feel: pain is real, and its *badness* lies in the disorder it involves (see Todd Calder, "Is the Privation Theory of Evil Dead?", *American Philosophical Quarterly* 44, 2007).

If evil is a lack, what causes it? Augustine's answer in *The City of God* is that the bad will has no efficient cause at all:

> Let no one, therefore, look for an efficient cause of the evil will; for it is not efficient, but deficient, as the will itself is not an effecting of something, but a defect. … Now, to seek to discover the causes of these defections,—causes, as I have said, not efficient, but deficient,—is as if some one sought to see darkness, or hear silence.
>
> — Augustine, *The City of God* XII.7 (tr. Marcus Dods)

The question "why did the first sinner choose badly?" is declared unanswerable in principle. This is either deep or evasive; decide which.

### "If I am deceived, I am"

Twelve centuries before Descartes, Augustine answered the Academic skeptics (see [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) with an argument that will look very familiar in [Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md):

> For if I am deceived, I am. For he who is not, cannot be deceived; and if I am deceived, by this same token I am.
>
> — Augustine, *The City of God* XI.26 (tr. Marcus Dods)

*Si fallor, sum.* Descartes' contemporaries noticed the parallel at once (Antoine Arnauld pointed it out in the Fourth Objections), and Descartes replied that he used the point for a different purpose: to build a whole system of knowledge on it, not just to rebut skeptics.

### What is time?

Book XI of the *Confessions* is the most-cited text on time before Kant. It begins with an admission every philosopher should frame:

> What then is time? If no one asks me, I know: if I wish to explain it to one that asketh, I know not.
>
> — Augustine, *Confessions* XI.14.17 (tr. E. B. Pusey)

The puzzle: the past no longer exists, the future does not yet exist, and the present has no duration. So how can time be measured? Augustine's answer is that time is a "distension of the mind" (*distentio animi*): past and future exist as present memory and present expectation. This is the ancestor of mind-dependent theories of time from Kant's "form of inner sense" to Husserl, against Aristotle's time as "the number of motion."

### Two cities

When Alaric's Goths sacked Rome in 410, pagans blamed the Christians for abandoning the old gods. Augustine's reply grew into the twenty-two books of *The City of God*, whose organizing image is this:

> Accordingly, two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self.
>
> — Augustine, *The City of God* XIV.28 (tr. Marcus Dods)

The two cities are not church and state but two orientations of love, mixed in every institution until the end of history. The political lesson is sober realism: expect no salvation from politics. Reinhold Niebuhr and Hannah Arendt (whose dissertation was on Augustine's concept of love) both drew on it. In a sermon Augustine summed up his method as *crede, ut intellegas*, believe so that you may understand (*Sermon* 43): as a student trusts a teacher before seeing why the theorem holds.

## Boethius: philosophy in a prison cell

Anicius Manlius Severinus Boethius (c. 477–524) was a Roman aristocrat serving the Ostrogothic king Theodoric. He planned to translate all of Plato and Aristotle into Latin; he finished only Aristotle's logic, which was most of what the Latin West knew of Aristotle for six centuries. His commentary on Porphyry's *Isagoge* posed the **problem of universals** (do genera and species exist in reality or only in the mind?) that medieval philosophers argued about until Ockham.

Then he fell. Accused of treason and magic, he was imprisoned near Pavia and executed. While waiting, he wrote *The Consolation of Philosophy*, alternating prose and verse. It opens with Boethius in bed, dictating self-pitying poems with the Muses. A woman of uncertain height appears, her robe embroidered with the Greek letters Pi and Theta (practical and theoretical philosophy) and torn by sects that each grabbed a piece. She is Philosophy, and she is not pleased:

> 'Who,' said she, 'has allowed yon play-acting wantons to approach this sick man—these who, so far from giving medicine to heal his malady, even feed it with sweet poison?'
>
> — Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy* I, prose 1 (tr. H. R. James)

Philosophy here is medicine, a Hellenistic idea (compare [the Stoicism course, Chapter 7](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md)), and its drug is argument. Strikingly, in a work by a Christian facing death, Christ is never mentioned: the consolation comes from reason alone.

### Fortune's wheel

In Book II Philosophy speaks in the voice of Fortune, and the speech became one of the most copied images in medieval art:

> This is my art, this the game I never cease to play. I turn the wheel that spins. I delight to see the high come down and the low ascend. Mount up, if thou wilt, but only on condition that thou wilt not think it a hardship to come down when the rules of my game require it.
>
> — Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy* II, prose 2 (tr. H. R. James)

Fortune's gifts (wealth, office, fame) are by nature on loan, so their loss is no injustice: if you accepted the ride up, you accepted the ride down. Philosophy goes further: fame, for instance, measured against eternity, "seems not short-lived merely, but altogether nothing" (II, prose 7). True happiness must be something that cannot be lost against your will; Books III–IV argue that this is God, the supreme good.

### Foreknowledge and freedom

Book V raises the problem that has never left the philosophy of religion:

> 'It seems,' said I, 'too much of a paradox and a contradiction that God should know all things, and yet there should be free will. For if God foresees everything, and can in no wise be deceived, that which providence foresees to be about to happen must necessarily come to pass.'
>
> — Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy* V, prose 3 (tr. H. R. James)

In premise form:

1. God infallibly knows today that you will do X tomorrow.
2. If God infallibly knows that you will do X, it is necessary that you do X.
3. If it is necessary that you do X, you do not do X freely.
4. So you do not do X freely.

Boethius answers in two steps. First, premise 2 is ambiguous. It is necessary that *if* God knows you will do X, you will do X (necessity of the consequence, *necessitas consequentiae*); it does not follow that your doing X is itself necessary (*necessitas consequentis*). Second, and more original: God is not in time, so God does not *fore*know anything. Philosophy defines eternity this way:

> Now, eternity is the possession of endless life whole and perfect at a single moment.
>
> — Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy* V, prose 6 (tr. H. R. James)

God sees all of time as we see the present. And seeing something happen does not make it happen:

> For even as ye, when at one and the same time ye see a man walking on the earth and the sun rising in the sky, distinguish between the two, though one glance embraces both, and judge the former voluntary, the latter necessary action: so also the Divine vision in its universal range of view does in no wise confuse the characters of the things which are present to its regard, though future in respect of time.
>
> — Boethius, *The Consolation of Philosophy* V, prose 6 (tr. H. R. James)

Nelson Pike ("Divine Omniscience and Voluntary Action," *Philosophical Review* 74, 1965) restated the dilemma using the fixity of the past: you cannot now make God's belief of yesterday false. A timeless God has no yesterday, so Boethius escapes; but critics ask whether a timeless being can know what time it is now, or act in history. Linda Zagzebski's *The Dilemma of Freedom and Foreknowledge* (1991) maps the options.

## Anselm: that than which nothing greater can be conceived

Anselm of Aosta (1033–1109), a monk at Bec in Normandy and later Archbishop of Canterbury, took Augustine's motto as a program. He first titled the *Proslogion* "Faith Seeking Understanding" (*fides quaerens intellectum*), and its opening chapter ends:

> For I do not seek to understand that I may believe, but I believe in order to understand. For this also I believe,—that unless I believed, I should not understand.
>
> — Anselm, *Proslogion* 1 (tr. S. N. Deane, modernized text of the Medieval Sourcebook)

He wanted a single argument that would need nothing but itself to prove that God exists. What he found, in chapter 2, has been called the **ontological argument** since Kant gave it that name. It starts from the Psalm verse "The fool has said in his heart, There is no God" and asks what the fool understands when he hears the word "God":

> Hence, even the fool is convinced that something exists in the understanding, at least, than which nothing greater can be conceived. For, when he hears of this, he understands it. And whatever is understood, exists in the understanding. And assuredly that, than which nothing greater can be conceived, cannot exist in the understanding alone. For, suppose it exists in the understanding alone: then it can be conceived to exist in reality; which is greater.
>
> — Anselm, *Proslogion* 2 (tr. S. N. Deane, modernized text of the Medieval Sourcebook)

Laid out:

1. "God" means "a being than which nothing greater can be conceived" (call it G).
2. Even the fool understands this phrase, so G exists in the understanding.
3. Suppose G exists only in the understanding.
4. Then we can conceive G existing in reality as well, which would be greater.
5. Then we can conceive something greater than G, which contradicts (1).
6. So G exists in reality as well as in the understanding.

It is a *reductio ad absurdum* ([Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md)) with no premise about the world. Chapter 3 adds a modal version (G cannot even be *conceived* not to exist), revived by Norman Malcolm (1960) and Alvin Plantinga (*The Nature of Necessity*, 1974): if a maximally great being is so much as possible, one exists.

### The lost island

The first critic, the monk Gaunilo of Marmoutiers, wrote a *Reply on Behalf of the Fool* with a parody: by the same reasoning you could prove that an island more excellent than all others exists. Anselm (who asked that Gaunilo's reply be copied with his book, an admirable act of fairness) answered that the argument applies only to that than which nothing greater can be conceived, not to the greatest of a kind. There is no intrinsic maximum of island-excellence (you can always add a palm tree), whereas knowledge and power have intrinsic maxima. Leibniz will add that the argument works only if a perfect being is possible, which must be shown.

### Aquinas's objection, and Kant's

Two centuries later Thomas Aquinas rejected the argument, not because it is invalid but because it does not move from concept to reality:

> Yet, granted that everyone understands that by this word "God" is signified something than which nothing greater can be thought, nevertheless, it does not therefore follow that he understands that what the word signifies exists actually, but only that it exists mentally.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 2, a. 1, reply to obj. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

God's existence is self-evident *in itself* but not *to us*, since we do not grasp God's essence. Kant's objection is different: existence is not a real predicate, so adding it to a concept makes nothing greater ([Chapter 13](13-kant.md)). Graham Oppy's *Ontological Arguments and Belief in God* (1995) guides the literature.

## The philosophers of Islam: *falsafa*

While Latin Europe had Boethius's logic and little else, the Abbasid caliphs in Baghdad sponsored a translation movement (eighth to tenth centuries) that put almost all of Aristotle, much of Galen, and Neoplatonic texts into Arabic. The result was *falsafa* (from Greek *philosophia*), practiced alongside *kalām*, rational theology done by Muslim theologians. Al-Farabi (d. 950), "the Second Teacher" after Aristotle, held that religion presents in images what philosophy knows in concepts, and that the ideal ruler is philosopher and prophet at once.

### Avicenna

Abu Ali al-Husayn ibn Sina (980–1037), Latinized as Avicenna, was the most influential philosopher of the medieval world after Aristotle himself. By his own account he mastered medicine as a teenager and read Aristotle's *Metaphysics* forty times without understanding it, until a cheap secondhand copy of al-Farabi's little treatise on its aims, bought in a book market, made it click; he gave alms in thanks. His *Canon of Medicine* was taught in Europe into the seventeenth century.

Three Avicennian ideas shaped everything after.

**Essence and existence.** You know *what* a phoenix is (its essence, *māhiyya*, "whatness") without knowing *whether* one exists. So existence is not part of the essence of any ordinary thing; such a thing is **possible in itself** (*mumkin al-wujūd*), and if it exists, it gets its existence from a cause.

**The Necessary Existent.** From this he built a proof that starts from existence itself, not from physics:

1. Something exists.
2. Whatever exists is either necessary in itself or possible in itself.
3. If something is possible in itself, its existence must be explained by a cause outside it.
4. The whole collection of possible things is itself possible, so it too needs a cause outside it.
5. A cause outside the collection of all possible things cannot be possible.
6. So there exists a being necessary in itself (*wājib al-wujūd*), whose essence just is its existence.

Step 4 means the argument need not rule out an infinite chain: even an infinite collection of contingent beings needs explaining. The classic objection is Hume's ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)): explain each member of a collection and you have explained the collection.

**The flying man.** In the *De Anima* of his encyclopedia *The Healing* (*al-Shifāʾ*), Avicenna asks you to imagine a person created all at once, fully mature, floating in empty air, eyes covered, limbs spread so that they touch nothing, not even each other. Such a person would affirm that he exists without affirming that he has a body, so (Avicenna concludes) the self is not the body. (This is a paraphrase.) The parallel with Descartes is striking, and so is the gap: from "I can know I exist without knowing I have a body" it does not follow that "I am not a body."

The *Compendium on the Soul* bundled with this course is a short early treatise. Its last section contains one of the clearest medieval statements of the problem of induction, used to argue that our certainty about first principles cannot come from experience:

> Indeed experience, although it shows us that every animal we perceive moves on chewing the lower jaw, yet it does not supply us with a convincing judgment that such is the case with every animal; for were this true, it would not be licit for the crocodile to exist which moves his upper jaw on chewing.
>
> — Avicenna, *A Compendium on the Soul*, Section Tenth (tr. Edward Abbott van Dyck)

Since we are certain that the whole is greater than its part, and since no number of observations could give that certainty, the certainty must come from a "godly outflow" from a separate intellect, the **Active Intellect** that "stands towards the rational soul in the stead of light to sight" (same section). Hume will draw the opposite conclusion from the same crocodile-shaped gap.

### Al-Ghazali's counterattack

Abu Hamid al-Ghazali (1058–1111), the most famous professor in Baghdad, suffered a crisis in 1095 that left him unable to speak, and became a wandering Sufi. His *Incoherence of the Philosophers* (*Tahāfut al-falāsifa*) used the philosophers' own logic against them. He charged them with unbelief on three points: that the world is eternal, that God knows particulars only "in a universal way," and that there is no bodily resurrection.

His most famous argument, in the Seventeenth Discussion, concerns causation. When cotton touches fire and burns, we observe only that the burning happens *with* the contact, not *through* it. Nothing in the observation shows a necessary connection. God could create the contact without the burning, as in the story of Abraham thrown into the fire unharmed. The regularity we call "nature" is God's habit (*ʿāda*), not a necessity in things. This is **occasionalism**, and its epistemological core (we never perceive necessary connection) reappears almost exactly in Hume, though Hume draws a skeptical moral where al-Ghazali drew a theological one.

### Averroes and the *Decisive Treatise*

Abu al-Walid Muhammad ibn Rushd (1126–1198), Latinized as Averroes, was a judge (*qāḍī*) in Seville and Córdoba and physician to the Almohad caliph. His commentaries on Aristotle earned him the Latin title "the Commentator," and he answered al-Ghazali in *The Incoherence of the Incoherence*.

His *Decisive Treatise* is framed as a legal ruling (*fatwā*): does the religious Law forbid philosophy, permit it, or command it? His verdict is that for those capable of it, philosophy is obligatory, because the Qurʾān commands people to reflect on creation, and demonstration is the best kind of reflection. Then comes the sentence that sums up the harmony position:

> As this Law is true and leads to the consideration of the knowledge of God, we Muslims should believe that rational investigation is not contrary to Law, for truth cannot contradict truth, but verifies it and bears testimony to it.
>
> — Averroes, *Decisive Discourse* (tr. Mohammad Jamil-ur-Rehman)

What if a demonstrated conclusion conflicts with the literal sense of scripture? Then the scriptural passage must be interpreted (*taʾwīl*) non-literally, as Arabic usage permits. And what about the danger that philosophy misleads people? Averroes answers with an image a judge would love:

> We would even say that a man who prevents another fit for it, from studying the books of philosophy, because certain worthless people have been misled by them, is like a man who refused a thirsty man cold and sweet water, till he died, because some people under the same circumstances have been suffocated by it and have died.
>
> — Averroes, *Decisive Discourse* (tr. Mohammad Jamil-ur-Rehman)

The qualification "fit for it" matters. Averroes divides people into those persuaded by rhetoric (the majority), by dialectic (the theologians), and by demonstration (the philosophers). Scripture speaks to all three at once, and allegorical readings should not be published to the masses. Elitist? Openly. Dishonest? Averroes would say no: the images are true, as a good analogy is true. His Latin followers (Siger of Brabant at Paris) were accused of a **double truth**, true in philosophy and false in faith, and in 1277 the Bishop of Paris condemned 219 propositions. Modern scholars (Richard C. Taylor) doubt anyone held such a doctrine, and Averroes rejected it outright.

## Maimonides: a guide for the perplexed

Moses ben Maimon (1138–1204) was born in Córdoba, fled the Almohad persecution with his family, and settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), where he became the leader of the Jewish community and a court physician. *The Guide for the Perplexed* (in Arabic, *Dalālat al-ḥāʾirīn*, about 1190) is written for a believing Jew who has studied philosophy and is perplexed because the literal sense of scripture seems to contradict reason.

### Negative theology

Scripture speaks of God's hand and God's anger; Maimonides spends dozens of chapters showing such terms are figurative. Then he goes further: even "wise" and "powerful" cannot apply to God as they apply to us, because God is absolutely one and simple.

> Know that the negative attributes of God are the true attributes: they do not include any incorrect notions or any deficiency whatever in reference to God, while positive attributes imply polytheism, and are inadequate, as we have already shown.
>
> — Maimonides, *The Guide for the Perplexed* I.58 (tr. M. Friedländer)

"Living" means not like the dead; each negation removes a wrong picture, as a sculptor reveals a figure by cutting stone away. The endpoint is silence:

> The idea is best expressed in the book of Psalms, "Silence is praise to Thee" (lxv. 2). It is a very expressive remark on this subject; for whatever we utter with the intention of extolling and of praising Him, contains something that cannot be applied to God, and includes derogatory expressions.
>
> — Maimonides, *The Guide for the Perplexed* I.59 (tr. M. Friedländer)

This **apophatic** theology (from Greek *apophasis*, denial) runs from the Neoplatonists to today. Aquinas replied (*Summa* I, q. 13) that terms apply to God by **analogy**: "good" in God and in Socrates is neither simply the same nor simply different.

### Was the world created?

Aristotle held that the world is eternal; scripture says it was created. Maimonides argues that neither side has a demonstration (Aristotle shows only that the world *as it now runs* cannot have arisen naturally), and says plainly that scripture would not stop him if a proof existed:

> We do not reject the Eternity of the Universe, because certain passages in Scripture confirm the Creation; for such passages are not more numerous than those in which God is represented as a corporeal being; nor is it impossible or difficult to find for them a suitable interpretation.
>
> — Maimonides, *The Guide for the Perplexed* II.25 (tr. M. Friedländer)

Why then accept creation? Because reason leaves the question open, and Aristotle's necessary emanation would leave no room for a God who chooses, for miracles or prophecy. Faith decides where reason is silent. Aquinas adopted the same conclusion in *On the Eternity of the World*.

## Aquinas: the great synthesis

Thomas Aquinas (1225–1274) was born into a noble family near Aquino, between Rome and Naples. When he joined the Dominicans, a new mendicant order, his brothers kidnapped him and held him in the family castle for about a year. Large and quiet, he was nicknamed "the dumb ox"; his teacher Albert the Great reportedly said the ox's bellowing would be heard throughout the world. In December 1273, after an experience during Mass, he stopped writing; his secretary Reginald reported him saying that all he had written seemed like straw. He died three months later, the *Summa Theologica* unfinished.

### How to read an article

The *Summa* is built from **articles**, each a miniature disputation, and the form is worth learning because you can use it yourself:

1. **The question**, phrased as a yes/no: "Whether God exists?"
2. **Objections** (*videtur quod*): the best arguments for the answer Aquinas will reject, stated fairly.
3. **On the contrary** (*sed contra*): usually an authority pointing the other way.
4. **I answer that** (*respondeo*): Aquinas's own argument.
5. **Replies to the objections**, one by one.

Most writers bury their opponents' arguments. Aquinas puts them first. The first objection to the existence of God, for example, is the problem of evil, stated in a single brutal paragraph:

> It seems that God does not exist; because if one of two contraries be infinite, the other would be altogether destroyed. But the word "God" means that He is infinite goodness. If, therefore, God existed, there would be no evil discoverable; but there is evil in the world. Therefore God does not exist.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 2, a. 3, obj. 1 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

### The division of labor

Aquinas distinguishes truths about God that reason can reach (that God exists, is one, is good) from truths that exceed reason (that God is a Trinity, that God became incarnate). The first are **preambles** to faith:

> The existence of God and other like truths about God, which can be known by natural reason, are not articles of faith, but are preambles to the articles; for faith presupposes natural knowledge, even as grace presupposes nature, and perfection supposes something that can be perfected.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 2, a. 2, reply to obj. 1 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Since "the contrary of a truth can never be demonstrated," arguments against faith "are difficulties that can be answered" (I, q. 1, a. 8). Compare Averroes: both hold that truth is one, but for Averroes a demonstration forces reinterpretation of scripture, while for Aquinas an argument against faith must contain an error, even if we cannot yet find it. The relation is summed up in a phrase:

> Since therefore grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, natural reason should minister to faith as the natural bent of the will ministers to charity.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 1, a. 8, reply to obj. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

### The Five Ways

Article 3 of Question 2 contains the most famous five paragraphs in natural theology. Each starts from an observed feature of the world and argues to a first cause. The first is the argument from motion, taken from Aristotle's *Physics* VIII and *Metaphysics* XII ([Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md)):

> The first and more manifest way is the argument from motion. It is certain, and evident to our senses, that in the world some things are in motion. Now whatever is in motion is put in motion by another, for nothing can be in motion except it is in potentiality to that towards which it is in motion; whereas a thing moves inasmuch as it is in act.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 2, a. 3 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

"Motion" (*motus*) means any change from potentiality to actuality. The argument concludes:

> Therefore it is necessary to arrive at a first mover, put in motion by no other; and this everyone understands to be God.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 2, a. 3 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

The five are: (1) from motion, (2) from efficient causation, (3) from possibility and necessity (contingency), (4) from degrees of perfection, (5) from the order of nature (teleology, or "governance"). The third is closest to Avicenna:

> We find in nature things that are possible to be and not to be, since they are found to be generated, and to corrupt, and consequently, they are possible to be and not to be. But it is impossible for these always to exist, for that which is possible not to be at some time is not. Therefore, if everything is possible not to be, then at one time there could have been nothing in existence.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I, q. 2, a. 3 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

### Objections to the Five Ways

Three objections are standard; you should be able to state all three and the best replies.

**The infinite regress.** Why can't the movers go back forever? Aquinas granted that an infinite past could not be disproved. But a **per accidens** series (grandfather, father, son) differs from a **per se** series, where each member acts *now* only because the one before acts now: the hand moves the stick that moves the stone. Without a first member, nothing in a per se chain acts at all. The First Way concerns per se series (see Edward Feser, *Aquinas*, 2009).

**The quantifier shift in the Third Way.** From "each contingent thing at some time does not exist" Aquinas seems to move to "at some time, every contingent thing fails to exist." That is invalid, like moving from "everyone has a mother" to "someone is everyone's mother." Kenny (*The Five Ways*, 1969) pressed this. Defenders invoke a principle of plenitude inherited from Aristotle (in infinite time every possibility is realized), which is itself doubtful.

**The gap at the end.** Why is a first mover the God of Abraham rather than an impersonal principle? "This everyone understands to be God" is a sentence, not an argument. Aquinas's answer is that Questions 3–26 argue that a first cause must be simple, perfect, one, knowing and willing: the Five Ways are only the first step.

### Natural law

Aquinas's ethics applies the same structure to action. Just as the speculative intellect has first principles (a thing cannot both be and not be), the practical intellect has one too:

> Hence this is the first precept of law, that "good is to be done and pursued, and evil is to be avoided." All other precepts of the natural law are based upon this.
>
> — Thomas Aquinas, *Summa Theologica* I-II, q. 94, a. 2 (tr. Fathers of the English Dominican Province)

Reason grasps the objects of our natural inclinations (life, offspring, truth, society) as goods. This is **natural law** theory, treated in depth in [the Ethics course, Chapter 4](../learn-ethics/04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md).

## Ockham and the unraveling

William of Ockham (c. 1287–1347), an English Franciscan, was summoned to the papal court at Avignon on suspicion of heresy, concluded that the pope was the heretic, fled in 1328, was excommunicated, and spent his last twenty years under the emperor's protection in Munich.

### The razor

Ockham's name is attached to the principle of parsimony: do not multiply entities beyond necessity. That exact formula (*entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem*) does not appear in his writings; it seems to come from the seventeenth-century editor John Punch. What Ockham did write, repeatedly, is that "plurality must never be posited without necessity" (*numquam ponenda est pluralitas sine necessitate*), and he used the principle relentlessly. He did not invent the principle, but he made it a weapon.

### Nominalism

Most scholastics held that individuals of a kind share a real **common nature** (Socrates and Plato share humanity). Ockham argued that everything that exists is individual; universals are signs, natural concepts in the mind that signify many individuals. This is **nominalism** (from *nomen*, name), more precisely **conceptualism**. One argument: if humanity were one thing really in Socrates and Plato, destroying Socrates would destroy something in Plato. The payoff is an economical ontology of individual substances and qualities only. This is the ancestor of empiricist economy in Locke, Berkeley and Hume ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)) and of twentieth-century nominalism in Quine and Goodman ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)).

### God's absolute power

Ockham separated God's **ordained power** (*potentia ordinata*), what God has chosen to do, from God's **absolute power** (*potentia absoluta*), whatever involves no contradiction. Since cause and effect are distinct things, God could produce any effect without its usual cause, even an intuitive cognition of a star that does not exist (on what follows, see Marilyn McCord Adams, *William Ockham*, 1987). Two consequences: natural necessity becomes contingent on God's will, which, some historians argue, favors an empirical science that must look to see what God actually did; and natural theology shrinks. Reason can show a first conserving cause, Ockham thought, but not strictly that there is only one God or that the soul is immortal. Those must be believed. Historians disagree about how much this mattered. Hans Blumenberg (*The Legitimacy of the Modern Age*, 1966) and Michael Allen Gillespie (*The Theological Origins of Modernity*, 2008) see late medieval nominalism and its omnipotent, unpredictable God as the crisis to which modern philosophy, from Descartes on, was a response. Others see this as too neat a story. You will meet the modern side of it in the next two chapters.

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Is "medieval philosophy" philosophy?** In 1931 Émile Bréhier argued that "Christian philosophy" was a contradiction, like "Christian mathematics." Étienne Gilson replied in *The Spirit of Mediaeval Philosophy* (1932) that revelation had generated new philosophical ideas (creation from nothing, the person, the will) that reason then made its own.

**How to read the authors.** Leo Strauss (*Persecution and the Art of Writing*, 1952) argued that al-Farabi, Averroes and Maimonides wrote esoterically, hiding views closer to Aristotle beneath a pious surface; Maimonides himself warns in his Introduction that he scattered his views on purpose. Herbert Davidson and Kenneth Seeskin read the *Guide* as meaning what it says about creation.

**Did philosophy die in Islam after al-Ghazali?** Older histories said so. Recent scholarship (Robert Wisnovsky, Frank Griffel, Peter Adamson) shows that post-Avicennian philosophy flourished for centuries in the East, down to Mulla Sadra (d. 1640). The "decline" was largely an artifact of following only the texts translated into Latin.

## Living it

The medieval thinkers left you three practices that do not depend on sharing their faith.

**Write one article.** Take a belief you care about and write it as a scholastic article: the yes/no question; three objections stated so well that their holders would sign them; an authority on the other side; your answer; a reply to each objection. If you cannot write the objections, you do not yet understand your own position.

**Run Augustine's pear test.** Choose something you did this month that you cannot quite justify. Propose explanations and test them, as Augustine did: the thing itself, the company, the thrill of breaking a rule? If some of your actions aim at no good you would defend, the will, and not only knowledge, belongs in your ethics.

**Take Fortune's inventory.** Boethius's Fortune says she only lends. List what could be taken from you without any act of yours, then what could not. The aim is not Stoic detachment (for that stronger claim see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 6](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md)) but clarity about what you stake your happiness on. Test Boethius's claim, that a happiness you can lose against your will is not yet the one you want, against your list.

## Summary

- The medieval problem: how reason relates to revelation, from fideism through "faith seeking understanding" and harmony models to a narrowed natural theology.
- Augustine: the restless heart; the will as a power that can choose against the good (the pears); evil as privation of good; *si fallor, sum*; time as a distension of the mind; two cities formed by two loves; believe in order to understand.
- Boethius: philosophy as consolation; Fortune's wheel; the foreknowledge dilemma solved by distinguishing conditional from absolute necessity and by a timeless God who sees all time at once.
- Anselm's ontological argument defines God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived and argues by *reductio*; Gaunilo's island parody, Aquinas's concept/reality objection and Kant's "existence is not a predicate" are the main replies.
- Avicenna: essence versus existence, the Necessary Existent, the flying man. Al-Ghazali: no perceived necessary connection between causes and effects. Averroes: philosophy is obligatory for the capable; truth cannot contradict truth.
- Maimonides: only negative attributes apply to God; creation is accepted because eternity is undemonstrated and would undercut the Law.
- Aquinas: preambles of faith versus articles of faith; grace perfects nature; the Five Ways (with the per se series, quantifier-shift and "gap" objections); the first precept of natural law.
- Ockham: parsimony, nominalism, God's absolute power, and a much smaller natural theology, setting the stage for modernity.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether losing your job is a misfortune

**Lady Philosophy:** You look like Boethius did when I found him: lying in bed, composing complaints. What happened?

**You:** I was laid off. Eight years at the company, and they cut my whole team in an afternoon.

*Your turn: answer Philosophy's next question before opening the continuation. She will ask whether you have been wronged. Have you? Write three or four sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Yes, I have been wronged. I worked hard and was loyal, and they threw me away.

**Lady Philosophy:** Did they promise to keep you forever?

**You:** No. But there was an understanding.

**Lady Philosophy:** An understanding with whom? With the company, or with Fortune? When you took the job, you knew that markets change and companies cut costs. You took the ride up the wheel: the salary, the title, the sense of belonging. Did you think it a hardship then?

**You:** Of course not. But that is no argument. If someone lends me a house and then evicts me in a blizzard, I am still harmed, even if the house was only lent.

**Lady Philosophy:** Good: you distinguish being *harmed* from being *wronged*. Keep those apart. Were you wronged? Perhaps, if they lied or broke a contract. Were you harmed? Let us see. What exactly have you lost?

**You:** Income. Status. Colleagues. The feeling of being needed.

**Lady Philosophy:** And which of these did you own, in the sense that no one could take it without your consent?

**You:** None of them, I suppose. But that just means everything I valued was fragile.

**Lady Philosophy:** That is my point, and you have made it yourself. You asked happiness of things that can be taken away. Can a happiness that is always at risk of being taken away be the happiness you want?

**You:** Maybe not the *highest* happiness. But I am not a saint. I want income and colleagues, and losing them is bad. Boethius had a wife and sons and friends; you yourself reminded him of them, in Book II, as goods he still had.

**Lady Philosophy:** Well read. I did. I told him he was not as unfortunate as he claimed, because his best possessions were safe. I did not say they were worthless.

**You:** Then which is it? Are Fortune's gifts worthless, or are they goods you are lucky to keep?

**Lady Philosophy:** Here my teaching has two stages, and you are right to notice the tension. At first I treat you with gentle medicine: I count your remaining blessings. Later, in Book III, I give the stronger drug: that the supreme good is one, and is God, and that the gifts of Fortune are at best shadows of it.

**You:** The stronger drug only works if I believe in that supreme good. If I don't?

**Lady Philosophy:** Then you are left with this much, which still holds: the loss is real but not a wrong done by the world to you; it is the world being what it always was. And what remains in your power, your judgment, your character, your care for those you love, was never on the wheel.

**You:** So the refined position is: I have been harmed, perhaps not wronged; the loss is real but expected; and my sense of disaster comes partly from having forgotten that the goods were on loan.

**Lady Philosophy:** That is a good place to stop for today. Notice what you did not settle: whether there is a good that cannot be lost at all. Boethius thought there was. The Stoics thought it was virtue; the Christians, God. You will have to decide.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether the First Way proves God

**Thomas Aquinas:** You say no argument can prove that God exists. Let us test that with the simplest one. Do things in the world change?

**You:** Obviously.

*Your turn: Aquinas will now argue that whatever changes is changed by another. Do you accept that? What will you object? Write three or four sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I accept that changes have causes. But the chain of causes could go back forever. The Big Bang might have been caused by something earlier, and so on.

**Thomas Aquinas:** You are thinking of a series in time: grandfather, father, son. I agree that such a series might, for all reason can show, be infinite. I said so myself. My First Way is about another kind of series. When a hand moves a stick that moves a stone, does the stick move the stone by its own power?

**You:** No. It moves the stone only while the hand moves it.

**Thomas Aquinas:** And if there were a stick moved by a stick moved by a stick, without end, and no hand?

**You:** Then nothing would move. Each stick only passes on motion it receives. Infinitely many conduits with no source give you no flow.

**Thomas Aquinas:** That is the argument. In an essentially ordered series, each member acts here and now only because another acts on it here and now. Such a series needs a first member that acts without being acted on: something that is pure act, with no unrealized potential.

**You:** But why think anything in the world is in such a series? Physics explains the stone's motion by forces and fields, not by a chain of movers.

**Thomas Aquinas:** Does a field exist by its own nature, or because something keeps it in being? Ask not what started things, but what holds them in existence and in operation now.

**You:** That sounds like Avicenna. Everything whose essence does not include existence needs a cause of its existence now.

**Thomas Aquinas:** I learned much from him. Do you accept the premise?

**You:** I'm not sure. It assumes that existence needs explaining, that brute facts are not acceptable. Hume would say the universe might just be there, without a reason.

**Thomas Aquinas:** Then the disagreement is not about my logic but about the principle of sufficient reason. Would you accept a brute fact in a laboratory? If a substance appeared in your flask, would you be content to say, "It just exists"?

**You:** In a laboratory, no. But the universe as a whole might be different.

**Thomas Aquinas:** Why would the largest thing be exempt from the rule you apply to every part of it?

**You:** Because the rule was learned from parts. That is Hume's point too: we have no experience of universes being caused. And even if you get a first mover, you write "and this everyone understands to be God." That is not an argument.

**Thomas Aquinas:** Agreed, it is not. It is a promissory note. I spend twenty-four further questions paying it, arguing that pure act must be simple, perfect, one, intelligent. You may reject those arguments, but judge them before you call the debt unpaid.

**You:** Then here is where we are. If the principle of sufficient reason holds for existence, and if there are essentially ordered series, the First Way gives a first cause that is pure act. Whether that is God depends on later arguments. And whether the principle holds is the real crux.

**Thomas Aquinas:** That is a fair statement. Most who reject my argument have never located the crux so exactly. Go and read Hume, and then come back.
