# Chapter 4 — Socrates and the Sophists

In 399 BCE an Athenian jury of 501 citizens heard the case against a seventy-year-old stonemason's son who had never written a book, held no office, and spent his days asking people questions in the marketplace. They found him guilty of impiety and of corrupting the young, and sentenced him to death. His student Plato spent the next fifty years writing dialogues in which this man, Socrates, is the hero, and Western philosophy has looked back to him ever since as a founder and a martyr. Socrates did not appear from nowhere. He worked alongside the **sophists**, the first professional teachers in Greece, who sold training in argument and public speaking to ambitious young men and raised unsettling questions about truth, justice and the gods. To the Athenian public Socrates looked like one of them. This chapter asks what the sophists taught, what Socrates did differently, and why it got him killed. It reads closely the three short dialogues set around his trial and death: the *Euthyphro*, the *Apology* and the *Crito*.

## Athens and the market for words

Fifth-century Athens was a direct democracy. Several thousand male citizens met in the Assembly to decide on war, taxes and treaties by majority vote. Any citizen could propose a motion, and anyone sued in the courts had to plead his own case before juries of hundreds, selected by lot. There were no professional lawyers or politicians in our sense. Power belonged to whoever could persuade a crowd.

That created a market, and the sophists supplied it. The word *sophistês* first meant simply "expert" or "wise man." By the 430s it named a class of traveling teachers, most of them not Athenian, who charged high fees to teach *aretê*. The word is usually translated "virtue," but in this context it means something like "excellence" or "success": the ability to manage your household and your city well, and above all to speak persuasively. Protagoras puts the promise plainly in Plato's dialogue. A student who comes to him

> will learn that which he comes to learn. And this is prudence in affairs private as well as public; he will learn to order his own house in the best manner, and he will be able to speak and act for the best in the affairs of the state.
>
> — Plato, *Protagoras* 318e–319a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Socrates asks whether that means he teaches the art of politics and promises to make men good citizens. "That, Socrates, is exactly the profession which I make."

The sophists were brilliant, varied and, for their critics, scandalous. They took fees, which to an aristocratic sensibility made them tradesmen. They taught young men to argue both sides of a case, which looked like teaching them to lie. And many of them drew skeptical or relativist conclusions from the fact that different peoples have different customs. Xenophon has Socrates put the charge in its harshest form:

> he who sells it for money to the first bidder we name a sophist, … as though one should say a man who prostitutes his wisdom
>
> — Xenophon, *Memorabilia* 1.6.13 (tr. H. G. Dakyns)

Our negative word "sophistry" comes from Plato and Aristotle, who won the argument about the sophists' reputation. In the nineteenth century the historian George Grote and the philosopher Hegel began to rehabilitate them, as the Greek Enlightenment: the first thinkers to make human society, language and knowledge the objects of critical inquiry. Most scholars now hold some version of that view.

### Protagoras: man is the measure

Protagoras was the first and most famous of them, and Pericles trusted him to write the laws for a new Athenian colony. Diogenes Laertius preserves the opening of two of his books:

> "Man is the measure of all things: of those things which exist as he is; and of those things which do not exist as he is not." … "Concerning the Gods, I am not able to know to a certainty whether they exist or whether they do not. For there are many things which prevent one from knowing, especially the obscurity of the subject, and the shortness of the life of man."
>
> — Diogenes Laertius 9.51 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The second sentence is often called the earliest recorded statement of agnosticism, and according to Diogenes the Athenians burned his books for it. The first is harder to interpret. In the *Theaetetus* Socrates reads it as a doctrine about perception: the same wind feels cold to one person and warm to another, and there is no fact about whether it is "really" cold. Things "are to you such as they appear to you, and to me such as they appear to me" (152a). Extend that from wind to justice and you get **relativism**: what seems just to a city is just *for that city*, for as long as it seems so.

Socrates' reply in the *Theaetetus* is the classic **self-refutation argument** against relativism, and it is worth knowing in detail. If every belief is true for whoever holds it, then the belief of Protagoras' opponents, that Protagoras is wrong, is also true:

> And the best of the joke is, that he acknowledges the truth of their opinion who believe his own opinion to be false; for he admits that the opinions of all men are true.
>
> — Plato, *Theaetetus* 171a (tr. Jowett)

Protagoras can escape only by saying his doctrine is "true for me," and then it no longer says anything to anyone else. Whether the refutation is fair is still debated: it may beg the question by assuming an absolute sense of "true" that Protagoras rejects. But it set the pattern for every later argument against global relativism (see [the Ethics course, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md)).

Protagoras was no nihilist. In the "Great Speech" of Plato's dialogue he defends Athenian democracy with a myth. When the first humans gathered in cities they kept wronging one another, because they lacked political skill. Zeus sent Hermes to give them reverence and justice, and Hermes asked whether to distribute these like the crafts, to a few experts:

> 'Shall this be the manner in which I am to distribute justice and reverence among men, or shall I give them to all?' 'To all,' said Zeus; 'I should like them all to have a share; for cities cannot exist, if a few only share in the virtues, as in the arts.'
>
> — Plato, *Protagoras* 322c–d (tr. Jowett)

That is why the Assembly listens only to experts on shipbuilding but lets any citizen speak on justice. The myth is a serious argument for democracy, and Protagoras adds that virtue is taught to everyone, all the time, by parents, nurses, teachers and laws. A sophist is just someone who teaches it a little better. Socrates' response is to doubt that virtue can be taught at all, which leads, by way of a long argument, to the surprising conclusion that virtue is knowledge and *therefore* teachable after all. The dialogue ends with the two men having swapped positions.

### Gorgias: the power of speech

Gorgias came to Athens in 427 BCE as an ambassador from Sicily and dazzled the Assembly with his rhyming, antithetical prose. Two of his works survive.

- **The *Encomium of Helen*** argues that Helen of Troy is blameless for running off with Paris, whatever caused it: the gods, force, love, or persuasion. Speech, he says, is "a mighty lord" that works on the soul as drugs work on the body. The piece is a playful display, but its theory of persuasion as quasi-causal manipulation is serious and disturbing.
- **On What Is Not** (preserved in paraphrase by Sextus Empiricus, *Against the Logicians* 1.65–87) argues three theses: nothing exists; if anything existed, it could not be known; if it could be known, it could not be communicated. Whether this is parody of Parmenides or a real skeptical position, it is the first recorded argument for what we would now call metaphysical, epistemological and semantic nihilism (see [the Nihilism course, Chapter 2](../learn-nihilism/02-ancient-roots.md)).

In Plato's *Gorgias*, he claims that rhetoric is the master art because it gives power over every other expert:

> I have persuaded him to do for me what he would not do for the physician just by the use of rhetoric. And I say that if a rhetorician and a physician were to go to any city, and had there to argue in the Ecclesia or any other assembly as to which of them should be elected state-physician, the physician would have no chance
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 456b (tr. Jowett)

Socrates' answer is that rhetoric, as practiced, is not an art at all but a knack for producing pleasure. It is to justice what cookery is to medicine:

> if the physician and the cook had to enter into a competition in which children were the judges, or men who had no more sense than children, as to which of them best understands the goodness or badness of food, the physician would be starved to death.
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 464d–e (tr. Jowett)

Swap "voters" for "children" and "the attention economy" for "cookery," and the passage reads like a critique of modern media.

### Nature versus convention

The deepest sophistic idea was a distinction between ***physis*** (nature) and ***nomos*** (law, custom, convention). Greeks traveled, and Herodotus had shown them that customs differ wildly. The Greeks burn their dead and the Callatians eat theirs, and each is horrified by the other. If laws and morals vary from city to city, perhaps they are merely conventional. And if they are conventional, why obey them when you can get away with it?

Plato gives the most powerful statement of the radical view to Callicles, a young Athenian aristocrat in the *Gorgias*. Conventional morality, he says, is a trick by which the weak many restrain the strong few:

> nature herself intimates that it is just for the better to have more than the worse, the more powerful than the weaker; and in many ways she shows, among men as well as among animals, and indeed among whole cities and races, that justice consists in the superior ruling over and having more than the inferior.
>
> — Plato, *Gorgias* 483c–d (tr. Jowett)

Our laws, Callicles goes on, take the best of us from youth and "tame them like young lions," but a man with enough force "would trample under foot all our formulas and spells and charms" (484a). Nietzsche read this passage closely (see [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md)). In the *Republic* Thrasymachus offers a blunter version: "I proclaim that justice is nothing else than the interest of the stronger" (338c). Socrates' attempts to answer Callicles and Thrasymachus fill the rest of the *Gorgias* and the whole of the *Republic* (see [Chapter 5](05-plato.md) and [the Ethics course, Chapter 1](../learn-ethics/01-what-is-ethics.md)).

Not every use of the nature–convention distinction was cynical. Antiphon argued that by nature Greeks and barbarians are alike, since we all breathe through mouth and nose. The orator Alcidamas declared that nature made no one a slave. The distinction cuts both ways: it can unmask morality as a tool of the powerful, or unmask social hierarchy as merely conventional.

## Socrates: the man and the problem

Socrates (469–399 BCE) was the son of a stonemason and a midwife. He served as a heavy infantryman in three campaigns of the Peloponnesian War, famously steady in the retreat from Delium. He was poor, barefoot, and so ugly (snub nose, bulging eyes) that his friends joked about it. He wrote nothing.

This creates the **Socratic problem**: our four sources disagree.

- **Aristophanes**' comedy *Clouds* (423 BCE) presents a sophist "Socrates" who runs a "Thinkery," studies the heavens, and teaches students to make the weaker argument defeat the stronger. It is a caricature, but it shows how ordinary Athenians saw him.
- **Xenophon**, a soldier and gentleman-farmer, presents a practical moralist, pious and conventional, who gives sensible advice.
- **Plato** presents a moral and intellectual genius whose questioning is a divine mission. Plato's portrait changes over his career, and in the later dialogues "Socrates" voices Plato's own metaphysics.
- **Aristotle**, who arrived in Athens thirty years after the trial, separates the historical Socrates, who asked "What is it?" about the virtues, from Plato, who made the definitions into separately existing Forms (*Metaphysics* 13.4, 1078b27–32).

The usual scholarly compromise treats Plato's early dialogues (*Apology*, *Crito*, *Euthyphro*, *Laches*, *Charmides*, *Protagoras*, *Gorgias* and others) as the best evidence for the historical Socrates, while remembering that they are artful compositions, not transcripts. Xenophon at least confirms the agenda. Socrates avoided speculation about the heavens and

> himself never wearied of discussing human topics. What is piety? what is impiety? What is the beautiful? what the ugly? What the noble? what the base? What are meant by just and unjust? what by sobriety and madness? what by courage and cowardice?
>
> — Xenophon, *Memorabilia* 1.1.16 (tr. Dakyns)

That turn from nature to ethics is why the thinkers of [Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md) are called "Pre-Socratic." Cicero put it memorably: Socrates "was the first who brought down philosophy from the heavens, placed it in cities, introduced it into families, and obliged it to examine into life and morals, and good and evil" (*Tusculan Disputations* 5.4.10, tr. C. D. Yonge).

## The *Apology*: philosophy on trial

The *Apology* (from *apologia*, "defense speech," not "saying sorry") is Plato's version of what Socrates said at his trial. Plato was in the courtroom (34a), but the speech is certainly polished.

The formal charge:

> It says that Socrates is a doer of evil, who corrupts the youth; and who does not believe in the gods of the state, but has other new divinities of his own.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 24b–c (tr. Jowett)

Socrates says he fears his "old accusers" more: the long-standing prejudice, fed by comedy, that he is a sophist who "made the worse appear the better cause" and speculates about the heavens (18b–c, 19c). He denies both. He takes no fees, and he has no expertise to sell.

### The oracle and Socratic wisdom

Then why the reputation? Socrates tells the story of the oracle. His friend Chaerephon asked the priestess of Apollo at Delphi whether anyone was wiser than Socrates, and she answered that no one was. Socrates was baffled, "for I know that I have no wisdom, small or great" (21b). So he tried to refute the god by finding someone wiser. He questioned politicians, poets and craftsmen in turn, and found that each thought he knew important things he did not. (The craftsmen did know their crafts, but assumed this made them experts on everything else.) He concluded:

> the truth is, O men of Athens, that God only is wise; and by his answer he intends to show that the wisdom of men is worth little or nothing; he is not speaking of Socrates, he is only using my name by way of illustration, as if he said, He, O men, is the wisest, who, like Socrates, knows that his wisdom is in truth worth nothing.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 23a–b (tr. Jowett)

This is **Socratic wisdom**, or "Socratic ignorance": awareness of the limits of one's knowledge (see [Chapter 1](01-what-is-philosophy.md)). Note that Socrates does not claim to know *nothing*. In the *Apology* alone he claims to know that it is wrong to disobey a better person, and that he will not stop philosophizing. His disavowal is of expert knowledge of the most important things: what virtue is, and how to live.

### The mission

Socrates treats his questioning as a religious duty. Offered acquittal on condition that he stops, he says he would refuse:

> I shall obey God rather than you, and while I have life and strength I shall never cease from the practice and teaching of philosophy, exhorting any one whom I meet and saying to him after my manner: You, my friend,—a citizen of the great and mighty and wise city of Athens,—are you not ashamed of heaping up the greatest amount of money and honour and reputation, and caring so little about wisdom and truth and the greatest improvement of the soul, which you never regard or heed at all?
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 29d–e (tr. Jowett)

This is the core of the Socratic ethic: **care of the soul** comes before care of body, money or reputation, because "from virtue comes money and every other good of man" and not the other way round (30b). He also mentions the "divinity" the indictment referred to:

> This sign, which is a kind of voice, first began to come to me when I was a child; it always forbids but never commands me to do anything which I am going to do. This is what deters me from being a politician.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 31c–d (tr. Jowett)

Socrates' *daimonion* has puzzled readers for millennia. It is a private, nonrational warning that Socrates nonetheless trusts. It may be the only thing in the dialogues he accepts without argument.

### Verdict and sentence

He was convicted by about 280 votes to 220. Athenian procedure let the defendant propose an alternative penalty. Socrates proposed that, as a public benefactor, he should be given free meals in the town hall like an Olympic champion, and then, at his friends' urging, offered a fine. The jury chose death by a larger margin. His last words to them are among the most quoted in philosophy:

> Wherefore, O judges, be of good cheer about death, and know of a certainty, that no evil can happen to a good man, either in life or after death.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 41c–d (tr. Jowett)

> The hour of departure has arrived, and we go our ways—I to die, and you to live. Which is better God only knows.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 42a (tr. Jowett)

Why was he really prosecuted? The charges were religious, but the context was political. Athens had lost the Peloponnesian War in 404, and a pro-Spartan junta, the Thirty, had ruled by terror until democracy was restored in 403. The Thirty's leader, Critias, and the traitor Alcibiades had both been Socrates' companions. An amnesty barred prosecution for political offenses before 403, so "corrupting the youth" may have been the available charge. Socrates had refused to obey an illegal order from the Thirty (32c–d), but he had also openly mocked the democratic practice of choosing officials by lot. To many Athenians he looked like an enemy of the city.

## The *Euthyphro*: what is piety?

On his way to the preliminary hearing Socrates meets Euthyphro, a religious expert who is prosecuting his own father for murder. (A hired laborer killed a slave in a drunken rage; the father tied him up and left him in a ditch while waiting for advice, and the laborer died.) Prosecuting your father was shocking, so Euthyphro must be very sure he knows what piety is. Socrates, himself under indictment for impiety, asks to be taught.

The dialogue is the purest example of the Socratic "What is F?" question. Euthyphro's first answer is an example:

> Piety is doing as I am doing; that is to say, prosecuting any one who is guilty of murder, sacrilege, or of any similar crime--whether he be your father or mother, or whoever he may be--that makes no difference
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 5d–e (tr. Jowett)

Socrates explains that he wants something else:

> Remember that I did not ask you to give me two or three examples of piety, but to explain the general idea which makes all pious things to be pious.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 6d (tr. Jowett)

He wants a definition: one feature, common to all and only pious acts, that *explains* why they are pious, a standard "by which I may measure actions." The word translated "idea" is *eidos* or *idea*, which in Plato's later works will name the Forms (see [Chapter 5](05-plato.md)).

Euthyphro's second answer is that piety is what is dear to the gods. But the gods of Greek myth quarrel, and Socrates points out what they must quarrel about. Disputes about size and weight are settled by measuring and weighing; the disputes that make us angry are about "the just and unjust, good and evil, honourable and dishonourable" (7d). So the same act may be dear to one god and hateful to another, and pious and impious at once. Euthyphro revises: piety is what *all* the gods love. Then comes the most famous question in the philosophy of religion:

> The point which I should first wish to understand is whether the pious or holy is beloved by the gods because it is holy, or holy because it is beloved of the gods.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 10a (tr. Jowett)

This is the **Euthyphro dilemma**. Restated for monotheism: is an act right because God commands it, or does God command it because it is right?

- **If right because commanded**, morality looks arbitrary: had God commanded cruelty, cruelty would be right. And "God is good" becomes an empty compliment, meaning only "God does what God commands."
- **If commanded because right**, there is a standard of rightness independent of God that God follows. Then morality does not depend on God, and we might study it directly.

Euthyphro takes the second horn: the gods love the pious because it is pious. But then "loved by the gods" cannot be what piety *is*. It is only a feature, something that happens to be true of pious things, and it tells us nothing about why they are pious. Divine command theorists have answered with refinements ever since: Ockham embraced the first horn; Aquinas grounded the moral law in God's nature rather than his will; Robert Adams identified rightness with the commands of a God who is *essentially* loving. See [the Ethics course, Chapter 4](../learn-ethics/04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md) and [Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md).

After a third attempt (piety as service to the gods) circles back to "what is dear to the gods," Socrates complains that Euthyphro's definitions will not stay put, like the walking statues of Daedalus, Socrates' legendary ancestor. Euthyphro is the real Daedalus, he says: his words go "round in a circle." Socrates begs to start again, and Euthyphro flees:

> Another time, Socrates; for I am in a hurry, and must go now.
>
> — Plato, *Euthyphro* 15e (tr. Jowett)

The dialogue ends in *aporia*, perplexity. The irony is heavy. The man prosecuting his father for impiety cannot say what piety is, and the man about to be convicted of impiety is the only one who knows that he does not know.

## The *Crito*: should Socrates escape?

A month later Socrates is in prison awaiting execution. His wealthy friend Crito arrives before dawn: the guards are bribed and a refuge is ready in Thessaly. Crito's arguments are practical and emotional. People will think his friends were too cheap to save him, Socrates will be abandoning his children, and staying is cowardice that plays into his enemies' hands.

Socrates answers that they should not care what the many think, only what the one who knows thinks, and he lays down two principles they had agreed on before:

> I should like to know whether I may say the same of another proposition--that not life, but a good life, is to be chiefly valued?
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 48b (tr. Jowett)

> Then we ought not to retaliate or render evil for evil to any one, whatever evil we may have suffered from him.
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 49c (tr. Jowett)

Socrates admits that the second principle "has never been held, and never will be held, by any considerable number of persons" (49d), and that those who disagree about it "can only despise one another." It is a radical break with Greek popular morality, which held that justice means helping friends and harming enemies.

Then he imagines the Laws of Athens appearing and questioning him:

> 'Tell us, Socrates,' they say; 'what are you about? are you not going by an act of yours to overturn us--the laws, and the whole state, as far as in you lies? Do you imagine that a state can subsist and not be overthrown, in which the decisions of law have no power, but are set aside and trampled upon by individuals?'
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 50a–b (tr. Jowett)

The Laws give three arguments, which became the three main theories of political obligation:

1. **Gratitude, or parenthood.** The Laws brought him into being by sanctioning his parents' marriage, and nurtured and educated him. He owes them as a child owes a parent.
2. **Harm.** Disobedience, if generalized, would destroy the city. (Compare Kant's test of universalizability, [Chapter 13](13-kant.md).)
3. **Agreement.** By staying in Athens for seventy years when he was free to leave, he agreed to abide by its verdicts:

> But he who has experience of the manner in which we order justice and administer the state, and still remains, has entered into an implied contract that he will do as we command him.
>
> — Plato, *Crito* 51e (tr. Jowett)

This is an early, perhaps the earliest, statement of **tacit consent**, the idea Locke would develop and Hume would mock: a sailor carried aboard while asleep has not consented to the captain's authority just because he does not jump overboard (see [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)). The Laws also offer a middle path between obedience and rebellion: one must "persuade or obey." A citizen may argue against a law, but if he fails to persuade, he must comply.

Readers have long asked whether the *Crito* contradicts the *Apology*. In the *Apology* Socrates says he would disobey a court order to stop philosophizing. In the *Crito* he says he must obey the court's verdict. One reconciliation: in the *Apology* he would openly refuse to *do* wrong at the city's command, and accept the penalty. In the *Crito* he refuses to *evade* the penalty. That is exactly the stance of later civil disobedience, from Thoreau to Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail" (1963), which cites Socrates: break an unjust law openly and accept the punishment.

## The Socratic method: elenchus and irony

[Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md) set out the logic of the Socratic method. Here are its human side and its ethical purpose.

### Elenchus and aporia

The typical early dialogue runs like this. Socrates asks "What is F?" about some virtue: piety, courage (*Laches*), temperance (*Charmides*), friendship (*Lysis*). His partner, who claims to know, offers an answer. Socrates gets him to agree to further premises, shows that they contradict the answer, and the partner tries again. The dialogue ends in **aporia**, an impasse in which nobody knows the answer but everybody knows that they do not.

The experience is not pleasant. In the *Meno*, Meno complains that Socrates is like the stingray:

> you seem to me both in your appearance and in your power over others to be very like the flat torpedo fish, who torpifies those who come near him and touch him, as you have now torpified me, I think. For my soul and my tongue are really torpid, and I do not know how to answer you
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 80a–b (tr. Jowett)

Socrates replies that the torpedo fish is numb itself, and that he makes others perplexed only because he is perplexed himself. And the numbness is progress: Meno who once made "an infinite variety of speeches about virtue" now at least knows that he does not know. Whether the elenchus can ever establish *positive* conclusions, as Socrates sometimes claims (in the *Gorgias* he says his conclusions have been "fixed and riveted" in words "like bonds of iron and adamant," 508e–509a), is "the problem of the elenchus" that Gregory Vlastos made famous in 1983.

### Socratic irony

*Eirôneia* in Greek meant dissembling, saying less than you mean, and it was not a compliment. Thrasymachus accuses Socrates of it when Socrates claims to be too ignorant to define justice:

> How characteristic of Socrates! he replied, with a bitter laugh;—that's your ironical style! Did I not foresee—have I not already told you, that whatever he was asked he would refuse to answer, and try irony or any other shuffle, in order that he might avoid answering?
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 1, 337a (tr. Jowett)

Is Socrates' profession of ignorance sincere? Three readings compete:

- **Pretense.** He knows the answers and feigns ignorance as a teaching device, to get the student to find them. Thrasymachus' view.
- **Sincerity.** He really lacks the knowledge he seeks, and says so.
- **"Complex irony"** (Vlastos). He means it in one sense and not another. He lacks *certain, expert* knowledge of what virtue is, but has well-tested moral beliefs that have survived every elenchus.

Alcibiades, drunk at the end of the *Symposium*, gives the most memorable image of the irony. Socrates is like the hollow statues of Silenus sold in sculptors' shops, which open to reveal images of gods inside:

> his outer mask is the carved head of the Silenus; but, O my companions in drink, when he is opened, what temperance there is residing within!
>
> — Plato, *Symposium* 216d (tr. Jowett)

Søren Kierkegaard wrote his dissertation *On the Concept of Irony with Continual Reference to Socrates* (1841) and spent his career imitating him (see [Chapter 14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md)).

### The Socratic paradoxes

Behind the questions stands a set of positive theses so contrary to common opinion that they are called the **Socratic paradoxes**:

1. **Virtue is knowledge.** To know what is good is to be good.
2. **No one does wrong willingly.** Everyone wants what is really good for them. Wrongdoing is a mistake about what is good, so weakness of will (*akrasia*), knowingly doing what you think worse, is impossible (*Protagoras* 352b–358d).
3. **It is better to suffer injustice than to do it.** Polus asks whether Socrates would rather suffer than do injustice: "I should not like either, but if I must choose between them, I would rather suffer than do" (*Gorgias* 469c). Doing wrong damages your soul, the most important thing you have.
4. **A good person cannot be harmed** (*Apology* 41d). Others can take your property, your freedom and your life, but not your virtue, which is the only thing that matters.

Each has been fiercely contested. Aristotle replied that Socrates' account "is at variance with plain facts" (*Nicomachean Ethics* 7.2, 1145b27–28), and most of us know from experience what it is to do what we believe we should not. But the fourth paradox, through Antisthenes and the Cynics, became the foundation of Stoicism. Epictetus quotes Socrates more than any other philosopher (see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 2](../learn-stoicism/02-before-the-stoa.md) and [the Ethics course, Chapter 5](../learn-ethics/05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md)).

## Socrates and the sophists, compared

So was Socrates a sophist? The Athenian jury thought so, and they had a point. Both he and the sophists questioned traditional beliefs. Both argued with young men in public, and both taught them to argue. Both used clever, sometimes tricky arguments. Both turned philosophy from the cosmos to human affairs.

The differences Plato insists on are four:

| | The sophists (as Plato portrays them) | Socrates |
|---|---|---|
| Payment | high fees | none |
| Claim | to know and to teach virtue and success | to know only his own ignorance |
| Aim | persuasion and victory | truth, and the improvement of the soul |
| Form | long display speeches | short question and answer |

The third difference is the one that matters. Socrates thought that how you argue is a moral matter, because an argument aimed at winning, not at truth, corrupts both speakers (compare Schopenhauer's catalogue of tricks in [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md)). Whether Plato portrayed the sophists fairly is another question. Protagoras in Plato's own dialogue is dignified, humane and nobody's fool.

Socrates' influence spread through his followers into a cluster of "Socratic schools":

- Plato's **Academy** ([Chapter 5](05-plato.md)).
- Antisthenes, who took from Socrates the self-sufficiency of virtue and became the forerunner of the **Cynics** and so of the **Stoics** ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)).
- Aristippus of Cyrene, who took from him the pursuit of happiness and founded the hedonist **Cyrenaics**.
- Euclides of Megara, who combined Socrates with Parmenides and founded the **Megarian** school of logicians.

Nearly every school of the next five centuries claimed him.

## Living it

- **Ask the Euthyphro question of your own rules.** For any rule you follow because an authority says so (a religion, a company, a parent, a party), ask whether the authority says so because it is right, or whether it is right because the authority says so. If the first, try to state the reason directly.
- **Practice the "What is F?" question.** When a debate stalls, look for the key term ("fair," "violence," "freedom") and ask each side to define it. Most disagreements that feel factual are partly definitional.
- **Notice when you are being "cooked for."** Cookery pleases; medicine helps. When content is optimized for engagement, ask which one you are being served.
- **Hold one Socratic principle for a week.** Try "Do not return harm for harm" (*Crito* 49c). Note when it is hard, and what it costs and gains you.
- **Accept the torpedo's sting.** The next time an argument leaves you unable to say what you thought you knew, record the confusion instead of covering it up. It is the first step of inquiry.

## Summary

- Democratic Athens created a demand for persuasion. The sophists met it as professional teachers of *aretê* and rhetoric, and as the first critical theorists of society and knowledge.
- Protagoras taught "man is the measure" and agnosticism about the gods, and defended democracy with a myth of justice given to all. Socrates' self-refutation argument against relativism (*Theaetetus* 171a) is still standard.
- Gorgias praised speech as a power over souls and argued, in *On What Is Not*, a three-stage skepticism. Callicles and Thrasymachus turned the nature–convention distinction against morality itself.
- Socrates (469–399 BCE) wrote nothing. Our evidence comes from Aristophanes, Xenophon, Plato and Aristotle, and the "Socratic problem" is how to reconcile them.
- The *Apology* presents Socratic wisdom (knowing what one does not know), philosophy as a divine mission, care of the soul above all, the *daimonion*, and composure in the face of death. The trial had political as well as religious motives.
- The *Euthyphro* asks for a definition of piety rather than examples, and poses the dilemma of whether the pious is loved by the gods because it is pious or pious because loved.
- The *Crito* puts the good life above mere life, forbids retaliation, and gives three grounds of political obligation: gratitude, harm and tacit agreement.
- The Socratic method uses elenchus to reach aporia and is wrapped in an irony whose sincerity is debated. The Socratic paradoxes (virtue is knowledge; no one does wrong willingly; it is better to suffer than to do wrong; a good person cannot be harmed) shaped the Cynics, the Stoics and all of Greek ethics.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether something is good because God commands it

**Socrates:** You told me that what is right is simply what God commands. Then answer me as Euthyphro could not. Does God command it because it is right, or is it right because God commands it?

**You:** It's right because God commands it. God is the source of morality, not something under a standard.

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Socrates:** Then suppose God had commanded cruelty for its own sake. Would cruelty then be right?

**You:** God would never command that.

**Socrates:** Why not? Because cruelty is wrong?

**You:** Because God is good.

**Socrates:** And what do you mean when you say God is good? If "good" means "what God commands," then "God is good" means only "God commands what God commands." Is that all the praise you meant to give?

**You:** No. I mean something more: that God is loving, just and merciful.

**Socrates:** So you know what love, justice and mercy are independently of God's commands, well enough to praise God for having them?

**You:** I suppose I do. But they come from God's nature, not from some standard outside God.

**Socrates:** That is a new answer, and a better one. You now say that rightness depends on God's nature, not God's will. But is God's nature good because it is loving and just, or are love and justice good because they belong to God's nature?

**You:** That's the same question one level up.

**Socrates:** It is. Can you answer it without either making goodness arbitrary or placing it outside God?

**You:** Perhaps God's nature just *is* the good, so there's no gap between them to ask about.

**Socrates:** Perhaps. Then tell me one more thing. If someone did not believe in God, could they still discover that cruelty is wrong?

**You:** Yes. Plenty of atheists know that.

**Socrates:** Then whatever grounds morality, we seem able to learn it without first learning theology. That is all Euthyphro's dilemma needs to show for our purposes: that ethics can be studied on its own.

*What we learned:* Taking the "right because commanded" horn threatens to make God's goodness empty. Grounding morality in God's nature (Aquinas, Adams) is a more defensible position, but it invites the same question one level up. Either way, moral knowledge seems available without theology. See [the Ethics course, Chapter 4](../learn-ethics/04-divine-command-and-natural-law.md).

### Dialogue 2 — On whether you must obey an unjust verdict

**Socrates:** Suppose you were in my place, condemned unjustly, with the guards bribed and a ship waiting. Would you go?

**You:** Yes. The verdict was unjust, so I owe it nothing. Staying would just let the injustice win.

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Socrates:** Was the trial conducted according to the laws?

**You:** Yes. The procedure was legal; the jury just got it wrong.

**Socrates:** Then is it the laws that wronged you, or the jurors?

**You:** The jurors.

**Socrates:** And by escaping, whom do you harm: the jurors, or the laws?

**You:** I suppose I undermine the laws, since I'm treating a legal verdict as optional.

**Socrates:** If everyone treated verdicts they thought unjust as optional, would there be any verdicts?

**You:** No. Every losing party thinks the verdict is unjust.

**Socrates:** So you would claim for yourself a right you could not let everyone have?

**You:** But that proves too much. It would mean no one should ever resist any law, even a terrible one.

**Socrates:** Did I say that? I told the jury I would go on philosophizing even if they ordered me to stop.

**You:** Then what's the difference?

**Socrates:** In that case I would refuse to *do* wrong, openly, and accept the penalty. Here you would be *escaping* the penalty in secret, by bribery. One persuades the city or bears its judgment; the other does neither.

**You:** So civil disobedience is acceptable, but evading punishment isn't?

**Socrates:** That is how King read me, and Thoreau before him. But consider whether the Laws ask too much. Did you agree to them by staying, if you had nowhere else to go?

**You:** Not really. Most people can't just leave their country.

**Socrates:** Then the argument from agreement is weaker for you than for me, who could have left for seventy years. Notice how the weight shifts depending on whom we speak of.

*What we learned:* The Laws' arguments distinguish disobeying in order to avoid doing wrong (open, with the penalty accepted) from escaping a lawful penalty in secret. That distinction is the template for civil disobedience. The tacit-consent argument is strongest for those who are genuinely free to leave. See [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md).

### Dialogue 3 — On whether each person is the measure of truth

**Protagoras:** You find the wind cold, Socrates finds it warm. Who is right?

**You:** Neither. It's cold for me and warm for him. There's no further fact.

*Your turn: answer Protagoras in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Protagoras:** Good. And justice: Athens says one thing, Sparta another. Who is right?

**You:** Same answer. Just for Athens, just for Sparta.

**Protagoras:** You are my pupil already. Now, a man comes to me and says, "Protagoras is wrong; some things are true for everyone." Is his belief true?

**You:** True for him.

**Protagoras:** And is it true for him that his belief is true for everyone, or only for him?

**You:** He believes it's true for everyone, so... it's true for him that it's true for everyone.

**Protagoras:** And my belief that it is true only for him?

**You:** True for you.

**Protagoras:** Then can either of us ever be wrong?

**You:** No, not by your theory.

**Protagoras:** Then what do I teach, and why do people pay me?

**You:** I don't know. If no one is ever wrong, there's nothing to improve.

**Protagoras:** In the *Theaetetus* I answer that some beliefs are not truer but *better*: the wise man is like a doctor who changes a sick man's taste so that food seems sweet to him again. I make what seems to a city better, not truer.

**You:** But then "better" isn't relative. You're saying some appearances really are better than others.

**Protagoras:** You have caught me as Socrates did. Either I keep relativism and lose my profession, or keep my profession and admit one thing that is not relative.

**You:** So the measure doctrine works for the wind, but not for everything.

**Protagoras:** Many would grant that much: about how things feel, each of us is the measure. The question is how far that territory extends.

*What we learned:* Global relativism struggles to make sense of disagreement, error and improvement, and Protagoras' "better, not truer" reply concedes a nonrelative standard. A restricted relativism about appearances, tastes or some conventions is much more defensible. See [the Ethics course, Chapter 3](../learn-ethics/03-relativism-and-egoism.md).
