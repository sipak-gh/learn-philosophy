# Chapter 12 — The Empiricists

One winter evening around 1671, five or six friends met in a London chamber to discuss morality and religion and quickly got stuck. One of them, a physician and political secretary named John Locke, suggested that before arguing about such questions they should first ask what the human mind is capable of knowing at all. He thought the inquiry would fill a sheet of paper. It took him nearly twenty years and became the *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689). The tradition it began, **British empiricism**, holds that all our ideas come from experience. Followed rigorously, by George Berkeley and then by David Hume, this sensible-sounding principle produces some of the strangest conclusions in philosophy: that matter does not exist, that we have no idea of causal necessity, that there is no self we can observe, and that we have no rational grounds for expecting the sun to rise tomorrow. This chapter follows that development step by step, because each step is an argument you will have to answer if you want to keep your common sense.

Previous: [Chapter 11 — Descartes and the Rationalists](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md). Next: [Chapter 13 — Kant](13-kant.md).

## Locke: the mind as white paper

John Locke (1632–1704) trained in medicine at Oxford, worked with the chemist Robert Boyle, and served as physician and secretary to the Earl of Shaftesbury, founder of the Whig party. When Shaftesbury's plots against the Catholic heir to the throne failed, Locke fled to the Netherlands (1683–1689), returning after the Glorious Revolution with the manuscripts of the *Essay* and the *Two Treatises of Government*, both published in 1689–1690. His political philosophy, with its natural rights, consent and right of revolution, appears in [Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md) and [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md). Here we follow his theory of knowledge.

Locke describes his ambition modestly. In an age of great natural philosophers, "the incomparable Mr. Newton" among them:

> it is ambition enough to be employed as an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish that lies in the way to knowledge
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, Epistle to the Reader

This picture of philosophy as the "under-labourer" of science, clearing conceptual confusion so that inquiry can proceed, became a model for much twentieth-century philosophy ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)). Locke's aim is to map the limits of the understanding, so that we do not waste effort on questions beyond us and do not despair of those within reach:

> The Candle that is set up in us shines bright enough for all our purposes.
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* I.i.5

### Against innate ideas

Book I attacks the doctrine, held by Descartes and the Cambridge Platonists, that some ideas and principles (of logic, of morality, of God) are born with us. The standard argument for innateness was **universal consent**: everyone agrees that whatever is, is; so the principle must be innate. Locke answers that universal agreement would not prove innateness if there were another explanation of it, and that there is no universal agreement anyway: "children and idiots" have no notion of the law of identity, and moral principles vary widely across cultures. A defender might reply that innate ideas are present *implicitly* and become conscious when we reason. Then, says Locke, every truth we can ever come to know is innate, and the doctrine says nothing.

### All ideas from experience

Book II states the positive thesis:

> Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas:—How comes it to be furnished? Whence comes it by that vast store which the busy and boundless fancy of man has painted on it with an almost endless variety? Whence has it all the MATERIALS of reason and knowledge? To this I answer, in one word, from EXPERIENCE.
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.i.2

The image is usually given in Latin as ***tabula rasa***, a scraped writing tablet (Locke's own image here is "white paper"). Experience has two sources: **sensation**, of external things, and **reflection**, the mind's perception of its own operations (thinking, doubting, willing). The mind passively receives **simple ideas** (red, cold, solid) and actively combines, compares and abstracts them into **complex ideas** (a horse, gratitude, infinity).

Note what Locke means by "idea": "whatsoever is the object of the understanding when a man thinks." It covers sensations, images and concepts. This broad, ambiguous word will cause much trouble.

### Primary and secondary qualities

Locke adopts the distinction from Galileo and Boyle ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)). **Primary qualities** are inseparable from bodies:

> These, which I call ORIGINAL or PRIMARY qualities of body, are wholly inseparable from it
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.viii.9

They are solidity, extension, figure, motion or rest, and number. **Secondary qualities** (colors, sounds, tastes, smells, warmth) are:

> nothing in the objects themselves, but powers to produce various sensations in us; and depend on those primary qualities, viz. bulk, figure, texture, and motion of parts
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.viii.10

Our ideas of primary qualities **resemble** their causes; ideas of secondary qualities do not. There is roundness in the snowball as there is in my idea of it, but nothing like my sensation of cold. Locke's evidence includes the famous experiment of putting one hand that is hot and one that is cold into the same basin of lukewarm water: it feels cold to one hand and warm to the other, but the water cannot be both. This is a **representative theory of perception**: we perceive ideas, which represent things that we never perceive directly.

The picture has a weak spot. If I perceive only my ideas, how do I know that they resemble anything, or that anything lies behind them? I can never step outside my ideas to compare them with the originals. Berkeley will exploit this.

### Substance: something, he knew not what

We observe qualities (yellow, heavy, malleable) regularly occurring together, and we suppose they inhere in some underlying **substance** that supports them. But what idea do we have of the support itself?

> So that if any one will examine himself concerning his notion of pure substance in general, he will find he has no other idea of it at all, but only a supposition of he knows not what SUPPORT of such qualities which are capable of producing simple ideas in us
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.xxiii.2

He compares the philosopher to a story about an Indian who said the world rested on an elephant, and the elephant on a tortoise, and when pressed about what held up the tortoise, "replied—SOMETHING, HE KNEW NOT WHAT." Locke does not reject substance; he thinks we cannot do without it. But an empiricist who admits that a central concept has no empirical content has left a door open.

### Personal identity

In the second edition (1694), Locke added a chapter that founded the modern debate on **personal identity**. What makes you the same person as the child in your old photographs? Not the same matter, since your atoms are replaced. Not the same soul, since we cannot observe souls and, for all we know, a soul could be swapped while everything else remains. Locke separates three questions: the same **substance**, the same **man** (a living human organism) and the same **person**:

> a thinking intelligent being, that has reason and reflection, and can consider itself as itself, the same thinking thing, in different times and places
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.xxvii.9

What makes a person the same is consciousness, especially memory:

> as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.xxvii.9

The thought experiment that launched a thousand science-fiction plots: if "the soul of a prince, carrying with it the consciousness of the prince's past life," entered the body of a cobbler, the cobbler's body would now house the prince, the same *person* but not the same *man*. Why does this matter? Because "person" is "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit": it is the concept we use for responsibility, reward and punishment.

Two objections became classics. Joseph Butler (1736) argued the account is **circular**: memory presupposes personal identity, since I can only genuinely remember my own experiences. Thomas Reid (1785) posed the **brave officer** paradox: a boy is flogged for stealing apples; as a young officer he remembers the flogging; as an old general he remembers taking the enemy standard but not the flogging. By Locke's account the general is the officer, and the officer is the boy, but the general is not the boy, which violates the transitivity of identity. Contemporary theories (Derek Parfit's in particular) build on Locke's insight while replacing direct memory with overlapping chains of psychological connection ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)).

### Molyneux's problem

Locke's friend William Molyneux sent him a question, which Locke printed in the second edition:

> "Suppose a man BORN blind, and now adult, and taught by his TOUCH to distinguish between a cube and a sphere
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* II.ix.8, quoting William Molyneux

If his sight were restored, could he tell, by sight alone and before touching, which is the cube and which the sphere? Molyneux and Locke said no: the connection between how things look and how they feel must be learned. Rationalists like Leibniz said yes, because the geometrical idea is common to both senses. The question became an experimental one, and a 2011 study of patients who received sight after cataract surgery in India (Held and colleagues) found that they could not immediately match by sight objects they knew by touch, but learned to within days.

## Berkeley: to be is to be perceived

George Berkeley (1685–1753) was an Irish Anglican clergyman, later Bishop of Cloyne, who published his main philosophical works in his mid-twenties: the *Principles of Human Knowledge* (1710) and the *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous* (1713). He spent three years in Rhode Island trying to found a college in Bermuda; the town of Berkeley, California, is named after him because of his poem predicting that the course of empire would move west. He believed that philosophy had made itself unnecessary trouble:

> we have first raised a dust and then complain we cannot see.
>
> — George Berkeley, *Principles of Human Knowledge*, Introduction §3

### Against abstract ideas

Locke held that the mind can form **abstract general ideas**, for example the idea of a triangle "neither oblique nor rectangle, neither equilateral, equicrural, nor scalinon; but all and none of these at once" (IV.vii.9). Berkeley tried and failed. Every idea of a triangle he can frame is of some particular triangle. General words work by letting one particular idea stand for all others of its kind. This matters because the most abstract idea of all, Berkeley argues, is **matter**: something that exists independently of being perceived, with no color, sound or taste. If abstract ideas are impossible, there is no such idea.

### Immaterialism

The thesis of the *Principles*:

> Their ESSE is PERCIPI, nor is it possible they should have any existence out of the minds or thinking things which perceive them.
>
> — George Berkeley, *Principles of Human Knowledge* I.3

*Esse est percipi*: for sensible things, to be is to be perceived. Berkeley thinks this is close to self-evident once we attend to what we mean:

> It is indeed an opinion STRANGELY prevailing amongst men, that houses, mountains, rivers, and in a word all sensible objects, have an existence, natural or real, distinct from their being perceived by the understanding.
>
> — George Berkeley, *Principles of Human Knowledge* I.4

His arguments, set out at length in the *Three Dialogues*:

1. **From the nature of sensible things.** What do we perceive? Colors, shapes, sounds, textures. These are sensations, or ideas. An idea cannot exist unperceived. So sensible things cannot exist unperceived.
2. **The collapse of the primary/secondary distinction.** Locke's arguments for the mind-dependence of secondary qualities (they vary with the perceiver) apply equally to primary ones: size and shape vary with distance and viewpoint, and speed with the observer. And we cannot even conceive of an extended thing with no color. So if colors are in the mind, so is extension.
3. **Pain and heat.** Philonous gets Hylas to agree that intense heat is a pain, and pain cannot exist in an unperceiving thing:

> PHIL. But is not the most vehement and intense degree of heat a very great pain? HYL. No one can deny it. PHIL. And is any unperceiving thing capable of pain or pleasure? HYL. No, certainly.
>
> — George Berkeley, *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous*, First Dialogue

4. **The "master argument."** Try to conceive a tree existing unperceived:

> But, say you, surely there is nothing easier than for me to imagine trees, for instance, in a park, or books existing in a closet, and nobody by to perceive them. I answer, you may so, there is no difficulty in it; but what is all this, I beseech you, more than framing in your mind certain ideas which you call BOOKS and TREES, and the same time omitting to frame the idea of any one that may perceive them?
>
> — George Berkeley, *Principles of Human Knowledge* I.23

In conceiving the unperceived tree you are perceiving it in imagination. Critics reply that Berkeley confuses the act of conceiving (which is mine) with its content (a tree no one perceives). I can conceive of a world without me in it without being in that world.

### God and the unperceived tree

If to be is to be perceived, does my desk vanish when I leave the room? Berkeley's answer is that there is always a perceiver: God. Our ideas of sense, unlike those of imagination, are not under our control and come in regular order; they must be caused by a mind, and their order is what we call the laws of nature:

> The ideas imprinted on the Senses by the Author of nature are called REAL THINGS
>
> — George Berkeley, *Principles of Human Knowledge* I.33

A limerick by Ronald Knox has a young man finding it odd that a tree should continue to be when no one is about in the quad; an anonymous reply, signed "Yours faithfully, God," assures him that the tree continues to be, since observed by God.

Berkeley insisted he was defending common sense against the skeptic. On Locke's view, there is a gap between our ideas and an unknowable material world, which opens the door to doubting the world. On Berkeley's, the things we see and touch just are the real things; there is no gap and nothing to doubt. When Boswell told Samuel Johnson that Berkeley's doctrine could not be refuted, Johnson kicked a large stone so hard that he rebounded from it, and declared that this was how he refuted it. But Berkeley never denied that stones are hard or that kicking them hurts; he denied only that their hardness is anything beyond what is perceived. Johnson's foot gave him one more idea.

Berkeley's own summary of his project:

> the same Principles which, at first view, lead to Scepticism, pursued to a certain point, bring men back to Common Sense.
>
> — George Berkeley, *Three Dialogues between Hylas and Philonous*, Third Dialogue

Hume thought otherwise. Berkeley's arguments, he wrote in the *Enquiry*, are in reality merely sceptical, for "they admit of no answer and produce no conviction."

## Hume: the science of man

David Hume (1711–1776) is widely regarded as the greatest philosopher to write in English. Born in Edinburgh, he had a breakdown in his late teens from the strain of study, recovered in France, and wrote *A Treatise of Human Nature* (1739–40) in his twenties. It sold poorly; in his short autobiography Hume said it was stillborn. He recast the first book as the more elegant *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), was twice rejected for professorships because of his reputation for atheism, and became famous as a historian (his *History of England* was a bestseller) and essayist. He was cheerful, generous and well loved; in Paris he was called "le bon David." His *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion* were published only after his death, at his insistence.

Hume's ambition was to be the Newton of the mind, building a **science of man** on experience and observation. The result was a theory of the mind as a system of perceptions moved by association, and a series of skeptical arguments that changed philosophy. He was also aware that the abstract philosopher can lose touch with life, and gave himself advice in Nature's voice:

> Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* I.4

### Impressions and ideas

Hume divides perceptions into **impressions** (sensations, passions and emotions as they first appear) and **ideas** (the fainter copies of them in thought and memory). The difference is one of force and vivacity: the pain of a burn versus remembering it. The **copy principle**:

> all our ideas or more feeble perceptions are copies of our impressions or more lively ones.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* II.13

Even the idea of God, analyzed, comes from reflecting on our own mind's operations and "augmenting, without limit, those qualities of goodness and wisdom." Hume immediately offers a counterexample to his own principle: a person who has seen every shade of blue except one, shown the full spectrum with a gap, could probably imagine the **missing shade of blue** without an impression of it. Hume dismisses the case as "so singular" that it does not merit altering the general maxim. Critics have found this unconvincing.

The copy principle becomes a weapon. Of any philosophical term, ask: from what impression is this idea derived? If none can be found, the term is meaningless. Hume uses this test on causation, the self and substance.

### Hume's fork

> All the objects of human reason or enquiry may naturally be divided into two kinds, to wit, Relations of Ideas, and Matters of Fact.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* IV.20

**Relations of ideas** (geometry, algebra, arithmetic) are known by thought alone, and their denial is a contradiction. **Matters of fact** are different:

> The contrary of every matter of fact is still possible; because it can never imply a contradiction, and is conceived by the mind with the same facility and distinctness, as if ever so conformable to reality. That the sun will not rise to-morrow is no less intelligible a proposition, and implies no more contradiction than the affirmation, that it will rise.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* IV.21

This distinction corresponds to Leibniz's truths of reason and truths of fact ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)), but Hume uses it differently: it closes the door on rationalist metaphysics. Kant will call the two types analytic and synthetic, and ask whether there might be a third ([Chapter 13](13-kant.md)). The logical positivists will revive the fork as a criterion of meaning ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)).

### The problem of induction

All reasoning about matters of fact beyond what we observe and remember rests on **cause and effect**. And our knowledge of causes comes entirely from experience: Adam, however intelligent, could not have inferred from the transparency of water that it would drown him. Before experience, any effect could follow any cause; you could not tell that one billiard ball striking another would move it rather than stop, bounce back or turn into a pigeon.

Now the crucial question. Having observed that A has always been followed by B, we infer that the next A will be followed by B. What justifies this inference? It assumes that **the future will resemble the past** (the "uniformity of nature"). Can that assumption be justified?

1. Not by relations of ideas, since there is no contradiction in nature changing its course.
2. Not by experience, since any argument from experience that the future will resemble the past already assumes that the future will resemble the past. That would be circular.
3. So it cannot be justified at all.

This is the **problem of induction**, perhaps the most discussed argument in modern philosophy. Note what Hume does *not* say. He does not say we should stop making inductive inferences; we cannot, and "none but a fool or madman will ever pretend to dispute the authority of experience." He says the inference is not founded on reason. It is founded on **custom**, or habit:

> Custom, then, is the great guide of human life. It is that principle alone which renders our experience useful to us, and makes us expect, for the future, a similar train of events with those which have appeared in the past.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* V.36

Responses include Kant's (causation is a precondition of experience, [Chapter 13](13-kant.md)); Karl Popper's (science does not use induction but bold conjectures and attempted refutations); Nelson Goodman's "new riddle" ("grue"), which shows the problem is even worse than Hume thought; and various probabilistic and pragmatic justifications ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

### Causation and necessary connection

When we say that the cue ball *caused* the eight ball to move, we mean more than that one moved after the other. We mean there is a **necessary connection**: the second *had* to move. Apply the copy principle: from what impression does the idea of necessary connection come? We see the first ball move, then the second. We see no third thing, a force or power, linking them. In a single case, we can observe only succession and contiguity. After many cases, we observe **constant conjunction**. But nothing in the many cases is different from the single one, except that after repetition, the mind, on seeing the first, is carried by habit to expect the second. That feeling of expectation is the impression from which the idea of necessity is copied. Necessity, says Hume, is in the mind, not in the objects.

He offers two definitions of cause:

> we may define a cause to be an object, followed by another, and where all the objects similar to the first are followed by objects similar to the second. Or in other words where, if the first object had not been, the second never had existed.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* VII.60

The first is the **regularity** theory; the clause "in other words" in fact introduces a different, **counterfactual** analysis, developed by David Lewis in the 1970s. Interpreters disagree whether Hume believed there is no necessity in nature (the traditional reading) or only that we can have no idea of it, as the "New Hume" interpreters (Galen Strawson, John Wright) argue.

### The self as a bundle

Descartes thought the self was the most certain thing of all ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). Hume looked for it:

> For my part, when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.iv.6

There is no impression of a simple, continuing self, so no idea of one. What we call the self is:

> nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.iv.6

This **bundle theory** resembles the Buddhist doctrine of *anattā*, no-self ([Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md)); Alison Gopnik has argued that Hume may have learned of Buddhist ideas through a Jesuit at La Flèche, where he wrote the *Treatise*. In the Appendix to the *Treatise*, Hume confessed that he could not explain what binds the perceptions into one bundle, and that his account of personal identity was a "labyrinth."

### Skepticism and its cure

Book I of the *Treatise* ends in a crisis. Reason, applied consistently, undermines itself; Hume finds himself in "the most deplorable condition imaginable." And then:

> Most fortunately it happens, that since reason is incapable of dispelling these clouds, nature herself suffices to that purpose, and cures me of this philosophical melancholy and delirium, either by relaxing this bent of mind, or by some avocation, and lively impression of my senses, which obliterate all these chimeras. I dine, I play a game of backgammon, I converse, and am merry with my friends
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* I.iv.7

This is Hume's **mitigated skepticism**: we cannot answer the skeptic, but we cannot believe him either, and nature will not let us stop believing in bodies, causes and the future. Philosophy's proper role is to correct our everyday reasonings and to restrain dogmatism, the "Pyrrhonian" doubt of ancient skepticism being healthy in small doses ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)). The *Enquiry* ends with a more aggressive conclusion:

> If we take in our hand any volume; of divinity or school metaphysics, for instance; let us ask, Does it contain any abstract reasoning concerning quantity or number? No. Does it contain any experimental reasoning concerning matter of fact and existence? No. Commit it then to the flames: for it can contain nothing but sophistry and illusion.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* XII.132

(Readers have noticed that the *Enquiry* itself contains no reasoning concerning quantity or number.)

### Miracles and the design argument

Section X of the *Enquiry*, "Of Miracles," applies the logic of evidence to religious testimony:

> A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* X.87

> A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature; and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* X.90

The general maxim:

> That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* X.91

Hume adds that witnesses to miracles have historically been few, credulous, eager for wonders and far away. The argument anticipates Bayesian reasoning about base rates: Carl Sagan's slogan that "extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence" is its descendant.

In the *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, three characters debate the **design argument**. Cleanthes presents it at its strongest:

> Look round the world: contemplate the whole and every part of it: You will find it to be nothing but one great machine, subdivided into an infinite number of lesser machines
>
> — David Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Part II

Philo, the skeptic, takes it apart. The analogy between the universe and a machine is weak; we have experience of many machines being made, but of only one universe and never of its origin. Even if the analogy held, it would not establish one infinite, perfect God. For all we know, the world:

> was only the first rude essay of some infant deity, who afterwards abandoned it, ashamed of his lame performance
>
> — David Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Part V

And the problem of evil remains:

> EPICURUS's old questions are yet unanswered. Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?
>
> — David Hume, *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*, Part X

The attribution of these questions to Epicurus is probably mistaken; they come from Lactantius, who attributed them to him. Eighty years later, Darwin showed how the appearance of design can arise without a designer, completing what Philo began.

### Reason and the passions

Hume's moral philosophy belongs to the ethics course ([Hume and the sentimentalists](../learn-ethics/08-hume-and-the-sentimentalists.md)), but two claims belong here because they apply his epistemology to ethics. First:

> Reason is, and ought only to be the slave of the passions, and can never pretend to any other office than to serve and obey them.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* II.iii.3

Reason can tell us about matters of fact and relations of ideas; it can inform us that an object of desire exists or how to get it. But it cannot by itself move us to act. Second, the **is–ought gap**: in every system of morality, Hume notices, the author proceeds in the ordinary way and then:

> of a sudden I am surprized to find, that instead of the usual copulations of propositions, is, and is not, I meet with no proposition that is not connected with an ought, or an ought not.
>
> — David Hume, *A Treatise of Human Nature* III.i.1

No reason is given for how an "ought" can be deduced from premises containing only "is." Whether Hume meant that this is impossible, or only that it needs explaining, is itself debated ([the ethics course on metaethics](../learn-ethics/17-metaethics.md)).

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Was Locke a representationalist?** The traditional "veil of ideas" reading has Locke's mind trapped behind its ideas. John Yolton and others argue that for Locke ideas are the means *by which* we perceive things, not the objects we perceive instead of them.

**Was Berkeley an idealist or a phenomenalist?** Is a table a collection of ideas in God's mind, or the actual and possible ideas of finite perceivers? Both readings fit some texts.

**Hume's skepticism or naturalism?** Norman Kemp Smith (1941) argued that Hume's primary aim was not skepticism but a naturalism in which belief is grounded in feeling rather than reason. Most current interpreters accept some version of this, but disagree about how far it rescues Hume from his skeptical arguments.

**Women and early modern empiricism.** Damaris Masham, Catharine Trotter Cockburn (*A Defence of Mr. Locke's Essay*, 1702) and Mary Astell (a critic of Locke from a Cartesian-Platonist position) took part in these debates, and recent histories include them.

## Living it

**Run the copy-principle test.** Pick a big concept you use often: "freedom," "the economy," "my true self," "fairness," "energy" in its wellness sense. Ask Hume's question: from what impression is this idea derived? What experiences would count for or against its application? You will find some ideas that survive, some that need a definition, and possibly one or two that dissolve.

**Proportion belief to evidence.** For a week, when you hear a surprising claim (in the news, from a friend, on social media), ask Hume's question about miracles: which would be more surprising, that this is true or that the source is wrong? Do not decide every case. Just notice how often the second is more probable.

**Look for yourself.** Sit quietly for five minutes and try to observe the self that is having your experiences, as Hume did. Note what you find. Many meditators report something like Hume's result; some philosophers say the search is confused from the start, because the self is the observer, not an observed object. Which do you find?

## Summary

- Locke: philosophy as "under-labourer"; no innate ideas; the mind as white paper; sensation and reflection; simple and complex ideas; primary qualities (resembling) and secondary qualities (powers); substance as "something, he knew not what"; personal identity as continuity of consciousness (objections from Butler and Reid); Molyneux's problem.
- Berkeley: no abstract ideas; *esse est percipi*; the collapse of the primary/secondary distinction; the master argument; God as the perceiver and author of the order of nature; immaterialism as a defense of common sense against skepticism.
- Hume: impressions and ideas; the copy principle and the missing shade of blue; the fork (relations of ideas vs. matters of fact); the problem of induction; custom as the guide of life; causation as constant conjunction plus the mind's expectation; the bundle theory of the self; mitigated skepticism; miracles; the critique of the design argument; reason as the slave of the passions; is–ought.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether things exist when no one is looking

**Philonous:** You are certain your kitchen exists right now, though you are not in it?

**You:** Of course. If I go back, it will be there, with the same dishes I left in the sink.

*Your turn: Philonous will ask what you mean by "the kitchen exists." Before reading on, write two or three sentences defining what it is for a thing to exist unperceived.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It means the kitchen is there, with its shape and colors, whether or not anyone perceives it.

**Philonous:** Its colors. Is the color of your kitchen tiles the same in daylight, at dusk and under a yellow lamp?

**You:** No, but the real color is the daylight one.

**Philonous:** Why daylight? Is the sun's light privileged over a lamp? And to a bee, which sees ultraviolet, the tiles look different again. Which perceiver gets the true color?

**You:** Fine. Color depends on the perceiver. Locke already said that. But the shape and size of the kitchen do not.

**Philonous:** From the doorway the table is large; from the street through the window it is small; a rectangle seen at an angle is a trapezoid. Do these not vary just as color does?

**You:** They do, but physics tells us the real size, independent of viewpoint. We measure it.

**Philonous:** With a ruler, which you see and touch. Every measurement ends in a perception. Can you describe the table's real shape without any reference to how it would look or feel?

**You:** I can describe it mathematically: a set of coordinates.

**Philonous:** And those numbers mean something to you only because you know what it would be like to see or touch something with those dimensions. Now try to imagine the kitchen with no one perceiving it.

**You:** I am imagining it: dark, empty, the dishes in the sink.

**Philonous:** And who is doing the imagining?

**You:** I am. But that is a trick. I am imagining a kitchen that no one perceives. The content of my thought does not include me, just as I can think of the world before I was born without being in it.

**Philonous:** That is the best answer to my argument, and many think it succeeds. Let me ask instead: what would be different, for you or anyone, if the kitchen did not exist while unperceived but always reappeared exactly as expected?

**You:** Nothing I could observe. But the best explanation of why the dishes are still there, and why the milk has gone sour on schedule, is that the kitchen went on existing and changing.

**Philonous:** Or that the Author of nature keeps his ideas in good order. You see that the two hypotheses predict the same experiences.

**You:** Then here is where I stand. Your arguments show that the qualities I perceive depend on perceivers, and that my idea of matter is thinner than I thought. They do not show that unperceived things are impossible. And inference to the best explanation, which science uses everywhere, favors a world that goes on without me over an attentive God. I will take the existing kitchen, and a clearer sense of what "exists" means.

**Philonous:** You will at least admit that you came in thinking the question was trivial.

</details>

### Dialogue 2 — On whether the sun will rise tomorrow

**David Hume:** You expect the sun to rise tomorrow. On what grounds?

**You:** It has risen every day for billions of years.

*Your turn: Hume will ask why the past should be any guide to the future. Write two or three sentences giving the best justification you can.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Because nature follows laws. The earth rotates, and the laws of physics tell us it will keep doing so.

**David Hume:** And how do you know these laws will hold tomorrow?

**You:** They always have.

**David Hume:** Then you are reasoning: what has held in the past will hold in the future, because it has held in the past. Is that not circular?

**You:** It is circular. But it works. Induction has been reliable.

**David Hume:** Reliable *until now*. To conclude it will continue to be reliable is to make one more inductive inference.

**You:** All right. Suppose I cannot justify induction without circularity. Should I then stop expecting the sun to rise?

**David Hume:** Could you, if you tried?

**You:** No. I would still set my alarm.

**David Hume:** Then you have discovered what I discovered. Nature has not left this to our reasoning; custom determines us. I do not call it irrational to expect the sunrise. I say only that it is not reason that produces the expectation.

**You:** That still sounds like bad news for science. Is physics then just a set of habits?

**David Hume:** Habits that we correct by more experience, by comparing cases, by proportioning our belief to the evidence. The wise man and the fool both rely on custom; the wise man's custom is better disciplined. What else would you want from science?

**You:** A guarantee. Some reason to think that our methods track the truth and not just what we are used to.

**David Hume:** Then you want what no one has.

**You:** Perhaps. Karl Popper said science does not need induction: it proposes hypotheses and tries to refute them. A theory that survives tests is not proven, only not yet falsified.

**David Hume:** And when you build a bridge on a theory that has survived tests, are you not expecting it to keep surviving? You have put the habit back in by the side door.

**You:** Then here is my position. I accept that induction cannot be justified by deduction or by non-circular argument. But I do not think this makes it irrational. Being rational may simply mean using the methods that are well-disciplined by experience, since there is no standpoint outside all experience from which to check them. That is your mitigated skepticism, isn't it?

**David Hume:** Close enough. And now, I think, it is time for backgammon.

</details>
