# Chapter 3 — The Presocratics

Around 585 BCE, on the coast of what is now western Turkey, a handful of Greeks began to give a new kind of answer to the question "What is the world made of, and why does it behave as it does?" Their answers were wrong in nearly every detail: everything is water, everything is air, everything is fire, nothing moves, everything moves. Yet the *kind* of answer was new. It appealed to natural principles instead of the moods of gods, it was offered for criticism instead of recited as tradition, and each thinker answered the last one with an argument. Within two centuries this conversation had produced the first deductive proof in philosophy (Parmenides), paradoxes that occupied mathematicians until the nineteenth century (Zeno), and a theory of atoms and the void that, by way of Epicurus and Lucretius, reached the founders of modern physics. This chapter follows the conversation from Thales to Democritus, and asks what survives of it.

## How we know what we know: fragments and testimonies

Not one book by any Presocratic survives whole. What we have comes in two kinds:

- **Fragments**: sentences that later writers quote, claiming to give the philosopher's own words. Heraclitus survives in about 130 of them, most a single sentence long. Parmenides is luckier: Sextus Empiricus and the sixth-century commentator Simplicius copied out about 160 lines of his poem.
- **Testimonies**: reports *about* what someone thought. Most go back to Aristotle and his student Theophrastus, who wrote a history of earlier physical theories. That history was excerpted, re-excerpted and garbled for eight hundred years by compilers known collectively as the **doxographers** (from *doxa*, "opinion").

Two cautions follow. First, every quotation reached us through someone with an agenda. Aristotle read his predecessors as fumbling toward his own four causes (see [Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md)); Christian writers such as Hippolytus quoted Heraclitus to show that heretics had stolen from pagans. Second, the standard reference numbers come from Hermann Diels and Walther Kranz, *Die Fragmente der Vorsokratiker* (6th ed., 1951–52). "DK 22 B30" means chapter 22 (Heraclitus), section B (fragments regarded as genuine), number 30; "A" numbers are testimonies. Burnet, whose translations this chapter quotes because they are in the public domain, numbers Heraclitus after the older edition of Ingram Bywater. Both numbers are given below.

Even the name "Presocratic" is a convenience. Several of these thinkers (Democritus, the later Pythagoreans) outlived Socrates, and they did not see themselves as a "before" to anything. Ancient writers more often called them *physikoi*, students of *physis*, "nature."

## The Milesians: what is everything made of?

Miletus was a rich trading city where Greek, Lydian, Egyptian and Babylonian knowledge met. Its three philosophers asked one question: what is the *archê*, the origin and underlying stuff of all things? The question assumes something bold, that beneath the variety of the world lies a unity that reason can find.

### Thales

Thales (fl. c. 585 BCE) wrote nothing that survives. Herodotus credits him with predicting a solar eclipse that stopped a battle between the Lydians and the Medes:

> suddenly the day became night. And this change of the day Thales the Milesian had foretold to the Ionians laying down as a limit this very year in which the change took place.
>
> — Herodotus, *Histories* 1.74 (tr. G. C. Macaulay)

Modern astronomers date that eclipse to 28 May 585 BCE. Whether Thales could really predict it is doubtful: Babylonian eclipse cycles could at best flag a possible year, which is just what Herodotus' careful phrase "laying down as a limit this very year" suggests.

Aristotle (*Metaphysics* 1.3, 983b20–27) is our main source for the philosophy. Burnet reduces his reports to three claims:

> (1) The earth floats on the water. (2) Water is the material cause of all things. (3) All things are full of gods. The magnet is alive; for it has the power of moving iron.
>
> — John Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 9, summarizing Aristotle

Why water? Aristotle guesses that Thales noticed that food is moist, that seeds are moist, and that heat itself is generated from moisture. The guess is Aristotle's, but it shows the shape of the new thinking: a single material that can freeze, flow and evaporate, and so plausibly become many things. "All things are full of gods" sounds like old religion, but the example given is a magnet, so the claim may be that the power to move belongs to nature itself; no Olympian needs to be invoked.

Two anecdotes about Thales became the founding jokes of the subject. The first, Plato's story of the Thracian servant who laughed when he fell into a well while watching the stars, you met in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-philosophy.md). Aristotle tells the counter-story. Mocked for his poverty, Thales foresaw a big olive harvest, quietly rented every olive press in Miletus and Chios in winter, and then sublet them at his own price:

> raising a large sum of money by that means, convinced them that it was easy for philosophers to be rich if they chose it, but that that was not what they aimed at
>
> — Aristotle, *Politics* 1.11, 1259a (tr. William Ellis)

Philosophy has been arguing ever since about which story is the true portrait.

### Anaximander

Anaximander (c. 610–546 BCE), Thales' younger fellow citizen, wrote the first Greek prose book on nature. He saw a problem with water as the *archê*. The world is a balance of opposites: hot and cold, wet and dry. If one of them were the original stuff, it would long ago have swallowed the others. So the origin must be something neutral and unlimited, which he called the *apeiron*, "the boundless." The single sentence of his that survives, preserved by Simplicius from Theophrastus, is the oldest piece of Western philosophical prose:

> And into that from which things take their rise they pass away once more, "as is ordained; for they make reparation and satisfaction to one another for their injustice according to the appointed time," as he says in these somewhat poetical terms.
>
> — Anaximander, DK 12 B1, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 13

The language is legal: opposites commit "injustice" by encroaching on one another (summer's heat on winter's cold) and then pay the penalty. The world is a law-governed order, a *kosmos*, and the law is impersonal.

Anaximander also produced the first known argument from symmetry. According to Aristotle (*On the Heavens* 2.13, 295b10–16), he held that the earth hangs unsupported at the center of everything and stays put because it has no more reason to move one way than another. Compare Thales, whose earth floats on water, which invites the question of what holds up the water. Anaximander's answer needs no turtles all the way down. He also guessed that the first humans developed inside fish-like creatures, since a human infant is too helpless to have survived as the first of its kind. That is not Darwin, but it is a naturalistic argument about origins.

### Anaximenes

Anaximenes (fl. c. 546 BCE) chose air as the *archê*, which can look like a step backward from the boundless. It was in fact a step forward, because he supplied a *mechanism*. Air becomes fire when it is rarefied, and wind, cloud, water, earth and stone as it is condensed. Every qualitative difference becomes a difference of quantity, of how packed the stuff is. That idea, that quality is reducible to quantity, will reappear in the atomists, in Galileo, and in modern physics. He also drew an analogy between the cosmos and the living body:

> "Just as," he said, "our soul, being air, holds us together, so do breath and air encompass the whole world."
>
> — Anaximenes, DK 13 B2, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 25

| Thinker | *Archê* | What was new |
|---|---|---|
| Thales | water | a single natural origin; nature is self-moving ("full of gods") |
| Anaximander | the boundless (*apeiron*) | an abstract principle; cosmic justice; the earth held in place by symmetry |
| Anaximenes | air | a mechanism of change (rarefaction and condensation); quality explained by quantity |

## Pythagoras and Xenophanes: number and the critique of the gods

### Pythagoras

Pythagoras of Samos (c. 570–495 BCE) moved to Croton in southern Italy and founded a community that was part school, part religious order. He wrote nothing, the community kept its teachings secret, and later Platonists credited him with nearly everything, so almost nothing can be firmly attributed to him. The tradition holds two big ideas.

1. **The soul is immortal and migrates** from body to body, human and animal. Hence the ascetic rules, the vegetarianism and the famous ban on beans. The poet Xenophanes, a contemporary, made the first known joke at a philosopher's expense:

   > "Once, they say, he was passing by when a dog was being ill-treated. 'Stop!' he said, 'don't hit it! It is the soul of a friend! I knew it when I heard its voice.'"
   >
   > — Xenophanes, DK 21 B7, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 55

2. **Things are, or are structured by, number.** The Pythagoreans discovered that the musical concords correspond to simple ratios of string lengths: 2:1 for the octave, 3:2 for the fifth, 4:3 for the fourth. If harmony is ratio, perhaps the whole cosmos is ratio. That conjecture, that nature is written in mathematics, is the most successful bad guess in history. Galileo, Kepler and every physicist since have been Pythagoreans in this sense. (The legend that a Pythagorean was drowned for revealing that the diagonal of a square is incommensurable with its side is exactly that: a legend.)

### Xenophanes

Xenophanes of Colophon (c. 570–475 BCE) was a wandering poet who lived past ninety. He turned the new critical spirit on religion:

> Yes, and if oxen and horses or lions had hands, and could paint with their hands, and produce works of art as men do, horses would paint the forms of the gods like horses, and oxen like oxen, and make their bodies in the image of their several kinds.
>
> — Xenophanes, DK 21 B15, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy*

and

> The Ethiopians make their gods black and snub-nosed; the Thracians say theirs have blue eyes and red hair.
>
> — Xenophanes, DK 21 B16, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy*

This is the first known **projection argument**: if every people's gods look like that people, the images tell us about the believers, not the gods. Feuerbach and Freud would turn the same argument against religion as such (see [Chapter 14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md)). Xenophanes drew a different moral. He proposed "One god, the greatest among gods and men, neither in form like unto mortals nor in thought" (DK 21 B23), who moves all things "by the thought of his mind" without toil. He objected that Homer and Hesiod had ascribed to the gods theft, adultery and deceit (DK 21 B11), things shameful among humans. Behind the objection lies a principle every later theology accepts: what is divine must be worthy of worship.

He also wrote what is often read as the first statement of fallibilism, the view that we can have true beliefs without certain knowledge:

> There never was nor will be a man who has certain knowledge about the gods and about all the things I speak of. Even if he should chance to say the complete truth, yet he himself knows not that it is so. But all may have their fancy.
>
> — Xenophanes, DK 21 B34, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy*

Paired with this is a note of hope: "The gods have not revealed all things to men from the beginning, but by seeking they find in time what is better" (DK 21 B18). Inquiry yields progress, not certainty. Karl Popper, who admired Xenophanes, thought this the best two-line summary of scientific method ever written (see [Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

## Heraclitus: the logos and the river

Heraclitus of Ephesus (fl. c. 500 BCE) was already called "the Obscure" in antiquity. He wrote in compressed, oracular sentences, and he seems to have enjoyed it. Of the god at Delphi he wrote that he "neither utters nor hides his meaning, but shows it by a sign" (Bywater 11, DK B93). That is a good description of his own style. He was also famously rude: "The learning of many things teacheth not understanding, else would it have taught Hesiod and Pythagoras, and again Xenophanes and Hekataios" (Bywater 16, DK B40).

### The logos

His book opened with a claim about a *logos*, a word that means "account," "word," "reason" and "proportion" all at once:

> Though this Word is true evermore, yet men are as unable to understand it when they hear it for the first time as before they have heard it at all. For, though all things come to pass in accordance with this Word, men seem as if they had no experience of them
>
> — Heraclitus, Bywater 2 (DK B1), tr. Burnet

The *logos* is both Heraclitus' account and the structure of reality that the account describes. It is "common to all," as public as the law of a city, yet most people live "as if they had a wisdom of their own" (Bywater 92, DK B2). "The waking have one common world, but the sleeping turn aside each into a world of his own" (Bywater 95, DK B89). The Stoics took up this idea of a rational order running through the cosmos and made it the center of their physics (see [the Stoicism course, Chapter 2](../learn-stoicism/02-before-the-stoa.md) and [Chapter 4](../learn-stoicism/04-stoic-physics.md)). Through Philo it reached the opening of the Gospel of John: "In the beginning was the Word."

### The unity of opposites

What does the logos say? Its content is that opposites form a unity:

> Men do not know how what is at variance agrees with itself. It is an attunement of opposite tensions, like that of the bow and the lyre.
>
> — Heraclitus, Bywater 45 (DK B51), tr. Burnet

A strung bow looks still, but it is held still by two forces pulling in opposite directions; slacken either and it stops being a bow. Heraclitus offers many such cases. "The way up and the way down is one and the same" (Bywater 69, DK B60): the same road is uphill or downhill depending on which way you walk. "The sea is the purest and the impurest water. Fish can drink it, and it is good for them; to men it is undrinkable and destructive" (Bywater 52, DK B61). Some opposites are relative to a perspective (good for fish, bad for people). Some succeed one another (day and night). Some are aspects of a single process (the road). Heraclitus' general thesis is that strife between opposites is not a defect of the world but the condition of its existence:

> We must know that war is common to all and strife is justice, and that all things come into being and pass away (?) through strife.
>
> — Heraclitus, Bywater 62 (DK B80), tr. Burnet

This is a deliberate reply to Anaximander, for whom the encroachment of opposites was an "injustice" needing repair. For Heraclitus the strife *is* the justice.

### Fire and flux

Heraclitus' *archê*, if he had one, was fire:

> This world, which is the same for all, no one of gods or men has made; but it was ever, is now, and ever shall be an ever-living Fire, with measures kindling, and measures going out.
>
> — Heraclitus, Bywater 20 (DK B30), tr. Burnet

Fire is a good emblem for his world because a flame is not a thing but a process: it keeps its shape only by constantly consuming fuel and giving off smoke. Note "measures": the change is lawful and the amounts balance, "even as wares for gold and gold for wares" (Bywater 22, DK B90).

Then there is the river, the most famous image in Presocratic philosophy:

> You cannot step twice into the same rivers; for fresh waters are ever flowing in upon you.
>
> — Heraclitus, Bywater 41–42 (DK B91, cf. B12), tr. Burnet

Scholars dispute what Heraclitus actually wrote. The version best attested as his own words (DK B12) says, roughly, that upon those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow. On that reading the point is subtler than "everything changes." The river *stays the same river* precisely because its waters change; a river whose water stopped flowing would be a lake. Identity is maintained through change, like the flame and the bow. Plato read Heraclitus as teaching universal flux, and one of Heraclitus' followers, Cratylus, reportedly concluded that you cannot step into the same river even once and in the end gave up speaking and merely wagged his finger (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 4.5, 1010a10–15). That is where flux becomes a reason to despair of knowledge, and it drove Plato toward unchanging Forms (see [Chapter 5](05-plato.md)).

Two more fragments are worth carrying around. "Nature loves to hide" (Bywater 10, DK B123): the real structure of things is not on the surface. And "Man's character is his fate" (Bywater 121, DK B119): the Greek *êthos anthrôpôi daimôn* says that a person's guiding spirit, their *daimôn*, is nothing but their own character.

## Parmenides: what is, is

If Heraclitus is the philosopher of change, Parmenides of Elea (fl. c. 480 BCE) is the philosopher who argued that change is impossible. He wrote in hexameter verse, the meter of Homer. His poem begins with a chariot ride to a goddess, who promises to teach him "the unshaken heart of well-rounded truth" and then "the opinions of mortals in which is no true belief at all." The surviving poem is therefore in two parts, the Way of Truth and the Way of Opinion.

What makes Parmenides a turning point is that the goddess does not simply reveal. She *argues*, and she tells him to "judge by argument the much disputed proof uttered by me" (DK B7). Here is the core:

> The first, namely, that *It is*, and that it is impossible for it not to be, is the way of belief, for truth is its companion. The other, namely, that *It is not*, and that it must needs not be,—that, I tell thee, is a path that none can learn of at all. For thou canst not know what is not—that is impossible—nor utter it; for it is the same thing that can be thought and that can be.
>
> — Parmenides, DK 28 B2–B3, tr. Burnet

The subject of "It is" is never named, which is part of the puzzle. On one influential reading it is "whatever can be inquired into." Put in the standard form of [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md), the argument runs:

1. Whatever can be thought of or spoken of must be (it must be *something*).
2. What is not is nothing.
3. So what is not cannot be thought of or spoken of.
4. Coming into being would mean coming from what is not; passing away would mean passing into what is not.
5. So nothing comes into being or passes away.
6. Change, motion, plurality and empty space would all require what-is-not somewhere: a time when a thing was not yet, a place where it is not, a gap between one thing and another.
7. So what is is ungenerated, imperishable, one, continuous and motionless.

From the first premise the goddess derives a whole list of results:

> One path only is left for us to speak of, namely, that *It is*. In it are very many tokens that what is is uncreated and indestructible; for it is complete, immovable, and without end. Nor was it ever, nor will it be; for now *it is*, all at once, a continuous one.
>
> — Parmenides, DK 28 B8.1–6, tr. Burnet

The conclusion is preposterous. Your senses tell you that things move and change; Parmenides says that is a mere name. Mortals give names to "coming into being and passing away, being and not being, change of place and alteration of bright colour" (B8.38–41), and the names are empty. Of all the philosophical traditions, only a few in India (see [Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) on Advaita Vedanta) have been as willing to follow an argument to a conclusion that denies the evidence of the senses.

Why take him seriously? Because the premises are hard to deny, and the history of philosophy for the next century is a series of attempts to find the flaw.

- **The flaw might be premise 1.** It seems to say that to think *of* something you must think of something that exists. But we think of unicorns, and we say true things like "Pegasus does not exist." How? That puzzle about *negative existentials* is still alive: Plato attacked it in the *Sophist*, and Russell's theory of descriptions (1905) is a direct descendant (see [Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)).
- **The flaw might be the ambiguity of "is."** "Is" can mean "exists" (*there is a cat*), "is identical to" (*Cicero is Tully*), or predication (*the cat is black*). Parmenides seems to slide between them. If what-is-not means "what is not black," it is easy to think and talk about.
- **The flaw might be premise 6.** The atomists, as we will see, accepted almost everything else and denied only that empty space is impossible.

Parmenides' lasting contributions are more important than his conclusion. He made **pure deductive argument** a way of finding out about reality. He drew a sharp line between **reason and the senses**, the line on which rationalism will later stand (see [Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). And he turned **being** itself into a subject of inquiry, which Aristotle would later name "first philosophy" and his editors "metaphysics."

## Zeno's paradoxes

Zeno of Elea (born c. 490 BCE) was Parmenides' student, and in Plato's *Parmenides* he explains his purpose:

> The truth is, that these writings of mine were meant to protect the arguments of Parmenides against those who make fun of him and seek to show the many ridiculous and contradictory results which they suppose to follow from the affirmation of the one. My answer is addressed to the partisans of the many, whose attack I return with interest
>
> — Plato, *Parmenides* 128c–d (tr. Jowett)

The strategy is *reductio ad absurdum* (see [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md)): assume your opponent's view, that there are many things and that they move, and derive a contradiction. Zeno reportedly wrote forty such arguments. Four about motion survive in Aristotle's *Physics* 6.9, 239b5–240a18. Burnet gives them crisply.

**The Dichotomy (the racecourse).**

> You cannot traverse an infinite number of points in a finite time. You must traverse the half of any given distance before you traverse the whole, and the half of that again before you can traverse it. This goes on *ad infinitum*
>
> — Zeno, as reported by Aristotle, *Physics* 239b11, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 163

**Achilles and the tortoise.**

> Achilles will never overtake the tortoise. He must first reach the place from which the tortoise started. By that time the tortoise will have got some way ahead. Achilles must then make up that, and again the tortoise will be ahead. He is always coming nearer, but he never makes up to it.
>
> — Zeno, as reported by Aristotle, *Physics* 239b14, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 163

**The Arrow.**

> The arrow in flight is at rest. For, if everything is at rest when it occupies a space equal to itself, and what is in flight at any given moment always occupies a space equal to itself, it cannot move.
>
> — Zeno, as reported by Aristotle, *Physics* 239b30, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 163

**The Stadium (moving rows).** Three rows of bodies: one at rest, two moving past it in opposite directions at equal speeds. Each moving row passes twice as many bodies of the other moving row as of the stationary one in the same time, so "half the time may be equal to double the time." This one is usually thought the weakest, because it seems to ignore relative velocity. If time and space come in indivisible minimum units, though, it bites: a body passing two units in one minimal instant must have been halfway past at some time *between* instants, and there is no such time.

The paradoxes work as a dilemma. If space and time are **infinitely divisible**, the Dichotomy and the Achilles seem to show that motion requires completing infinitely many tasks. If they are made of **indivisible minimum units**, the Arrow and the Stadium cause trouble. Either way, motion looks impossible.

| Paradox | Assumption attacked | Standard modern reply |
|---|---|---|
| Dichotomy | space infinitely divisible | an infinite series can have a finite sum: ½ + ¼ + ⅛ + … = 1 |
| Achilles | same | the same, plus simple algebra: you can calculate exactly where Achilles passes the tortoise |
| Arrow | time made of instants | motion is defined *over* intervals, not *at* instants; velocity at an instant is a limit (calculus) |
| Stadium | space and time made of minimal units | if there are no minimal units, the problem does not arise; if there are, it is a real problem |

The mathematical reply (sums of convergent series, Cauchy and Weierstrass's rigorous theory of limits in the nineteenth century, Cantor's theory of infinite sets) tells you *that* the total distance is finite and *where* Achilles catches up. Philosophers still argue about whether it answers Zeno's actual question: how can anyone *complete*, one after another, an infinite sequence of acts? Aristotle had his own answer (*Physics* 8.8, 263a–b): a line is only *potentially* divisible into infinitely many parts, and the runner does not actually perform infinitely many separate runs unless she stops at each point. Modern discussions of "supertasks" keep the question alive. One example is Thomson's lamp (1954): switch a lamp on and off at 1 minute, ½ minute, ¼ minute… until the two minutes are up. Is it on or off at the end?

Zeno has the rare distinction of being refuted by a dog. Diogenes the Cynic, according to a later story, answered the arguments against motion by standing up and walking away. That is a good joke and a bad argument. Zeno never doubted that things *appear* to move; he challenged anyone to give a consistent account of how they can.

## The pluralists: Empedocles and Anaxagoras

After Parmenides, anyone who wanted to save the world of change had to concede something. The pluralists conceded a great deal. They agreed that nothing comes into being from nothing and nothing perishes into nothing. What we call birth and death, they said, is only the mixing and separating of permanent ingredients.

### Empedocles

Empedocles of Acragas in Sicily (c. 495–435 BCE) was a poet, physician, politician and self-proclaimed god, and the legend has him leaping into the crater of Etna to prove his immortality (Diogenes Laertius 8.69). He named the permanent ingredients:

> Hear first the four roots of all things: shining Zeus, life-bringing Hera, Aidoneus, and Nestis whose tear-drops are a well-spring to mortals.
>
> — Empedocles, DK 31 B6, tr. Burnet

Under the divine names are fire, air (or earth; the identifications are disputed), earth and water. These four "roots" became the four elements of Western science for two thousand years. Two forces move them: **Love**, which mixes unlike things, and **Strife**, which separates them. The cosmos cycles endlessly between total mixture under Love and total separation under Strife:

> At one time it grew to be one only out of many; at another, it divided up to be many instead of one.
>
> — Empedocles, DK 31 B17.1–2, tr. Burnet

Empedocles also sketched a theory of the origin of living things that Aristotle later criticized and Darwin noticed. Random combinations of limbs and organs arose by chance, and only those that happened to fit together survived (Aristotle, *Physics* 2.8, 198b29–32). It is not natural selection, but it is the first attempt to explain apparent design without a designer.

### Anaxagoras

Anaxagoras of Clazomenae (c. 500–428 BCE) brought philosophy to Athens, where he was a friend of Pericles and was eventually prosecuted for impiety for calling the sun a red-hot stone. His ingredient list was unlimited. How can bread become hair and flesh? "How can hair come from what is not hair, or flesh from what is not flesh?" (DK 59 B10). His answer was that every kind of stuff is already present in everything else, in tiny amounts:

> In everything there is a portion of everything except Nous, and there are some things in which there is Nous also.
>
> — Anaxagoras, DK 59 B11, tr. Burnet

The exception is *Nous*, "Mind," which is unmixed, "infinite and self-ruled," and "has all knowledge about everything and the greatest strength" (B12). Mind set the original mixture spinning, and the vortex sorted things out into the world we see.

That was the first appearance of mind as a cosmic cause in Greek philosophy, and the young Socrates was thrilled by it. In the *Phaedo*, on his last day, he tells his friends what happened next:

> Then I heard some one reading, as he said, from a book of Anaxagoras, that mind was the disposer and cause of all, and I was delighted at this notion, which appeared quite admirable, and I said to myself: If mind is the disposer, mind will dispose all for the best, and put each particular in the best place
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 97b–c (tr. Jowett)

He bought the book and was bitterly disappointed. Anaxagoras used Mind only to start the vortex, and then explained everything by "air, and ether, and water, and other eccentricities." That, says Socrates, is like explaining why he is sitting in prison by describing his bones and muscles, forgetting the true cause:

> that the Athenians have thought fit to condemn me, and accordingly I have thought it better and more right to remain here and undergo my sentence; for I am inclined to think that these muscles and bones of mine would have gone off long ago to Megara or Boeotia--by the dog they would
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 98e–99a (tr. Jowett)

Socrates distinguishes the *cause* (his judgment about what is best) from the *condition* without which the cause could not operate (his bones and muscles). The passage marks a fork in the road of Western thought. One path explains things by their purposes: Plato, Aristotle's "final cause," and most medieval science. The other explains them by the mechanical behavior of their parts: the atomists, then Galileo, Descartes and Newton. Whether reasons and purposes can be reduced to mechanisms is still the central question of the philosophy of mind (see [Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)).

## The atomists: atoms and the void

Leucippus (fl. c. 440 BCE, so obscure that Epicurus doubted he existed) and his student Democritus of Abdera (c. 460–370 BCE) gave the most economical answer to Parmenides. They accepted that what *is* cannot come into being or pass away, that it is full and changeless, and that it cannot be divided. But instead of one such being there are infinitely many, too small to see: *atoma*, "uncuttables." And they took the step the Eleatics had forbidden. What-is-not, empty space, the void, exists too. Atoms move through the void and combine into the things we see:

> That atoms and the vacuum were the beginning of the universe; and that everything else existed only in opinion. That the worlds were infinite, created, and perishable. But that nothing was created out of nothing, and that nothing was destroyed so as to become nothing.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius 9.44, on Democritus (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Atoms differ only in shape, size, arrangement and position, as the letters A and N differ in shape, AN and NA in order, and Z and N in position (Aristotle, *Metaphysics* 1.4, 985b13–19). Everything else (color, taste, warmth) belongs not to the atoms but to the way atoms affect perceivers. The only surviving sentence of Leucippus states the determinism that follows:

> "Naught happens for nothing," he said "but everything from a ground and of necessity."
>
> — Leucippus, DK 67 B2, in Burnet, *Early Greek Philosophy* § 178

### Convention and nature

Democritus drew the epistemological conclusion. In a fragment preserved by Sextus Empiricus (DK 68 B9), he says that sweet, bitter, hot, cold and color exist "by convention" (*nomôi*), and in reality there are only atoms and void. Diogenes Laertius paraphrases:

> what is cold is cold in opinion, and what is hot is hot in opinion; but atoms and the vacuum exist in reality. And again he says: "But we know nothing really; for truth lies in the bottom."
>
> — Diogenes Laertius 9.72, on Democritus (DK 68 B117) (tr. Yonge)

This is the ancestor of the distinction between **primary qualities**, such as shape and motion, which belong to things themselves, and **secondary qualities**, such as color and taste, which are powers to produce sensations in us. Galileo drew it in 1623 and Locke made it famous (see [Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)). It is also the ancestor of a worry. If the senses report only "convention," what is the evidence for atoms? Galen preserves Democritus' reply, a little dialogue in which the senses rebuke the mind: wretched mind, you take your evidence from us and then try to overthrow us, but our overthrow will be your fall (DK 68 B125, paraphrased). A theory that undermines the evidence for itself is in trouble. That problem has followed scientific realism ever since (see [Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)).

### A cheerful materialism

Democritus was also the first Greek philosopher to leave a large body of ethics, mostly lost. Later tradition called him "the laughing philosopher." Diogenes Laertius says his highest good was *euthymia*, "cheerfulness":

> a condition according to which the soul lives calmly and steadily, being disturbed by no fear, or superstition, or other passion.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius 9.45 (tr. Yonge)

A century later Epicurus would combine Democritus' physics with this ideal of tranquility into a full philosophy of life (see [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)). The Roman poet Lucretius put the whole system into verse. His image of atoms is dust in a sunbeam:

> for behold whenever The sun's light and the rays, let in, pour down Across dark halls of houses: thou wilt see The many mites in many a manner mixed Amid a void in the very light of the rays, And battling on, as in eternal strife
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* 2.114–119 (tr. William Ellery Leonard)

In 1905 Einstein's analysis of Brownian motion, the jittering of pollen grains struck by invisible molecules, provided the decisive evidence that atoms exist. The atomists were right about atoms, more or less, for the wrong reasons. Their reasons were Parmenidean arguments about being and not-being, not experiments. That is a lesson worth thinking about.

## What the Presocratics invented

Take away the wrong answers, and the Presocratics left a toolkit that has been in continuous use since.

- **Naturalism**: explain nature by nature. Even Thales' gods are in the magnet, not on Olympus.
- **Unity beneath variety**: the search for one or a few principles that explain many phenomena, still the ideal of physics.
- **Criticism as method**: each thinker argues against his predecessor, and the conversation advances. Popper suggested that this "tradition of critical discussion" is the single most important Greek invention.
- **Pure argument**: Parmenides and Zeno show that reasoning alone can reach startling conclusions, and that the conclusions sometimes show the premises are confused.
- **Appearance and reality**: Heraclitus' hidden nature, Parmenides' Way of Opinion and Democritus' "convention" all distinguish how things seem from how they are.
- **Conservation**: "Nothing from nothing." Lucretius' version reads "Nothing from nothing ever yet was born" (1.150). It is the ancestor of every conservation law.

They also left open problems that the rest of this course takes up: the relation of the one to the many, of change to identity, of mind to matter, of reason to sense, of purpose to mechanism.

## Living it

- **Practice the Milesian move.** Pick a messy domain (your inbox, a city's traffic, your moods) and ask what single underlying principle could explain most of it. Then ask the next Milesian's question: what would your principle fail to explain?
- **Use Xenophanes as a mirror.** When you picture something beyond experience (God, the far future, a "good life"), ask how much of the picture is a portrait of yourself.
- **Find the bow in a conflict.** Heraclitus says some tensions hold things together. A department, a marriage or a team may be held in shape by two pulls; removing one could collapse the whole. Before "resolving" a tension, ask what it is holding up.
- **Run a Zeno drill.** When an argument reaches an absurd conclusion from plausible premises, do not just reject the conclusion. Find the premise to give up, as the atomists did with Parmenides.
- **Separate cause and condition.** In an explanation of someone's action, ask whether it gives their reasons (Socrates' "true cause") or only the machinery (his bones and muscles). Both are real; they answer different questions.

## Summary

- The Presocratics survive only in fragments and testimonies, mostly filtered through Aristotle and the doxographers. Cite by Diels–Kranz numbers and read every report with its reporter's agenda in mind.
- The Milesians sought the *archê*: Thales water, Anaximander the boundless (with impersonal cosmic justice and an argument from symmetry), Anaximenes air (with a mechanism of rarefaction and condensation).
- Pythagoreans linked number, harmony and the soul. Xenophanes attacked anthropomorphic gods with a projection argument and stated fallibilism.
- Heraclitus taught a common logos, the unity of opposites, strife as justice, fire as measured process, and the river whose identity depends on change.
- Parmenides argued deductively that what-is cannot come to be, change or be many. Diagnosing his error (about thinking of what-is-not, or about the ambiguity of "is") has occupied philosophy ever since.
- Zeno defended him with paradoxes of motion. Calculus answers *where* Achilles passes the tortoise; whether it answers how an infinite series of acts can be completed is still debated.
- Empedocles (four roots, Love and Strife) and Anaxagoras (a portion of everything in everything; cosmic Mind) saved change by making it the mixture of permanent ingredients. Socrates' disappointment with Anaxagoras marks the fork between purposive and mechanical explanation.
- Leucippus and Democritus answered Parmenides with atoms moving in a void. Their distinction between convention and reality anticipates primary and secondary qualities, and Democritean cheerfulness anticipates Epicurus.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether you can step into the same river twice

**Socrates:** You walk along the same river every morning, do you not? But Heraclitus says the waters are always different. So is it the same river or a different one?

**You:** It's the same river. The water changes, but the river doesn't.

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Socrates:** Then the river is not the water?

**You:** No. It's the river that has that water in it right now.

**Socrates:** What is it, then? The banks? The bed?

**You:** Partly. But the banks erode and the bed shifts over the years. Engineers can move a river's course entirely.

**Socrates:** So it is not the water, not the banks, and not the bed. Is it anything at all, or only a name, as Parmenides would say?

**You:** It's a pattern: water flowing in roughly this channel, in this direction, fed by these sources.

**Socrates:** A pattern that persists while all its materials change. Then you agree with Heraclitus more than you thought. The river is the same *because* the waters are different. If the water stopped flowing, would it still be a river?

**You:** No, it would be a lake, or a swamp.

**Socrates:** Now take yourself. The matter of your body is replaced slowly, cell by cell. Are you the same person who was born under your name?

**You:** I want to say yes, for the same reason: I'm a pattern, not a lump of stuff.

**Socrates:** And if the pattern changed (your memories, your character, your loves) while the matter stayed the same?

**You:** That seems worse. If my character were replaced I'd be gone, even with the same body.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps Heraclitus was right twice over: that we are rivers, and that "man's character is his fate." But tell me one more thing. If a pattern can change gradually into a very different pattern, at what point does the river become a different river?

**You:** I don't think there is a sharp point.

**Socrates:** Then "same" may be a word with blurry edges. Keep hold of that; you will need it when you meet the ship of Theseus and the problem of personal identity.

*What we learned:* Identity through change can belong to a pattern or process rather than to stuff, which is the reading of Heraclitus on which the river stays the same because its waters change. That moves the puzzle to the identity of patterns, which may be vague. See [Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) on personal identity.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether you can think about what is not

**Parmenides:** Tell me, young friend, can you think about nothing at all?

**You:** Sure. I'm thinking about unicorns right now, and unicorns don't exist.

*Your turn: answer Parmenides in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Parmenides:** When you think of a unicorn, is your thought about something, or about nothing?

**You:** About something: a white horse with a horn.

**Parmenides:** Then it is not about nothing. The horse is something, the horn is something, the white is something. You have only combined things that are.

**You:** Fine. But the unicorn itself doesn't exist, and I can still say "there are no unicorns." That's a true sentence about what is not.

**Parmenides:** Is it? What is the sentence about? If it is about unicorns, then there must be unicorns for it to be about.

**You:** Maybe it isn't about unicorns. Maybe it's about the world: it says that nothing in the world is a horned horse.

**Parmenides:** Ingenious. You have turned "unicorns are not" into "everything that is, is not a unicorn." Your sentence now speaks only of what is.

**You:** Exactly. So I can say "there are no unicorns" without referring to anything that doesn't exist.

**Parmenides:** Then do the same for empty space. When you say "there is a void between these two atoms," what is the sentence about?

**You:** It says that there's no body between them.

**Parmenides:** And yet the atomists treat the void as a *something*: it has size, it lies between, things move through it. Is it nothing or something?

**You:** Maybe it's a something that isn't a body. Space, not matter.

**Parmenides:** Then you have not refuted me. You have agreed that what-is-not cannot be, and renamed my what-is "body." Leucippus said that "is-not" is as real as "is." Did he mean nothing is something?

**You:** I think he meant that there's more than one way to be.

**Parmenides:** Then you must say what "is" means before you argue with me. Most do not.

*What we learned:* Parmenides' premise that one cannot think or speak of what is not survives easy counterexamples. One modern strategy, Russell's, rewrites negative existentials as general claims about what exists. Another distinguishes senses of "is." Both are ways of diagnosing his argument rather than ignoring it. See [Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md).

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether Achilles can catch the tortoise

**Zeno:** You laugh at my Achilles. Then tell me: before he catches the tortoise, must he first reach the place where the tortoise was?

**You:** Yes, but that doesn't matter. The gaps get smaller so fast that they add up to a finite distance. I can work out exactly where he passes it.

*Your turn: answer Zeno in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Zeno:** Tell me how you work it out.

**You:** Say Achilles runs ten times as fast and the tortoise starts 100 meters ahead. The gaps are 100, 10, 1, 0.1 and so on. The sum is 111.11… meters, which is finite. He passes it just past 111 meters.

**Zeno:** I grant the sum. I even grant that he passes it there. But I did not ask *where*. I asked *how*. How many stages has he completed when he reaches that point?

**You:** Infinitely many.

**Zeno:** And each stage must be completed before the next one begins?

**You:** Yes.

**Zeno:** Then name the last stage he completes before he catches up.

**You:** There isn't one. For every stage there's a next one.

**Zeno:** So he completes a series of acts that has no last member. Have you ever finished a task with no final step?

**You:** But the stages aren't separate acts. He doesn't stop and decide to run each one. He just runs.

**Zeno:** That is Aristotle's answer: the stages are only potential divisions, not actual ones. Then tell me, if he did stop for a moment at each point, would he never arrive?

**You:** If each stop took a fixed time, he'd never arrive. If the stops got shorter fast enough, he would.

**Zeno:** Then the paradox lives on in your own imagination. A runner who pauses at every point would complete infinitely many pauses in finite time, and you cannot say which one is last.

**You:** So the math tells me the answer, but not what it means to finish an infinite series.

**Zeno:** That is all I ever claimed. I did not deny that things appear to move. I asked whether your account of motion is consistent.

*What we learned:* The convergent-series reply shows that the distance and time are finite and locates the meeting point. The deeper question is whether an infinite sequence of acts can be completed, which is the question of "supertasks." Aristotle's distinction between potential and actual infinity is one answer.

</details>
