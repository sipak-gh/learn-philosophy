# Chapter 1 — What Is Philosophy?

Philosophy is the oldest of the disciplines and the hardest to define. It has no laboratory, no agreed body of results, and no single method, yet every science began inside it, and every serious life runs into its questions sooner or later: What is real? What can I know? What should I do? What is worth wanting? This chapter explains where the word comes from, what makes a question *philosophical*, how the field divides into branches, why the study has been thought worth a life (and, in one famous case, worth a death), and how to read a philosophical text so that it reads you back. It ends with a practice and three dialogues. Everything later in this course, and in the sibling courses on [Stoicism](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md), [nihilism](../learn-nihilism/01-what-is-nihilism.md) and [ethics](../learn-ethics/01-what-is-ethics.md), builds on the habits introduced here.

## A word that confesses ignorance

The Greek word *philosophia* joins *philos* ("loving, fond of") and *sophia* ("wisdom," originally skill or expertise of any kind: a good carpenter had *sophia*). So a philosopher is a *lover* of wisdom, not a possessor of it. The ancient biographers liked to say that the word was coined on purpose, as an act of modesty. Diogenes Laertius, writing a compilation of philosophers' lives in roughly the third century CE, reports the story:

> But Pythagoras was the first person who invented the term Philosophy, and who called himself a philosopher; when he was conversing at Sicyon with Leon, who was tyrant of the Sicyonians or of the Phliasians (as Heraclides Ponticus relates in the book which he wrote about a dead woman); for he said that no man ought to be called wise, but only God.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 1.12 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Later in the same book he adds the comparison that made the story famous: life is like the great festival at Olympia.

> And adds, that he used to compare life to a festival. "And as some people came to a festival to contend for the prizes, and others for the purposes of traffic, and the best as spectators; so also in life, the men of slavish dispositions," said he, "are born hunters after glory and covetousness, but philosophers are seekers after truth."
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 8.8 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

Treat the anecdote with suspicion. Heraclides of Pontus, the source, was a pupil of Plato who wrote philosophical fiction, and most scholars (for example Walter Burkert, in a classic 1960 article in *Hermes*) think the "Pythagoras coined the word" story is a back-projection of Plato's own ideal of the philosopher as one who *strives* for a wisdom only a god fully has. That is the first lesson of this course in miniature: a good story is not yet evidence, and the history of philosophy is full of good stories.

What survives the skepticism is the self-understanding the story expresses. A philosopher, on this picture, is defined by a relation to something he or she lacks. Plato makes the point explicitly in the *Symposium* (204a): the gods do not do philosophy, because they are already wise; the utterly ignorant do not do it either, because they do not know they lack anything. Philosophy happens in between, in the person who knows enough to know that he does not know. You will meet this idea again in [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) as Socratic ignorance, and in [Chapter 5](05-plato.md) as Platonic *eros*.

## Wonder, and a woman laughing

Where does philosophy start? Plato's answer, put into the mouth of Socrates talking to a gifted teenage mathematician, is famous:

> SOCRATES: I see, my dear Theaetetus, that Theodorus had a true insight into your nature when he said that you were a philosopher, for wonder is the feeling of a philosopher, and philosophy begins in wonder.
>
> — Plato, *Theaetetus* 155d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

The Greek word is *thaumazein*, "to wonder, to marvel, to be astonished." Aristotle repeats the thought at the beginning of his *Metaphysics* (982b12): people first began to philosophize, and still do, because of wonder, first at obvious puzzles and then at greater ones such as the phases of the moon and the origin of the universe. Notice what *thaumazein* is and is not. It is not vague awe at a sunset. In the *Theaetetus* it is the dizziness of a young man who has just been shown that he cannot explain how six dice can be "more" than four dice and "fewer" than twelve without the dice themselves changing. Wonder, in the philosopher's sense, is the moment when something ordinary becomes strange because you notice you do not understand it.

Russell opens his own little introduction with the same move. He looks at the table he is writing on:

> Is there any knowledge in the world which is so certain that no reasonable man could doubt it? This question, which at first sight might not seem difficult, is really one of the most difficult that can be asked.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. I

The table looks brown here and almost white where the light glints; it feels hard but is mostly empty space according to physics; it looks rectangular from no actual position in the room. So which is the *real* table? A few pages later, Russell has you wondering whether there is a table at all apart from your experiences. That is philosophy: the ordinary, looked at hard enough to wobble.

The same dialogue preserves the oldest philosopher joke. Thales of Miletus, the man Aristotle calls the founder of natural philosophy (see [Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)), was walking at night with his eyes on the stars:

> SOCRATES: I will illustrate my meaning, Theodorus, by the jest which the clever witty Thracian handmaid is said to have made about Thales, when he fell into a well as he was looking up at the stars. She said, that he was so eager to know what was going on in heaven, that he could not see what was before his feet. This is a jest which is equally applicable to all philosophers.
>
> — Plato, *Theaetetus* 174a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

It is worth pausing on who gets the last laugh. In the "digression" that follows (172c–177c), Socrates turns the joke around: the philosopher is clumsy in the law court, but put the lawyer in front of the question "What is justice itself?" and he is the one who stammers and gets dizzy. The German philosopher Hans Blumenberg wrote a whole book, *The Laughter of the Thracian Woman* (1987), tracing how later ages retold this scene, sometimes siding with Thales, sometimes with the servant. You should keep both sides of the joke in view all through this course. Philosophy that never looks at the ground is useless; a life that never looks up is small.

## What makes a question philosophical?

There is no definition of philosophy that all philosophers accept, and it is a sign of the subject that the question "What is philosophy?" is itself a philosophical question. But you can get a working grip by looking at the *kinds* of question philosophers ask. Compare:

| Question | Kind | Who answers it? |
|----------|------|-----------------|
| How many people in this city were convicted of theft last year? | empirical, descriptive | statisticians |
| Does punishment reduce crime? | empirical, causal | criminologists |
| Is it ever just to punish someone who did not intend the harm? | normative | philosophers (and everyone) |
| What *is* punishment, as opposed to revenge or quarantine? | conceptual | philosophers |
| Can anyone deserve anything, if all our actions have prior causes? | foundational, metaphysical | philosophers |

The bottom three questions share three features.

1. **They are not settled by collecting more data alone.** Data matter, but you could know every fact about every trial and still disagree about whether the punishments were *just*. Philosophical questions typically ask about *concepts* (what is knowledge? what is a cause?), about *norms* (what ought I to believe, to do, to value?), or about the *foundations* that other inquiries take for granted (does the past give any reason to expect the future?).
2. **They are general and reflexive.** They concern the framework within which ordinary questions are asked. A physicist asks what caused an event; a philosopher asks what causation is, and whether we know it exists.
3. **They are live for a reflective person.** You cannot opt out of having *some* answer. Even if you never think about whether lying can be justified, your behavior commits you to a view.

Russell gives a historical twist to the first feature. Philosophy looks as if it never solves anything, he says, partly because whenever it does, the solved part leaves home:

> It is true that this is partly accounted for by the fact that, as soon as definite knowledge concerning any subject becomes possible, this subject ceases to be called philosophy, and becomes a separate science.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. XV

Newton's great book was called *Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy* (1687). Psychology split off from philosophy in the late nineteenth century; logic became partly a branch of mathematics in the twentieth; linguistics and computer science both grew out of philosophical work on meaning and computation. On this "residue" view, philosophy is the nursery of the sciences, and what remains in it at any moment are the questions we do not yet know how to answer systematically.

### Does philosophy make progress?

Here is the strongest objection, stated as an argument:

1. A discipline makes progress only if, over time, its experts converge on answers to its central questions.
2. Philosophers have not converged on answers to the central questions of philosophy (free will, the nature of mind, the foundations of morality) in 2,500 years.
3. So philosophy has not made progress.

David Chalmers set out a version of this argument in "Why Isn't There More Progress in Philosophy?" (*Philosophy*, 2015), drawing on the 2009 PhilPapers Survey, in which professional philosophers split widely on almost every major question. Three replies are worth having:

- **Deny premise 1.** Progress can be negative (we have learned which answers *fail*, as with the "justified true belief" analysis of knowledge you will meet in [Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)), or it can consist in clearer distinctions (between validity and soundness, between the content of a belief and its justification). Williamson argues that philosophy progresses the way other theoretical fields do: through better models, more rigorous arguments, and the elimination of options, even without consensus.
- **The residue reply** (Russell's): the sciences are philosophy's success stories, which then change their names.
- **The way-of-life reply** (Hadot's, discussed below): for the ancient schools, the goal was not a set of results but a transformed person. Measured by that standard, "progress" is something that happens in a life, not in a journal.

None of these replies is fully satisfying, and you will see the debate again in [Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) and [Chapter 24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md). For now, note the method: the objection was stated as premises and a conclusion, and each reply targeted a premise. That is how philosophers argue, and [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md) will make it a skill.

## The branches of philosophy

The ancient Stoics divided philosophy into three parts, logic, physics and ethics, and compared it to an egg (logic the shell, ethics the white, physics the yolk) or to a garden (logic the fence, physics the soil and trees, ethics the fruit). The [Stoicism course, Chapter 3](../learn-stoicism/03-the-early-stoa.md) explores those images. The modern map has more regions but the same three continents: how we reason, what there is, and how to live.

| Branch | Greek root | Core question | Sample problem | Where in this course |
|--------|-----------|---------------|----------------|----------------------|
| **Logic** | *logos*, reason, word | What follows from what? | Is "every event has a cause, so something causes every event" valid? | [Ch. 2](02-logic-and-argument.md), [Ch. 6](06-aristotle.md), [Ch. 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Metaphysics** | *ta meta ta physika*, "the things after the physics" | What is there, and what is it like? | Are numbers real? Is time? Is there free will? | [Ch. 3](03-the-presocratics.md), [Ch. 5](05-plato.md), [Ch. 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Epistemology** | *epistēmē*, knowledge | What can we know, and how? | Can you know you are not dreaming? | [Ch. 12](12-the-empiricists.md), [Ch. 13](13-kant.md), [Ch. 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Ethics** | *ēthos*, character | How should one live? What is good, right, virtuous? | Is it wrong to break a promise to prevent a harm? | [the Ethics course](../learn-ethics/01-what-is-ethics.md), [Ch. 16](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md) |
| **Political philosophy** | *polis*, city | What makes power legitimate? What is justice? | Do you have a duty to obey an unjust law? | [Ch. 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md), [Ch. 22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Aesthetics** | *aisthēsis*, perception | What is beauty? What is art for? | Can a tragedy be good because it makes us suffer? | [Ch. 5](05-plato.md), [Ch. 6](06-aristotle.md) |
| **Philosophy of mind** | | What is a mind? How does it relate to the body? | Could a machine feel pain? | [Ch. 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Philosophy of religion** | | Does God exist? Can faith be rational? | Does evil disprove a good God? | [Ch. 9](09-faith-and-reason.md) |

A few notes on names. "Metaphysics" is an accident of library science: when Andronicus of Rhodes edited Aristotle's works in the first century BCE, he placed a set of treatises on being, substance and first causes *after* (*meta*) the books on nature (*physika*). Aristotle himself called the subject "first philosophy" or "wisdom." "Epistemology" is a nineteenth-century coinage (usually credited to the Scottish philosopher J. F. Ferrier, 1854). Ethics divides into **normative ethics** (what is right and good), **metaethics** (what moral claims *mean* and whether they can be true, the territory of the [nihilism course's chapter on error theory](../learn-nihilism/12-moral-nihilism-and-error-theory.md)), and **applied ethics** (abortion, animals, AI). Then there is a "philosophy of X" for almost every X: science, mathematics, language, law, history, biology, physics, technology. Each asks the conceptual and foundational questions that X's practitioners take for granted.

The map is a European one, and it does not carve every tradition at its joints. Classical Indian thought organizes itself into *darśanas* ("viewpoints" or "ways of seeing"), each typically combining a metaphysics, an account of valid means of knowledge (*pramāṇa*) and a path to liberation. Classical Chinese thought centers on the *dao* (way) of personal and political life, with arguments about human nature and language rather than about substance. [Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) treats both on their own terms. Be wary of the old claim that "real" philosophy only happened in Greece: Indian logicians had a detailed theory of inference and fallacy, and the Mohists of China debated the semantics of names, both well before most of Europe could read Aristotle.

## Three pictures of what philosophy is for

If you ask what philosophy is *for*, the tradition offers three main answers. They are not exclusive, and most great philosophers combine them.

### 1. Philosophy as examined life

The first picture comes from Socrates (469–399 BCE), who wrote nothing and was executed by his city for asking questions. At his trial, as Plato reconstructs it, he explains why he will not stop even to save his life:

> For if I tell you that to do as you say would be a disobedience to the God, and therefore that I cannot hold my tongue, you will not believe that I am serious; and if I say again that daily to discourse about virtue, and of those other things about which you hear me examining myself and others, is the greatest good of man, and that the unexamined life is not worth living, you are still less likely to believe me.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 37e–38a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Read the sentence carefully, because it is usually misquoted. Socrates does not say the unexamined life is not worth living *for anyone, in any respect*. He says that daily discussion of virtue is the greatest good for a human being, and the "unexamined life" (*anexetastos bios*) is "not livable for a human" (*ou biōtos anthrōpōi*), that is, not a life fit for the kind of being we are. The claim is about what makes human life *human*. It is still a strong claim, and one of the dialogues below asks whether you believe it.

On this first picture, philosophy is less a body of doctrine than an activity, the activity of testing beliefs that you live by. Socrates' image for himself is an insect:

> I am that gadfly which God has attached to the state, and all day long and in all places am always fastening upon you, arousing and persuading and reproaching you.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 30e (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

The French historian Pierre Hadot (1922–2010) generalized this into a thesis about all ancient philosophy: the Stoics, Epicureans, Skeptics and Platonists did not mainly offer theories; they offered *ways of life*, supported by "spiritual exercises" (*exercices spirituels*) such as meditating on death, examining one's conscience each evening, and taking the "view from above." Theories served the life. Plato's *Phaedo* has Socrates, on his last day, put the idea in its most startling form:

> For I deem that the true votary of philosophy is likely to be misunderstood by other men; they do not perceive that he is always pursuing death and dying.
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 64a (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Philosophy as "practice for dying" (*meletē thanatou*) sounds morbid. In context it means the training of attention away from bodily appetite and fear toward what reason can grasp, so that death, when it comes, takes away nothing you most care about. [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md) and the [Stoicism course, Chapter 12](../learn-stoicism/12-stoic-practices.md) show what such practices looked like in daily life. Hadot's thesis has critics: John Cooper, in *Pursuits of Wisdom* (2012), accepts that ancient philosophy was a way of life but insists that for the ancients it was *reason*, argument itself, that was supposed to do the transforming, not meditative technique.

### 2. Philosophy as foundational knowledge

The second picture sees philosophy as the discipline that secures the foundations of all knowledge, or builds the most general account of reality. Aristotle's "first philosophy," the medieval "queen of the sciences," Descartes' tree of knowledge (metaphysics the roots, physics the trunk, the other sciences the branches), and Hegel's system all belong here. Descartes begins his *Discourse on Method* (1637) with a mischievous remark about the raw material:

> Good sense is, of all things among men, the most equally distributed; for every one thinks himself so abundantly provided with it, that those even who are the most difficult to satisfy in everything else, do not usually desire a larger measure of this quality than they already possess.
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part I (tr. John Veitch)

His point is serious beneath the joke: we all have reason, but we use it badly, and philosophy is the discipline of using it well. His first rule of method is the foundational picture in a sentence:

> The first was never to accept anything for true which I did not clearly know to be such; that is to say, carefully to avoid precipitancy and prejudice, and to comprise nothing more in my judgement than what was presented to my mind so clearly and distinctly as to exclude all ground of doubt.
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part II (tr. John Veitch)

[Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) shows how far that rule took him, and what went wrong.

### 3. Philosophy as clarification

The third picture is more modest. John Locke, introducing his *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1690), says that in an age of great scientists the philosopher's job is janitorial:

> …it is ambition enough to be employed as an under-labourer in clearing the ground a little, and removing some of the rubbish that lies in the way to knowledge…
>
> — John Locke, *An Essay Concerning Humane Understanding*, "Epistle to the Reader" (1690)

The twentieth century took this "under-labourer" conception much further. Early analytic philosophers such as Russell and the young Wittgenstein held that many philosophical problems arise from misunderstanding the logic of our language, and that clearing up the confusion makes the problem disappear rather than solving it. [Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) tells that story.

### Hume's balance

David Hume, at the start of his *Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* (1748), compares "easy" philosophy, which moves the heart and improves conduct, with "abstruse" philosophy, which reasons carefully about the foundations of thought. He defends the abstruse kind, but he imagines Nature herself giving the philosopher a warning:

> Abstruse thought and profound researches I prohibit, and will severely punish, by the pensive melancholy which they introduce, by the endless uncertainty in which they involve you, and by the cold reception which your pretended discoveries shall meet with, when communicated. Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man.
>
> — David Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding* I.4

That last sentence is good advice for this whole course. You will meet arguments that seem to prove that you cannot know anything, that nothing has value, that you have no self. Take them seriously, follow them carefully, and keep living in the meantime. The [nihilism course](../learn-nihilism/01-what-is-nihilism.md) is in large part about what happens when people cannot do that.

## Why it matters

Why study something that rarely delivers definite answers? Russell's answer is the classic one, and worth reading slowly:

> The value of philosophy is, in fact, to be sought largely in its very uncertainty. The man who has no tincture of philosophy goes through life imprisoned in the prejudices derived from common sense, from the habitual beliefs of his age or his nation, and from convictions which have grown up in his mind without the co-operation or consent of his deliberate reason. To such a man the world tends to become definite, finite, obvious; common objects rouse no questions, and unfamiliar possibilities are contemptuously rejected.
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. XV

He goes on to say that philosophy "removes the somewhat arrogant dogmatism of those who have never travelled into the region of liberating doubt, and it keeps alive our sense of wonder by showing familiar things in an unfamiliar aspect." And he ends the book with a sentence that answers the progress objection directly:

> Philosophy is to be studied, not for the sake of any definite answers to its questions, since no definite answers can, as a rule, be known to be true, but rather for the sake of the questions themselves; because these questions enlarge our conception of what is possible, enrich our intellectual imagination and diminish the dogmatic assurance which closes the mind against speculation…
>
> — Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*, Ch. XV

You can sort the reasons for studying philosophy into four kinds, from most to least obvious.

1. **Skill.** Philosophy trains you to find the structure of an argument, spot an ambiguity, construct a counterexample, and write precisely. These skills transfer to law, medicine, software, policy and any job that requires judgment under uncertainty.
2. **Freedom.** The beliefs you were raised with, the slogans of your era, and the assumptions of your profession are all candidates for examination. You cannot choose your values freely if you have never seen an alternative stated well. This is Russell's point about the "tyranny of custom."
3. **Self-knowledge and integrity.** When you examine your beliefs together, you find they conflict: you believe people are responsible for their choices and that their choices are caused by things they did not choose; you believe all persons have equal worth and you treat strangers differently from friends. Philosophy will not always resolve the conflicts, but it lets you hold your views with knowledge of their costs. That is what Socrates meant by examining "myself and others."
4. **Meaning.** Every human life rests on some implicit answer to the question of what matters. Philosophy makes the answer explicit and lets you test it. The final chapter of this course, [Chapter 24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md), and the sibling courses on [Stoicism](../learn-stoicism/16-living-a-stoic-life.md), [ethics](../learn-ethics/22-building-your-ethics.md) and [nihilism](../learn-nihilism/15-beyond-nihilism.md) all return to this.

The strongest objection to the value of philosophy was voiced in antiquity by Callicles in Plato's *Gorgias* (484c–486d): philosophy is charming for a boy, but a grown man who keeps doing it becomes unmanly, useless in court and politics, and unable to defend himself if someone drags him off to prison. Callicles' warning was prophetic, since Socrates was dragged off and executed. Plato's reply occupies the rest of the *Gorgias* and much of the *Republic*: if you do not know what is good, all your power is only power to get what you *think* you want, and a tyrant who does whatever he pleases is the most unhappy of men. Whether that reply works is a question for [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) and [Chapter 5](05-plato.md).

## How to read a philosophy text

Philosophical texts are unlike novels or textbooks. They are compressed, they argue with people you have never heard of, and they often say the opposite of what a hurried reader thinks. Here is a method in six steps. Practice it on the *Apology*; [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) returns to the trial itself.

### Step 1: Find the question

Every philosophical text is an answer to a question, often one it does not state. Ask: what problem is this author trying to solve, and why did it seem urgent? The *Apology* answers a legal question (is Socrates guilty of impiety and of corrupting the young?) by answering a philosophical one (what kind of life is worth living, and what is wisdom?).

### Step 2: Find the thesis

State the main claim in one sentence of your own. If you cannot, you have not understood the text yet. Thesis of the *Apology*, roughly: *Socrates is wiser than others only in knowing that he knows nothing of the greatest matters, and his questioning of others is a service to the city and to the god, not a crime.* The famous line is:

> Well, although I do not suppose that either of us knows anything really beautiful and good, I am better off than he is,—for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows; I neither know nor think that I know.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 21d (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

### Step 3: Reconstruct the argument

Write the reasoning in numbered premises and a conclusion, adding unstated premises that the author needs. Take Socrates' argument near the end of the *Apology* that death is nothing to fear:

> Let us reflect in another way, and we shall see that there is great reason to hope that death is a good; for one of two things—either death is a state of nothingness and utter unconsciousness, or, as men say, there is a change and migration of the soul from this world to another.
>
> — Plato, *Apology* 40c (tr. Benjamin Jowett)

Reconstructed:

1. Death is either (a) dreamless nothingness or (b) migration of the soul to another place.
2. If (a), death is a gain, since a dreamless night is better than most days and nights of life.
3. If (b), death is a gain, since there one can converse with the great dead (and, Socrates adds, they do not execute people for asking questions there).
4. So death is a gain. (From 1–3, by the argument form called *constructive dilemma*, which [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md) names.)
5. (Unstated) One should not fear what is a gain.
6. So one should not fear death.

### Step 4: Evaluate

Now ask two separate questions (the distinction is the heart of [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md)). *Does the conclusion follow?* Here, yes: if 1–3 and 5 are true, 6 must be. *Are the premises true?* Premise 1 is a false dichotomy unless you can rule out a third option (a bad afterlife, the one most Athenians feared). Premise 2 assumes that unconsciousness can be *good for* someone who no longer exists to enjoy it, which Epicurus will deny in a different way ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)). So the argument is valid but contestable. Notice too that Socrates hedges: he says there is "great reason to hope," not that he has a proof. A careful reader catches the hedge.

### Step 5: Be charitable, then critical

The **principle of charity** says: before criticizing, interpret the author so that the argument is as strong as the text allows. Students often "refute" a great philosopher with an objection that the philosopher answered two pages later. Read the whole passage, look for the strongest version, and only then attack. The ancient rule of the Indian debating tradition is similar: before you may refute an opponent's view (the *pūrvapakṣa*, "prior position"), you must state it so well that its holder would accept your statement.

### Step 6: Place it in context

Ask what genre you are reading, because genre changes how you should read.

- **Dialogue** (Plato, Berkeley, Hume's *Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion*): the author's view may not be any one speaker's, and the drama matters. Who walks off in a huff, who is flattered, what is left unanswered?
- **Treatise** (Aristotle, Spinoza, Kant): technical vocabulary is defined and used precisely; keep a glossary.
- **Aphorism and fragment** (Heraclitus, Pascal, Nietzsche, the *Analects*): each line compresses an argument; context often comes from elsewhere in the text or from commentary.
- **Sutra and commentary** (Indian and Chinese traditions): a terse root text is read *through* a tradition of commentary, which is where much of the philosophy happens.
- **Letter, essay, meditation** (Seneca, Montaigne, Descartes, Marcus Aurelius): personal form, often a written spiritual exercise.

Translation matters too. In this course you will read older public-domain translations (Jowett's Plato, Legge's Confucius) because they are free and quotable, and the readings lists recommend modern ones because they are often more accurate. When a key term is translated differently in two versions, look up the original word; the course gives it in italics on first use.

### Citation systems

Philosophers cite classic texts by standard numbers that are the same across editions and translations. Learn them now; they let you find a passage in any edition.

| Author | System | Example | Meaning |
|--------|--------|---------|---------|
| Plato | Stephanus pages (1578 edition) | *Apology* 38a | page 38, section a |
| Aristotle | Bekker pages (1831 edition) | *Nicomachean Ethics* 1094b25 | page 1094, column b, line 25 |
| Presocratics | Diels–Kranz numbers | DK 22B30 | chapter 22 (Heraclitus), B = fragment, no. 30 |
| Diogenes Laertius | book.section | *Lives* 1.12 | book 1, section 12 |
| Kant | Akademie edition, or A/B for the first *Critique* | A51/B75 | page in the 1781 (A) and 1787 (B) editions |
| *Analects* | book.chapter | 2.17 | book 2, chapter 17 |

One more piece of reading advice comes from Aristotle, about matching your expectations to your subject:

> …for the man of education will seek exactness so far in each subject as the nature of the thing admits, it being plainly much the same absurdity to put up with a mathematician who tries to persuade instead of proving, and to demand strict demonstrative reasoning of a Rhetorician.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1.3, 1094b23–27 (tr. D. P. Chase)

Metaphysics and logic can sometimes reach proof. Ethics and politics usually cannot, and a reader who demands geometrical proof in ethics will find nothing but disappointment, while a reader who accepts mere rhetoric in logic will be fooled. Calibrate.

Finally, a warning against reading too much. Schopenhauer, a voracious reader, thought that a person who only reads becomes a warehouse of other people's opinions:

> Reading is thinking with some one else's head instead of one's own.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, "On Thinking for Oneself," in *The Art of Literature* (tr. T. Bailey Saunders)

The cure is not to stop reading but to write. After each reading in this course, write a paragraph stating the thesis, a numbered argument, and your strongest objection. The dialogues at the end of each chapter are designed to force exactly that.

## A map of this course and its siblings

This course is a survey, from the Presocratics to present debates, organized historically because philosophy is a conversation in which each speaker answers earlier ones. Three sibling courses go deeper where the survey can only point:

- **[Stoicism](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md)**: one ancient school, its physics, logic and ethics, and its influence up to modern cognitive therapy. This course gives Stoicism one section in [Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md).
- **[Ethics](../learn-ethics/01-what-is-ethics.md)**: the full field of moral philosophy, from Socrates to AI ethics. This course treats ethics where it shapes the history (Aristotle, Kant, Mill) and sends you there for depth.
- **[Nihilism](../learn-nihilism/01-what-is-nihilism.md)**: the challenge that nothing has meaning or value, and the responses to it. This course meets it in Nietzsche ([Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md)) and existentialism ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)).

The progression ramps up. Chapters 1–8 assume no background. By the last chapters you will be reading about current debates in the professional literature and should be able to follow a graduate seminar.

## Living it

The ancient schools treated philosophy as a practice, and so should you. For the next week, try the following, which combines the Socratic and the Russellian pictures above.

1. **Keep a question notebook.** Each day, write down one thing that struck you as strange: a moment of *thaumazein*. It might be a news story ("Can a company be blamed for anything?"), a feeling ("Why did I feel guilty for something I could not control?"), or an object, like Russell's table. Do not answer it yet. Just state it as precisely as you can.
2. **Run one Socratic audit.** Pick a belief you act on daily, ideally one you have never argued for: "My job is worth doing," "I should be loyal to my family whatever they do," "Success means earning more than my parents." Write it as a thesis. Then write the best argument for it in numbered premises. Then write the best objection you can find, ideally one that a person you respect would make. Note where you are unsure. This is the "examining myself" Socrates speaks of in the *Apology*, and it is uncomfortable for exactly the reason he gives: it reveals that you "think you know" things you do not.
3. **Practice Hume's balance.** When the audit unsettles you, notice whether you are tempted either to abandon the belief at once or to stop thinking about it. Do neither. Write down what you would need to learn to decide. Then go on with your day. "Be a philosopher; but, amidst all your philosophy, be still a man."

At the end of the week, reread your notebook. You will probably find that several of your questions are classic problems with names, and later chapters of this course will give you the best things ever said about them.

## Summary

- *Philosophia* means "love of wisdom": the philosopher is defined by a striving, not by possession. The story that Pythagoras coined the word is probably a Platonic back-projection.
- Philosophy begins in wonder (*thaumazein*): the moment something ordinary becomes puzzling. The Thracian servant's joke reminds us it must not lose sight of the ground.
- Philosophical questions are conceptual, normative or foundational; they cannot be settled by data alone, and everyone has some implicit answer to them.
- The main branches are logic, metaphysics, epistemology, ethics (normative, meta-, applied), political philosophy, aesthetics, and philosophy of mind, religion, science and so on. Other traditions organize the field differently (Indian *darśanas*, the Chinese *dao*).
- Three pictures of philosophy's purpose: an examined way of life (Socrates, Hadot), foundational knowledge (Aristotle, Descartes), and clarification (Locke's "under-labourer," analytic philosophy).
- The "no progress" objection can be answered by negative progress, the residue view (Russell), and the way-of-life view, though not decisively.
- To read philosophy: find the question, state the thesis, reconstruct the argument, evaluate validity and premises separately, be charitable first, and respect genre, translation and standard citation systems.

## Dialogues

These dialogues are imagined, in the tradition of Plato's. Answer before you open each continuation.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether philosophy is useless

**Socrates:** You are studying to become an engineer, I hear. And now you are reading philosophy as well. Why waste the time?

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** Honestly, I am not sure it is not a waste. Engineering builds bridges. Philosophy has argued about the same questions for 2,500 years.

**Socrates:** Then let us see. When you build a bridge, do you build it for no reason, or for some good?

**You:** For a good, obviously. So people can cross the river.

**Socrates:** And is it good for people to cross the river?

**You:** Usually. Unless an army is crossing it to attack a city.

**Socrates:** So engineering can tell you how to build the bridge, but can it tell you whether to build it, or for whom?

**You:** No. That is a question for politics, or ethics.

**Socrates:** And do the engineers and the politicians agree about what is good?

**You:** Not at all. But they settle it by voting, or by whoever pays.

**Socrates:** Callicles would like that answer: let the strongest decide. But tell me, when you are outvoted, do you think the vote has made the wrong thing right?

**You:** No. I would still think they were wrong.

**Socrates:** Then you have some opinion about what is good that a vote does not settle. Where did you get it?

**You:** From my parents, I suppose. My teachers. Things I have read.

**Socrates:** And have you examined it, to see whether it is true or only familiar?

**You:** Not really. That is what you will say philosophy is for.

**Russell:** Allow me to interrupt. I would add that the opinion was formed "without the co-operation or consent of" your "deliberate reason," as I put it. Philosophy does not promise you the right answer. It promises that whatever answer you hold, you will hold it with your eyes open.

**You:** But if philosophy never reaches a definite answer, how is holding a view "with my eyes open" better than holding it blindly?

**Socrates:** A fair question. Is a ship's pilot who knows where the rocks are, though he does not know the whole sea, better or worse than one who does not know there are rocks?

**You:** Better. He at least avoids the obvious wrecks.

**Socrates:** So perhaps philosophy is a chart of rocks: of answers that fail, contradictions you hold without noticing, and questions you thought were simple. Would an engineer despise such a chart?

**You:** No. Engineers study failures more than successes. I will grant this much: philosophy is not useless, but its use is not the same as a bridge's. It improves the person who decides what bridges are for.

**Socrates:** That is a better answer than most Athenians gave me. Whether it is *true* we can examine tomorrow.

*What we learned:* the "uselessness" objection assumes that only instrumental goods count, but every instrumental activity presupposes judgments about ends, and philosophy is the examination of those judgments. Compare Russell, *Problems*, Ch. XV, and Callicles' challenge in the *Gorgias*, discussed in [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md).

### Dialogue 2 — On Russell's table

**Russell:** You are sitting at a table. Tell me what color it is.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Russell in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** It is brown. Dark brown, with a lighter grain.

**Russell:** From where you sit. Now stand up and look at the end near the window, where the light falls.

**You:** That part looks nearly white, because of the glare.

**Russell:** So which is its *real* color, brown or white?

**You:** Brown. The white is just reflected light.

**Russell:** But you see the brown by reflected light too. Why privilege one angle? And in the dark, what color is it?

**You:** In the dark it has no visible color. But it still *is* brown. If I turned on a normal light, it would look brown.

**Russell:** Good: you are now defining its color by how it would look to a normal observer under normal light. That is a sensible move. But notice what you have done. The "real" color is no longer something you see; it is a disposition to cause certain appearances under certain conditions.

**You:** I suppose so. Is that a problem?

**Russell:** Not yet a problem, a discovery. Now touch it. It feels solid. Physics says it is mostly empty space with charged particles in motion. Which is the real table?

**You:** The physical one, presumably. My senses are just a rough guide.

**Russell:** But you know about the particles only through observations, which are also sensations. If the senses are only a rough guide, what makes the physicist's senses better than yours?

**You:** They use instruments and theory, and the theory predicts things. That gives them reason to trust it.

**Russell:** Excellent. So you distinguish *appearance* (what is given in sensation) from *reality* (what best explains the appearances). Philosophers have argued for centuries about whether we can know the reality at all, or only the appearances. Berkeley said there is nothing *but* appearances and minds. Kant said there is a reality we cannot know.

**You:** That sounds ridiculous. Of course the table exists when nobody looks at it.

**Russell:** I agree that it is the simplest hypothesis. But "it is the simplest hypothesis" is a different sort of reason from "I see it." You came in certain; you now hold the same belief, with a reason, and with a sense of what it would take to doubt it.

**You:** Then my certainty was really a habit, and now it is a hypothesis. I am not sure I like that.

**Russell:** You need not like it. That is "liberating doubt." It enlarges what you think possible, and it will make the chapters on Descartes, Locke, Berkeley and Kant much more interesting.

*What we learned:* even simple perceptual beliefs depend on assumptions about "normal conditions" and about what explains appearances; the distinction between appearance and reality is the entrance to epistemology and metaphysics. See Russell, *Problems*, Ch. I, and [Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md).

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether the unexamined life is worth living

**Socrates:** I told the jury that the unexamined life is not worth living for a human being. You look doubtful.

**You:** …

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I am. My grandmother never read a word of philosophy. She worked hard, raised five children and was kind to everyone. Are you saying her life was not worth living?

**Socrates:** Did she never ask herself whether she was doing right?

**You:** Of course she did. She worried about whether she was too strict with my uncle, and whether she should have left her village.

**Socrates:** Then she examined her life. Did I say that only those who argue in the marketplace examine their lives?

**You:** You made it sound that way. "Daily to discourse about virtue."

**Socrates:** I said that was the greatest good for a human being. A life can be good without having the greatest good in it. But let me ask: suppose a man never once asked whether what he did was right, never once wondered whether his opinions were true. Would his life be lived by him, or by his habits?

**You:** By his habits, I suppose. But he might still be happy, and harmless.

**Hume:** If I may: I fear Socrates sets the bar too high. Most men are governed by custom, and custom is "the great guide of human life." A man may be decent and content without examining a thing. Nature has not left our conduct to depend on our reasoning.

**Socrates:** Then you think examination adds nothing to such a life?

**Hume:** It adds something, a refinement, a protection against superstition and fanaticism. But I would not call the unreflective life "not worth living." That is the zealot's language.

**You:** That is closer to what I think. Examination is valuable, but it is not the condition of a life having value.

**Socrates:** Consider one case. A man is decent because his community is decent. Then the tyrants come, and his neighbors begin informing on each other. What will his habits tell him to do?

**You:** To go along with his neighbors. That is what habit is.

**Socrates:** And will he notice that he has become wicked?

**You:** Perhaps not. That is the danger. So the unexamined life is fragile: it is only as good as its surroundings.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps my claim was about what makes a life *one's own* and safe from corruption, not about what makes it pleasant.

**You:** Then I would put it this way: an unexamined life can be good, even admirable, but its goodness is borrowed and unprotected. Examination makes it yours. I still would not say such a life is "not worth living."

**Socrates:** You have improved on my words with a distinction I did not draw before the jury. I had only an hour, and they were in a hurry to kill me.

*What we learned:* "the unexamined life is not worth living" (*Apology* 38a) is best read as a claim about the specifically human good and about moral reliability, not as contempt for ordinary people. Hume's alternative, custom as a guide, appears in [Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md); the worry about decent people under tyranny returns in [Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) and in the [Ethics course, Chapter 18](../learn-ethics/18-moral-psychology-and-responsibility.md).
