# Chapter 7 — Hellenistic Philosophy

Alexander the Great died in Babylon in June 323 BCE, aged thirty-two, and his empire broke apart among his generals. Aristotle, suspected of Macedonian sympathies, left Athens the next year and died soon after. The world he left behind was larger, richer and less safe than the one Plato and Aristotle had theorized. The independent city-state, the *polis* that Aristotle called the natural home of a political animal, now lived under the shadow of kings. Greek was spoken from Sicily to Afghanistan. A citizen could no longer expect to shape his city's fate in the assembly, and many found themselves far from home, under rulers they had not chosen, in a world run by fortune.

Philosophy changed its center of gravity. Questions of being and knowledge were now pursued for the sake of one overriding question: how can a human being live well, and without fear, in a world like this? Four movements gave four answers. The **Cynics** said: strip away convention and live like a dog, by nature. The **Epicureans** said: understand that the world is atoms and void, that the gods do not care and that death is nothing, and you will be free to enjoy simple pleasures among friends. The **Stoics** said: the world is governed by divine reason, virtue is the only good, and everything else is indifferent. The **Skeptics** said: suspend judgment on everything, and peace of mind will follow like a shadow. The Stoics are kept short here because they have a course of their own ([learn-stoicism](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md)); the ethics of all four is examined in [the Ethics course, Chapter 6](../learn-ethics/06-hellenistic-ethics.md).

## A smaller polis, a larger world

"Hellenistic" is a modern label for the three centuries between Alexander's death (323 BCE) and the battle of Actium (31 BCE), when Rome absorbed the last of the Greek kingdoms. It was long dismissed as an age of epigones and librarians. In philosophy that reputation is undeserved. The Hellenistic schools produced the first full theory of propositional logic (the Stoics), the most sophisticated ancient theory of knowledge and its limits (the Skeptics), the most thoroughgoing materialism before the seventeenth century (the Epicureans), and the idea that every human being is a citizen of a single world (the Cynics and Stoics). Much of it survives only in fragments and hostile reports, which is why it was undervalued.

Three features of the age shaped its philosophy.

First, **Athens remained the university town**. Plato's Academy and Aristotle's Lyceum carried on, and two new schools opened around 306–300 BCE: Epicurus' Garden, a private property outside the walls, and Zeno's lectures in the Painted Stoa (*stoa poikilē*), a public colonnade on the Agora that gave the Stoics their name. The schools competed fiercely for students.

Second, **the schools were communities, not just doctrines**. Each had a founder venerated almost as a saint, a body of teachings that members memorized, and a style of life. You did not merely hold Epicurean views; you lived as an Epicurean, with Epicurean friends, eating Epicurean meals.

Third, **philosophy was explicitly therapeutic**. Its aim was to cure the soul of the false beliefs that cause anxiety, anger, grief and fear. Cicero, a Roman who studied in Athens and later translated all four schools into Latin, put it bluntly:

> Philosophy is certainly the medicine of the soul, whose assistance we do not seek from abroad, as in bodily disorders, but we ourselves are bound to exert our utmost energy and power in order to effect our cure.
>
> — Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 3.3 (tr. Yonge)

The French historian Pierre Hadot made this the key to all ancient philosophy: its texts were written less to inform than to *form*, to train the reader through "spiritual exercises" into a different way of seeing. [Chapter 1](01-what-is-philosophy.md) discussed that thesis and its critics. Whatever its merits for Plato and Aristotle, for the Hellenistic schools it is close to self-description. Each offers a diagnosis, a cure and an ideal of health. For Epicureans and Skeptics the health is called *ataraxia*, "untroubledness," freedom from disturbance. For the Stoics it is *apatheia*, freedom from the passions, and a smooth flow of life. For the Cynics it is *autarkeia*, self-sufficiency.

The medical model raises a worry to hold onto through the chapter. A doctor aims at health, not truth. If a belief calms you, should a therapeutic philosopher teach it whether or not it is true? Epicureans and Stoics answer that only true beliefs calm reliably. The Skeptics give a stranger answer.

## The Cynics: philosophy as street theater

The Cynics were less a school than a provocation. Their founder, by tradition, was Antisthenes (c. 445–365 BCE), a companion of Socrates who admired his master's indifference to comfort and opinion. Their great performer was Diogenes of Sinope (c. 412–323 BCE), exiled from his Black Sea city after a scandal involving the currency. Their name comes from *kyōn*, "dog," an insult the Cynics adopted as a badge: dogs eat, sleep and copulate in public, without shame, and are loyal to their friends. The [Stoicism course, Chapter 2](../learn-stoicism/02-before-the-stoa.md) treats the Cynics as Stoicism's ancestors; here the focus is on what they were arguing.

Diogenes left no surviving books. What we have is a cloud of anecdotes in Diogenes Laertius, many of them doubtless invented, which together form a kind of philosophical cartoon. His conversion story is about a mouse:

> And when, as Theophrastus tells us, in his Megaric Philosopher, he saw a mouse running about and not seeking for a bed, nor taking care to keep in the dark, nor looking for any of those things which appear enjoyable to such an animal, he found a remedy for his own poverty.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 6.22 (tr. Yonge)

The argument behind the mouse is serious. Most of what we think we need is not needed by nature; it is needed only by convention, by the opinions of others. Each unnecessary need is a chain, because whatever you need can be taken from you. So reduce your needs to what nature requires and you become invulnerable. Diogenes slept in a large storage jar, carried a cloak, a wallet and a cup, and kept simplifying:

> On one occasion he saw a child drinking out of its hands, and so he threw away the cup which belonged to his wallet, saying, "That child has beaten me in simplicity."
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 6.37 (tr. Yonge)

Laertius sums up his program in a triple antithesis: "he opposed confidence to fortune, nature to law, and reason to suffering." Each pair matters. Against fortune, the Cynic sets a self that has nothing to lose. Against *nomos*, law and custom, he sets *physis*, nature, picking up the old Sophistic contrast from [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) and turning it from a debate topic into a way of life. Against suffering he sets the trained endurance of a man who has practiced hunger, cold and contempt.

The most famous scene shows what invulnerability looks like:

> Once, while he was sitting in the sun in the Craneum, Alexander was standing by, and said to him, "Ask any favour you choose of me." And he replied, "Cease to shade me from the sun."
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 6.38 (tr. Yonge)

The ruler of the known world has nothing the beggar wants. Elsewhere Laertius reports that Alexander said "that if he had not been Alexander, he should have liked to be Diogenes." Whether or not the meeting happened, the story became the model of the philosopher's independence from power.

Diogenes' method was **public shamelessness** (*anaideia*) used as argument. He ate in the marketplace, which Athenian manners forbade, and did worse things there too. The point was to expose conventions as conventions: if an act is harmless in private, what exactly is shameful about it in public? Asked what he gained from philosophy, he said, "If no other, at least this, that I am prepared for every kind of fortune." Reproached for going into dirty places, he replied, "The sun too penetrates into privies, but is not polluted by them." In daylight he walked about with a lit lamp: "I am looking for a man." Real human beings, living by reason and nature, were hard to find among the citizens.

He had little patience for metaphysics. When Plato was lecturing on his Forms, using the nouns "tableness" and "cupness," Diogenes cut in with "I, O Plato!" and went on: "see a table and a cup, but I see no tableness or cupness." Plato's reply, as Laertius reports it, was that Diogenes had eyes but not intellect. It is the debate about universals from [Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md), played as slapstick.

Two of Diogenes' slogans had a long future. The oracle had told him, the story goes, to *deface the currency* (*paracharattein to nomisma*). The word *nomisma*, coin, is cognate with *nomos*, custom, and Diogenes took the pun as his mission: to restamp the counterfeit values of society. And when someone asked where he came from, "he replied, 'A Citizen of the world.'" The Greek is *kosmopolitēs*, the word's first recorded use. For Diogenes it was mainly negative: he owed no city anything. The Stoics turned it into the positive ideal of a community of all rational beings, and through them it reached Kant and modern human rights.

Crates of Thebes gave away a fortune to become a Cynic, and Hipparchia, from a wealthy family, married him against her parents' wishes and shared his life in the streets; she is one of the few ancient women philosophers Laertius records. Crates in turn taught a young Phoenician merchant named Zeno.

Was Cynicism philosophy or performance? Both. Its arguments are simple, but they are arguments: if a good can be taken from you, it is not truly yours; what nature requires is little and easily had; convention is not nature. The questions remain live. Every minimalist and every satirist who mocks the powerful to their face is in the Cynic line. The obvious objection is that Diogenes depended on the society he mocked: he begged from the citizens whose conventions he flouted, and his performance needed an audience that still felt shame. Total Cynicism is parasitic. Partial Cynicism, a training in needing less, survives in every school that followed.

## Epicurus and the Garden

Epicurus (341–270 BCE) was born on Samos to an Athenian family, studied with followers of Plato and Democritus, and opened schools in Mytilene and Lampsacus before buying a house and garden in Athens around 306 BCE. The Garden admitted women and slaves, which scandalized critics who assumed it must therefore be an orgy. It was nearer to a commune. Members lived simply, ate bread and water with a little cheese on feast days, studied the master's writings, and celebrated his birthday and a monthly memorial feast. Epicurus wrote some three hundred books. Almost all are lost, but Diogenes Laertius copied three letters and a list of forty *Principal Doctrines* into his tenth book, which is why it is the best surviving source.

The doctrine was meant to be portable and memorizable. A friend in a distant city could carry it in summary and repeat it in a crisis. Its spirit appears in the letter Epicurus wrote as he was dying of a kidney stone:

> We have written this letter to you on a happy day to us, which is also the last day of our life. For strangury has attacked me, and also a dysentery, so violent that nothing can be added to the violence of my sufferings. But the cheerfulness of my mind, which arises from the recollection of all my philosophical contemplations, counterbalances all these afflictions.
>
> — Epicurus, *Letter to Idomeneus*, in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 10.22 (tr. Yonge)

The structure of Epicurean thought is simple. Human beings are made miserable chiefly by two fears, fear of the gods and fear of death, and by desires that cannot be satisfied. Physics removes the fears. Ethics, a correct theory of pleasure and desire, removes the rest.

### Atoms, the swerve and the gods

Epicurus took over Democritus' atomism ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)) and made it the foundation of a cure. Everything that exists is atoms moving in an infinite void. The atoms are indivisible, unchanging and infinite in number; worlds form and dissolve by their chance collisions; the soul is a cluster of fine atoms spread through the body, and it disperses at death.

The best exposition is not by Epicurus at all but by a Roman poet, Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 99–55 BCE), whose *On the Nature of Things* sets the system out in six books of Latin verse. Lucretius presents Epicurus as a hero who defeated a monster:

> Whilst human kind
> Throughout the lands lay miserably crushed
> Before all eyes beneath Religion--who
> Would show her head along the region skies,
> Glowering on mortals with her hideous face--
> A Greek it was who first opposing dared
> Raise mortal eyes that terror to withstand
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* 1.62–67 (tr. Leonard)

The monster is *religio*, superstitious fear of the gods. Lucretius then tells the story of Agamemnon sacrificing his daughter Iphigenia at Aulis to get a fair wind for Troy, and ends with one of the most quoted lines in Latin: *tantum religio potuit suadere malorum*. Leonard renders it: "Such are the crimes to which Religion leads."

The first principle of the cure is the old Eleatic axiom turned against the gods: "Nothing from nothing ever yet was born." If nothing comes from nothing, then nothing comes from divine whim either. Every event has natural causes, even when we cannot see them, and the fear of gods comes from ignorance of those causes.

There is a problem. Democritus' atoms fall through the void along fixed paths; if they never deviated, Epicurus saw, two things would follow. They would never collide, so no worlds would form, and everything would be fated, so there would be no free will. His solution was the notorious **swerve** (*clinamen*):

> The atoms, as their own weight bears them down
> Plumb through the void, at scarce determined times,
> In scarce determined places, from their course
> Decline a little--call it, so to speak,
> Mere changed trend.
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* 2.217–220 (tr. Leonard)

Lucretius uses the swerve to break "the covenants of fate" and make room for "this free will for creatures o'er the lands." Critics from Cicero onward have mocked it as an uncaused event invented to save a conclusion. In its defense, it is a remarkably early recognition that strict mechanism threatens freedom, a problem still with us ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)). But randomness does not obviously help: a choice caused by a random swerve seems no more *mine* than one caused by a fixed fall.

Epicurus did not deny that gods exist. He held that they are blessed, immortal beings living in the spaces between worlds, and that precisely because they are perfectly happy, they do not meddle with us:

> That which is happy and imperishable, neither has trouble itself, nor does it cause it to anything; so that it is not subject to the feelings of either anger or gratitude; for these feelings only exist in what is weak.
>
> — Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 1, in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 10.139 (tr. Yonge)

The *Letter to Menoeceus* adds a twist that infuriated his opponents: "that man is not impious who discards the Gods believed in by the many, but he who applies to the Gods the opinions entertained of them by the many." The truly impious, on this view, are those who imagine the gods as jealous, angry and bribable. Many ancient readers concluded that Epicurus was an atheist in all but name, and in later Jewish usage *apikoros*, "Epicurean," came to mean a heretic.

### Death is nothing to us

The second fear is death, and here Epicurus produced the most famous argument in Hellenistic philosophy:

> Accustom yourself also to think death a matter with which we are not at all concerned, since all good and all evil is in sensation, and since death is only the privation of sensation. … Therefore, the most formidable of all evils, death, is nothing to us, since, when we exist, death is not present to us; and when death is present, then we have no existence.
>
> — Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus*, in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 10.124–125 (tr. Yonge)

Set out as a syllogism, in the manner of [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md):

1. Something is bad for a person only if that person can experience it (all good and evil lie in sensation).
2. When death is present, the person no longer exists, so cannot experience anything.
3. Therefore death is not bad for the person who dies.
4. It is irrational to fear what is not bad for you.
5. Therefore it is irrational to fear death.

The second *Principal Doctrine* compresses it further: "Death is nothing to us; for that which is dissolved is devoid of sensation, and that which is devoid of sensation is nothing to us." Lucretius added a second argument, the **symmetry argument**. Before we were born, an infinite time passed of which we felt nothing, and nobody is troubled by it:

> Nothing to us was all fore-passed eld
> Of time the eternal, ere we had a birth.
> And Nature holds this like a mirror up
> Of time-to-be when we are dead and gone.
> And what is there so horrible appears?
>
> — Lucretius, *On the Nature of Things* 3.972–976 (tr. Leonard)

The arguments do not deny that dying can be painful or that others' deaths grieve us. Their target is the terror of being dead, of the nothingness itself, which Epicurus thought poisons the whole of life and drives people to superstition, greed and war. Lucretius has a sharp passage arguing that the torments of the mythical underworld are really pictures of this life: Tantalus is the superstitious man, Sisyphus the politician forever seeking office.

Are the arguments sound? The most influential modern reply is the **deprivation account**, defended by Thomas Nagel in his essay "Death" (1970). Premise 1 is false, Nagel argues: some things are bad for us without being experienced. A person betrayed behind his back, whose friends mock him and whose reputation is ruined though he never learns of it, has been harmed. Death is bad because of what it takes away, the goods of the life that would otherwise have continued. On this account you need not be around to suffer the loss for it to be your loss. Against the symmetry argument, Nagel notes that you could have died later but could not have been born much earlier and still been *you*. Epicureans reply that a harm with no subject when it occurs is a puzzling thing. The debate is pursued in [the Ethics course, Chapter 6](../learn-ethics/06-hellenistic-ethics.md) and, from the side of meaninglessness, in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 2](../learn-nihilism/02-ancient-roots.md).

Epicurus thought the argument freed us to *enjoy* life. Knowing that death is nothing "makes the mortality of life pleasant to us, inasmuch as it sets forth no illimitable time, but relieves us for the longing for immortality." A finite life, rightly understood, is complete at every moment.

### Pleasure rightly understood

Epicurus was a hedonist: pleasure is the good. "We affirm, that pleasure is the beginning and end of living happily," says the *Letter to Menoeceus*, because every animal from birth seeks it and flees pain. English still uses "epicure" for a lover of fine food. He saw the misunderstanding coming:

> When, therefore, we say that pleasure is a chief good, we are not speaking of the pleasures of the debauched man, or those which lie in sensual enjoyment, as some think who are ignorant, and who do not entertain our opinions, or else interpret them perversely; but we mean the freedom of the body from pain, and of the soul from confusion.
>
> — Epicurus, *Letter to Menoeceus*, in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 10.131 (tr. Yonge)

The key is a distinction between two kinds of pleasure. **Kinetic** pleasures are the pleasures of a process: eating when hungry, drinking when thirsty, the pleasant motion of satisfying a need. **Katastematic** (static) pleasure is the state of not lacking anything: the condition of a body without pain (*aponia*) and a mind without disturbance (*ataraxia*). Epicurus held that the static state is the highest pleasure, and that once it is reached, pleasure cannot be increased, only varied. "The limit of the greatness of the pleasures is the removal of everything which can give pain." A glass of water to the thirsty is as good as wine, and the man who needs wine will be miserable when it runs out.

This connects to a famous classification of desires:

> Of the desires, some are natural and necessary, some natural, but not necessary, and some are neither natural nor necessary, but owe their existence to vain opinions.
>
> — Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 30 [29 in modern editions], in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 10.149 (tr. Yonge)

Laertius' gloss gives the examples: drink when thirsty is natural and necessary; expensive food is natural but not necessary; and the third kind "are such as crowns, or the erection of statues." Natural and necessary desires are few and easy to satisfy. Vain ones, for fame, power and wealth, are unlimited, because they rest on endless comparison. Satisfy the first, enjoy the second when they come without effort, dissolve the third by argument. Unlike the Cynic, the Epicurean does not *refuse* luxury; he is free of it, and so can enjoy it when it comes. In Epicurus' paradox, "those men enjoy luxury most completely who are the best able to do without it."

The calculation of pleasure is long-term. "We do not choose every pleasure," Epicurus writes, and "we think many pains better than pleasures, when a greater pleasure follows them." So the highest virtue is prudence, which teaches "that it is not possible to live pleasantly unless one also lives prudently, and honourably, and justly." Virtue is a means to pleasure, not an end; the Stoics thought that made it hollow.

Two social doctrines follow. The first concerns **friendship**: "Of all the things which wisdom provides for the happiness of the whole life, by far the most important is the acquisition of friendship" (*Principal Doctrines* 28; 27 in modern editions). The Garden was built around it. Critics asked whether friendship valued for the security it brings is real friendship; some later Epicureans answered that it begins in utility and comes to be loved for its own sake.

The second concerns **justice**, and it is the first social contract theory in the Western tradition:

> Justice has no independent existence; it results from mutual contracts, and establishes itself wherever there is a mutual engagement to guard against doing or sustaining mutual injury.
>
> — Epicurus, *Principal Doctrines* 35 [33], in Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 10.150 (tr. Yonge)

Justice is "a covenant of what is suitable, leading men to avoid injuring one another, and being injured." Animals that cannot make agreements have no justice. And then the hard-edged conclusion: "Injustice is not intrinsically bad; it has this character only because there is joined with it a fear of not escaping those who are appointed to punish." This is Glaucon's challenge from Plato's *Republic* ([Chapter 5](05-plato.md)) accepted rather than refuted, softened only by the observation that the unjust man can never be sure he will escape detection, and so never has *ataraxia*. Hobbes would rebuild the same theory with a sovereign in place of the fear ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)).

The Epicurean ideal was a quiet life: *lathe biōsas*, "live unnoticed." Avoid politics, which breeds anxiety and enemies; cultivate friends and your garden. The obvious criticism is that this is an ethics for the comfortable, which withdraws from injustices it could address. The Epicurean answer is that most political striving is driven by the unlimited desires, and that a world of Gardens would need less politics.

## The Stoics, in brief

Zeno of Citium (c. 334–262 BCE), a Phoenician merchant from Cyprus, arrived in Athens after a shipwreck, read a book about Socrates in a bookshop, and asked where men like that could be found. The bookseller pointed to Crates the Cynic, who happened to be passing. He studied with Crates, then with Platonists and dialecticians, and later joked about his loss: "I now find that I made a prosperous voyage when I was wrecked." He began teaching in the Painted Stoa around 300 BCE. His successors Cleanthes and above all Chrysippus (c. 279–206 BCE) built Stoicism into a complete system, and Roman Stoics, Seneca, Epictetus and the emperor Marcus Aurelius, turned it into the most widely read philosophy of the Roman Empire.

Stoicism is the subject of a whole course. The [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md) covers the early Stoa ([Chapter 3](../learn-stoicism/03-the-early-stoa.md)), physics ([Chapter 4](../learn-stoicism/04-stoic-physics.md)), logic and knowledge ([Chapter 5](../learn-stoicism/05-logic-and-knowledge.md)), virtue ([Chapter 6](../learn-stoicism/06-virtue-and-the-good.md)), the passions ([Chapter 7](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md)), and Epictetus and Marcus ([Chapters 10](../learn-stoicism/10-epictetus.md) and [11](../learn-stoicism/11-marcus-aurelius.md)). What follows is only what a survey needs, set against the Epicureans.

**Physics.** Where Epicurus saw atoms and chance, the Stoics saw a single, continuous, living cosmos pervaded by *logos*, divine reason, which they identified with a fiery breath (*pneuma*) and called God, Nature, Fate and Providence. Everything happens by fate, in an unbroken chain of causes, and everything happens for the best. They took the *logos* from Heraclitus ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)). For Epicurus the gods are idle and the world an accident; for the Stoics the world *is* god, and nothing in it is an accident.

**Ethics.** The goal of life is to live in agreement with nature, which for a rational animal means in agreement with reason, which means virtuously. Laertius reports:

> Zeno was the first writer who, in his treatise on the Nature of Man, said, that the chief good was confessedly to live according to nature; which is to live according to virtue, for nature leads us to this point.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 7.87 (tr. Yonge)

The radical claim, inherited from the Cynics and ultimately from Socrates, is that **virtue is the only good** and vice the only evil. Health, wealth, reputation and life itself are "indifferents." Health is "preferred" to sickness, and the wise pursue it, but such things cannot make a life good or bad. This is where the Stoics differ from Aristotle, for whom external goods are part of happiness ([Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md)), and from Epicurus, for whom virtue is a means to pleasure.

**The dichotomy of control.** The practical core of Roman Stoicism is a single distinction. Epictetus, a former slave who taught in Nicopolis around 100 CE, opens his *Handbook* with it:

> There are things which are within our power, and there are things which are beyond our power. Within our power are opinion, aim, desire, aversion, and, in one word, whatever affairs are our own. Beyond our power are body, property, reputation, office, and, in one word, whatever are not properly our own affairs.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 1 (tr. Higginson)

Put your good in the first category and you cannot be harmed; put it in the second and you are a slave to whoever controls it. The diagnosis of suffering follows:

> Men are disturbed not by things, but by the views which they take of things. Thus death is nothing terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death, that it is terrible.
>
> — Epictetus, *Enchiridion* 5 (tr. Higginson)

Via Albert Ellis and Aaron Beck, that sentence became a founding idea of cognitive behavioral therapy: change the judgment and the emotion changes. Marcus Aurelius, writing private notes on campaign, put it as a formula: things "reach not unto the soul," and

> it is from the opinion only which is within, that all the tumult and all the trouble doth proceed. … This world is mere change, and this life, opinion.
>
> — Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.3 (tr. Casaubon)

**Stoics and Epicureans compared.** Both are materialist, both seek freedom from disturbance, and both locate our misery in false judgments. But the Stoic universe is providential and the Epicurean one is not; the Stoic good is virtue, the Epicurean good pleasure; the Stoic is a citizen of the cosmos with duties to every rational being, the Epicurean withdraws into a garden of friends.

## The Skeptics: peace through suspended judgment

The Stoics claimed that the wise person has secure knowledge, grounded in "cognitive impressions" that could not be false. The fourth Hellenistic movement denied that anyone has such knowledge, and claimed that giving up the search for it brings the peace the others promised. *Skeptikos* means "inquirer." There were two skeptical traditions, which sometimes merged: the Pyrrhonists, and the Academics in Plato's own school.

### Pyrrho

Pyrrho of Elis (c. 365–275 BCE) went east with Alexander's army and is said to have met the "naked sages" of India, whose influence on him is much debated (see [Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md)). He wrote nothing. Our main report says he taught the "necessity of suspending one's judgment," and lived by it:

> For he used to say that nothing was honourable, or disgraceful, or just, or unjust. And on the same principle he asserted that there was no such thing as downright truth; but that men did everything in consequence of custom and law. For that nothing was any more this than that. And his life corresponded to his principles; for he never shunned anything, and never guarded against anything; encountering everything, even waggons for instance, and precipices, and dogs, and everything of that sort; committing nothing whatever to his senses. So that he used to be saved, as Antigonus the Carystian tells us, by his friends who accompanied him.
>
> — Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 9.61–62 (tr. Yonge)

Laertius at once adds that Aenesidemus, a later Pyrrhonist, denied this and said Pyrrho never acted imprudently; and Pyrrho lived to nearly ninety, which is hard to do while walking into wagons. The wagon stories are probably hostile caricature, but they state the main objection to skepticism in its most vivid form: can anyone actually *live* without beliefs? A better anecdote, from the Stoic Posidonius, shows the ideal. On a ship in a storm, while the other passengers despaired, Pyrrho pointed to a pig on deck calmly eating, and said "that it became a wise man to preserve an untroubled spirit in that manner." (Yonge's translation garbles the scene so that Pyrrho himself is "exhibiting himself on deck eating a pig"; the Greek has him point to the pig.) The pig is untroubled because it has no opinions about whether the storm is bad.

### The Academy turns skeptical

Meanwhile, Plato's Academy turned skeptical. Around 268 BCE its head, Arcesilaus, revived what he took to be the real Socratic method: to argue on both sides of every question and affirm nothing, as Socrates did when he claimed to know only that he knew nothing ([Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md)). Cicero, himself an Academic, described the lineage:

> This manner of philosophizing, of disputing all things and assuming nothing certainly, was begun by Socrates, revived by Arcesilaus, confirmed by Carneades, and has descended, with all its power, even to the present age
>
> — Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* 1.5 (tr. Francklin, rev. Yonge)

The Academics' main target was the Stoic theory of knowledge. For any impression the Stoics called certain, Arcesilaus produced an indistinguishable false one: identical twins, two eggs, a dream, a hallucination. If a true impression can always be matched by a false one we cannot tell apart from it, no impression is certain, and the wise person must withhold assent.

Carneades (214–129 BCE) was the greatest of them. He read all the Stoics, especially Chrysippus, and said, "If Chrysippus had not lived, I should never have existed." In 155 BCE Athens sent him to Rome as an ambassador. According to later sources, he lectured one day in praise of justice and the next day, just as eloquently, against it; the elder Cato was so alarmed that he hurried the embassy's business through to get the philosophers out of the city before they corrupted the young. Carneades answered the charge that skeptics cannot act by developing a theory of the **persuasive** (*pithanon*): we may act on impressions that are convincing, consistent with others and tested, without claiming they are certainly true. This is an early probabilism, and a good description of how science and courts actually work.

### Sextus Empiricus

The Pyrrhonist tradition was revived by Aenesidemus in the first century BCE, and it is known to us almost entirely through Sextus Empiricus, a Greek physician of the late second century CE. His *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* is the only complete surviving handbook of ancient skepticism. Sextus defines skepticism not as a doctrine but as an ability (*dynamis*): the ability "to place the phenomenal in opposition to the intellectual 'in any way whatever,'" so that, through the equal weight of the opposed considerations, one reaches "first the state of suspension of judgment" and "afterwards that of imperturbability" (*Outlines of Pyrrhonism* 1.8, tr. Patrick).

Every element is technical. The skeptic opposes appearances to appearances (this tower looks round from far off but square close up), thoughts to thoughts, or appearances to thoughts. The opposing considerations turn out to be of equal weight (*isostheneia*). Unable to prefer either, the skeptic suspends judgment (*epochē*), which Sextus defines as "a holding back of the opinion, in consequence of which we neither deny nor affirm anything." And then, unexpectedly, tranquility (*ataraxia*) follows, "repose and tranquillity of soul."

Why should suspending judgment bring peace? Sextus explains with a parable:

> For that which is related of Apelles the painter happened to the Sceptic. It is said that as he was once painting a horse he wished to represent the foam of his mouth in the picture, but he could not succeed in doing so, and he gave it up and threw the sponge at the picture with which he had wiped the colors from the painting. As soon, however, as it touched the picture it produced a good copy of the foam.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* 1.28 (tr. Patrick)

The skeptics began, like everyone, by seeking truth in order to be at peace. They failed to settle the disputes and gave up, and peace came anyway, "as if by chance, as the shadow follows a body." The reason is psychological. Someone who believes that things are good or bad *by nature* is anxious twice over: anxious when he lacks the good things, and anxious to keep them when he has them. Someone who holds no such belief still feels hunger and pain, but is spared the extra layer of torment that comes from judging them terrible.

The machinery for producing equal weight consisted of standard argument patterns, the **modes** or tropes. The oldest set has ten. As Patrick's Sextus lists them, "The first is based upon the differences in animals; the second upon the differences in men; the third upon the difference in the constitution of the organs of sense; the fourth upon circumstances; the fifth upon position, distance, and place." The rest concern mixtures, quantities, relativity, frequency, and customs and laws. Each shows that the same object appears differently to different perceivers or under different conditions, and that we have no neutral standpoint from which to decide which appearance is true. Honey tastes sweet to us and bitter to someone with jaundice; dogs smell what we cannot; the Egyptians embalm their dead and the Romans burn them. A later set of five modes, attributed to Agrippa, is still the backbone of epistemology: any claim either rests on a disagreement, or on an infinite regress of reasons, or on a circular argument, or on an unproven assumption, or is relative. That is the "regress problem" every theory of justification since has had to answer.

The objection that Pyrrho walked into wagons comes back in a more serious form, the **apraxia** ("inaction") objection: a person with no beliefs cannot act, because action requires believing that this is food and that is a cliff. Sextus answers by distinguishing *appearances* from *beliefs*. The skeptic does not doubt that honey appears sweet: "it appears to us that honey is sweet. This we concede." What he suspends is the further claim about what honey is really like. And appearances are enough to live by. The skeptic follows a fourfold guide to everyday life:

> Sometimes it is directed by the guidance of nature, sometimes by the necessity of the feelings, sometimes by the tradition of laws and of customs, and sometimes by the teaching of the arts.
>
> — Sextus Empiricus, *Outlines of Pyrrhonism* 1.23 (tr. Patrick)

Nature gives him perception and thought; hunger leads him to food; custom tells him to be pious; his craft (Sextus was a doctor) tells him how to treat a patient. He does all this "without expressing a decided opinion." Is this coherent? Critics ask whether one can follow custom without at least believing that this is what custom requires. Whether the Pyrrhonist suspends *all* beliefs or only theoretical ones (the "rustic" versus "urbane" readings) is still debated.

Note the irony. The Skeptics promised the same *ataraxia* as Epicurus, by the opposite route. Epicurus said: learn the truth about atoms and death, and you will be at peace. Sextus said: stop trying to learn the truth, and you will be at peace. Two such opposed methods claiming the same result should make you wonder whether *ataraxia* is the same state in each case.

## What survived

The schools lost their institutional homes in the first century BCE, when the Roman general Sulla sacked Athens in 86 BCE, but the ideas spread through Rome. Cicero translated them into Latin and coined much of the West's philosophical vocabulary in doing so (*qualitas* and *moralis* among them). Stoicism became the moral idiom of the Roman elite and shaped early Christian ethics; Paul argued with Stoics and Epicureans in Athens, according to the Book of Acts, and the Church Fathers borrowed Stoic natural law ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)). Epicureanism, which denied providence and an afterlife, became the philosophy Christians most despised; Dante put Epicurus and "all his followers" in burning tombs among the heretics, those "Who with the body mortal make the soul" (*Inferno* 10, tr. Longfellow).

Then came a series of rediscoveries. In 1417 the Italian humanist Poggio Bracciolini found a manuscript of Lucretius in a German monastery, and atomism re-entered European thought; Pierre Gassendi in the seventeenth century built a Christianized Epicurean atomism that influenced Boyle and Newton ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)). Sextus was printed in Latin in 1562, and his modes landed on a Europe torn by the Reformation's quarrels over the criterion of religious truth. Montaigne had Pyrrhonist sayings painted on the beams of his library and took as his motto *Que sais-je?*, "What do I know?" Descartes' method of doubt was an attempt to defeat skepticism by going through it, and Hume called himself a mitigated skeptic and argued that nature, not reason, keeps us from Pyrrho's wagons ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)). Stoicism shaped Spinoza, Kant's ethics, and today's self-help shelves and therapy rooms. Cynicism lived on in Rabelais, Swift and every satirist since. The four Hellenistic answers to the question "how should I live in a world I cannot control?" are still the main options people reach for.

## Living it

- **Sort your desires.** List ten things you want this month. Mark each N (natural and necessary), U (natural but unnecessary), or V (vain: comes from opinion, comparison or status). For one V desire, ask what would remain if no one else could ever know you had satisfied it.
- **Practice the Cynic test.** Go one day without a convenience you assume you need: the phone, the car, the coffee. Notice how much of the discomfort is the thing's absence and how much is the thought of it.
- **Rehearse the Epicurean argument.** When a fear of death, or of any future loss, arises, ask: when it is present, will I be there to suffer it? Then ask what the deprivation account would say. Which feels truer at three in the morning?
- **Use the dichotomy of control.** Before a stressful event, divide a page in two: what is up to me, and what is not. Put your effort into the first column and your acceptance into the second.
- **Suspend one judgment.** Choose a contested question on which you hold a firm view you have not studied. Find the best argument on the other side and state it fairly. If the arguments now seem balanced, try saying "it seems so to me" instead of "it is so," and see whether you feel more anxious or less.

## Summary

- After Alexander's death in 323 BCE, philosophy turned to the question of how to live well, and without fear, in a world ruled by kings and fortune. The schools treated philosophy as medicine for the soul.
- The **Cynics** (Antisthenes, Diogenes, Crates, Hipparchia) opposed nature to convention, lived with almost nothing, and used public shamelessness as argument. Diogenes called himself a "citizen of the world."
- **Epicurus** built a cure on atomism: the gods do not intervene, the swerve allows worlds and free will, and the soul disperses at death. "Death is nothing to us": where death is, we are not. Lucretius added the symmetry argument; the deprivation account replies that death takes away future goods.
- The highest pleasure is the absence of pain and disturbance. Desires are natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, or vain. Friendship is wisdom's greatest gift, and justice is a contract for mutual protection.
- The **Stoics** held that a providential *logos* governs all, that virtue is the only good, and that we are disturbed by our judgments, not by things. See the [Stoicism course](../learn-stoicism/01-why-stoicism.md).
- The **Skeptics** recommended suspending judgment. The Academics Arcesilaus and Carneades attacked the Stoic criterion of truth; Carneades proposed acting on the persuasive. Sextus set opposing considerations of equal weight against each other through the modes, leading to *epochē* and then *ataraxia*, and answered the *apraxia* objection by living by appearances, nature, custom and craft.
- Through Rome, the four schools passed into Christian and modern thought: Lucretius into early modern atomism, Sextus into Montaigne, Descartes and Hume, the Stoics into ethics and modern therapy.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether death can harm the one who dies

**Epicurus:** You look troubled, my friend, and you tell me it is the thought of dying. Tell me, then: when you are dead, where will you be to suffer?

**You:** Nowhere, I suppose. That's exactly the problem. I won't be anywhere. Everything I care about will go on without me, and I'll miss all of it.

*Your turn: answer Epicurus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Epicurus:** "I'll miss all of it." Who will do the missing?

**You:** Nobody, I suppose. But I don't mean I'll feel a lack. I mean the lack will be real, even if nobody feels it. If I die at forty, I lose the forty years I would have had.

**Epicurus:** Lose them from where? Were they in your pocket?

**You:** They would have been mine. Say a man's friends mock him behind his back and he never finds out. Isn't he harmed?

**Epicurus:** Tell me what the harm consists in, if not in some pain he feels.

**You:** In being betrayed. In his life going worse than he thinks it is.

**Epicurus:** Then you hold that a man's life can go well or badly in ways he never feels. I deny it, but let it pass. When does this harm of death happen to you? While you are alive, you are not dead. When you are dead, you are not there to be harmed.

**You:** Maybe the harm doesn't happen at a time. It's a fact about my life as a whole: it was shorter than it could have been.

**Epicurus:** Then consider the time before your birth. Your life is shorter at that end too. You could have been born when Pericles was alive. Do you grieve over that?

**You:** No. But that's different. If I'd been born then, I wouldn't be me. I'd be a different person with different parents. I can't have started earlier. I could have ended later.

**Epicurus:** That is a better answer than most of my students give. Let us say, then, that death takes from you goods you might have had. Does fearing it help you get them?

**You:** No. Fearing it might even waste them.

**Epicurus:** Then even if you win the argument against me, you have not won the right to be afraid. A short life fully enjoyed is complete. A long life spent dreading its end is never enjoyed at all.

*What we learned:* The Epicurean argument depends on the premise that all harm is felt harm. The deprivation account denies it and survives the symmetry argument by noting that we could have lived later but not have been born earlier. Even if death can be bad for us, though, Epicurus' practical point stands: fear of death is a separate harm, and it falls on the living.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether anyone can live without beliefs

**Sextus:** You tell me the train leaves at nine. Do you know that it does?

**You:** Yes. It's on the timetable, and I've taken that train for years.

*Your turn: answer Sextus in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Sextus:** Has the timetable ever been wrong?

**You:** Occasionally. There are strikes and cancellations. But usually it's right.

**Sextus:** Then the timetable says nine, and experience says it is sometimes wrong. Each consideration pulls a different way. Which is stronger?

**You:** The timetable, obviously. It's right ninety-nine times in a hundred.

**Sextus:** And you know it will not be the hundredth time today?

**You:** No, I can't know that. But I don't need certainty to get on a train. I'll go to the station at nine.

**Sextus:** Excellent. So will I. You see, we do the same thing. The only difference is that you add, "and I know it leaves at nine." What does that addition give you?

**You:** Confidence, I suppose. And if it's late, the right to be annoyed.

**Sextus:** Ah. The right to be annoyed. I go to the station because it appears to me that the train leaves at nine, as hunger leads me to food. When it is late, I am inconvenienced, but I am spared the additional torment of thinking that something has gone *wrong*, against the nature of things.

**You:** But you can't do this all the way down. You must at least believe that you're hungry, that the station is over there, that trains exist.

**Sextus:** It appears to me that I am hungry; I do not doubt the appearance. What I doubt are the philosophers' claims about what hunger is and whether the station exists apart from my perceiving it. Those I leave to the dogmatists, and they have been arguing for six hundred years.

**You:** Then your skepticism is just for theories. In ordinary life you're exactly like me.

**Sextus:** In ordinary life I am like you, minus your anxiety. Is that a small difference?

*What we learned:* The *apraxia* objection says skeptics cannot act. Sextus replies that action needs only appearances, habits and needs, not beliefs about how things really are. Whether this skepticism is radical depends on where the line between appearance and belief falls, and the dispute between the "rustic" and "urbane" readings is about exactly that. The practical claim, that dogmatic confidence adds suffering when the world disappoints it, can be tested in one's own life.

</details>

### Dialogue 3 — On whether shame is only convention

**Diogenes:** You are embarrassed because I ate my lunch in the middle of the marketplace. Is eating shameful?

**You:** No, of course not. But there's a time and place for it. People don't eat in the middle of the square, and you're making everyone uncomfortable.

*Your turn: answer Diogenes in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Diogenes:** If eating is not shameful at home, how does it become shameful thirty paces away?

**You:** It's not the act. It's the setting. There are conventions, and they help people live together. Conventions about traffic aren't natural either, but you'd be a fool to ignore them.

**Diogenes:** A good answer. Some conventions are useful, like driving on the same side of the road. Which are useful and which are only habit?

**You:** I'd say a convention is useful if it prevents harm or helps us cooperate.

**Diogenes:** Then what harm does my eating in the market do?

**You:** Maybe none. But some conventions that look silly protect something. Covering our bodies, keeping some things private: they protect dignity, especially for people who are vulnerable.

**Diogenes:** And the convention that a rich man is honored and a poor man despised. What does that protect?

**You:** Nothing worth protecting. All right, that one I'd deface.

**Diogenes:** So you agree that the currency contains counterfeit coins. How will you find them, if not by testing each one?

**You:** By thinking about them, not by eating lunch in the square.

**Diogenes:** Thinking alone is cheap. You knew the convention about the rich was false before you met me, yet you bow to rich men. My lunch is an argument people cannot ignore. Your thoughts change nothing.

**You:** But you live on what other people give you. You need the society you're mocking.

**Diogenes:** I need bread and a little water. They need my contempt more than I need their bread.

*What we learned:* The Cynic strategy is to treat every convention as guilty until proved useful, and to test it by public violation. The test exposes real counterfeits: status, wealth, empty honors. But it can miss what some conventions protect, especially for the vulnerable, and the Cynic's independence depends on a society he refuses to support. The useful part is the question, which can be asked of any custom: who does this protect, and from what?

</details>
