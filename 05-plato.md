# Chapter 5 — Plato

Around 387 BCE a forty-year-old Athenian aristocrat bought land near a grove sacred to the hero Akademos, just outside the city walls, and started a school. It ran, in one form or another, for about three centuries, and its name has stuck to every academy since. The founder had been born Aristocles; his wrestling coach, the story goes, renamed him *Platon*, "Broad," for his build. He had watched his teacher Socrates be put to death by a democracy, had been sold into slavery (briefly, the legend says) after quarreling with a Sicilian tyrant, and would go back to Sicily twice more in the hope of turning a young tyrant into a philosopher-king. Both attempts failed. The writing did not. Plato left us some thirty-five dialogues, a few of disputed authorship, and as far as we know every work he published survives. They set the agenda for most of what came after. Alfred North Whitehead's famous line is only a slight exaggeration: the European tradition, he said, "consists of a series of footnotes to Plato" (*Process and Reality*, 1929).

This chapter covers the core of Plato's thought. We start with how to read him and why he wrote dialogues at all. Then come the theory of Forms, the *Meno*'s puzzle about learning, the *Phaedo*'s arguments that the soul outlives the body, and the long argument of the *Republic*: the soul in three parts, the just city and its philosopher-kings, and the sun, the line and the cave. We finish with the *Symposium*'s ladder of love, Plato's own criticisms of his theory, and his long afterlife. The ethics of the *Republic* gets its full treatment in [the Ethics course, Chapter 5](../learn-ethics/05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md). Here we concentrate on the metaphysics, the theory of knowledge, and the politics.

## How to read Plato

Plato never speaks in his own voice. Apart from a few letters of disputed authenticity, everything he wrote is drama: conversations among named characters at named places and dates. Socrates is usually the main speaker, but in the late dialogues he fades out, and in the *Laws* he does not appear at all. So "Plato thinks X" is always an inference. It is a reasonable one when the leading speaker argues for X at length and the same view comes back in several dialogues, but good readers remember that it is an inference.

Why dialogues? Part of the answer is in the *Phaedrus*. Socrates tells a story about the Egyptian god Theuth, inventor of writing, who presents his gift to the king Thamus:

> for this discovery of yours will create forgetfulness in the learners' souls, because they will not use their memories; they will trust to the external written characters and not remember of themselves. The specific which you have discovered is an aid not to memory, but to reminiscence, and you give your disciples not truth, but only the semblance of truth; they will be hearers of many things and will have learned nothing; they will appear to be omniscient and will generally know nothing
>
> — Plato, *Phaedrus* 274e–275b (tr. Jowett)

Swap "writing" for "search engines" or "chatbots" and the complaint has not dated. A written text, Socrates goes on, cannot answer back when questioned; it "always gives one unvarying answer." A dialogue is Plato's compromise. It is written, but it shows thinking as a live exchange, models objections and replies, and often ends without a verdict, so that the reader has to keep the argument going.

Scholars usually sort the dialogues into three rough groups. The dating comes partly from stylometry, the statistical study of prose style, which was pioneered on Plato in the nineteenth century.

| Period | Representative dialogues | Character |
|---|---|---|
| Early ("Socratic") | *Apology*, *Crito*, *Euthyphro*, *Laches*, *Charmides*, *Protagoras*, *Gorgias* | Socrates questions; definitions fail; usually *aporia* |
| Middle | *Meno* (transitional), *Phaedo*, *Symposium*, *Republic*, *Phaedrus* | confident doctrines: Forms, recollection, immortality, the tripartite soul |
| Late | *Parmenides*, *Theaetetus*, *Sophist*, *Statesman*, *Timaeus*, *Philebus*, *Laws* | self-criticism, technical method, cosmology, legislation |

This developmental story is standard but contested; "unitarians" read the dialogues as one philosophy laid out in stages, and Aristotle reports "unwritten doctrines" that appear in no dialogue. Treat every "Plato believed" as shorthand.

## The theory of Forms

### The problem

Recall the Socratic question from [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md): *what is* piety, courage, justice? Socrates wanted a single answer, one "idea" or "form" that all pious acts share and that makes them pious. What *kind of thing* is that answer? Not any particular pious act, since each is pious in one respect and impious in another.

Aristotle, who studied with Plato for twenty years, says the theory of Forms came out of exactly this pressure (*Metaphysics* 1.6, 987a29–b10). Plato had absorbed Heraclitus' teaching that the sensible world is always in flux and so cannot be known; then he met Socrates, who sought universal definitions. If knowledge needs a stable object and nothing sensible is stable, the objects of definition must be unchanging, non-sensible realities. Plato called them *eidē* or *ideai*, "Forms" or "Ideas," from the ordinary Greek for the look or shape of a thing. A Platonic Idea is not a thought in someone's head. It is what a true thought is *about*.

### The argument from imperfection

The *Phaedo* gives the clearest argument for Forms. Socrates asks Simmias whether there is such a thing as equality itself, over and above equal sticks and stones, and Simmias says yes, "and swear to it, with all the confidence in life." Two sticks that look equal will turn out, measured closely, to differ by a hair. They may look equal from one angle and unequal from another. Yet we judge them "equal but falling short," and we could not judge them to fall short unless we already grasped the standard:

> Then we must have known equality previously to the time when we first saw the material equals, and reflected that all these apparent equals strive to attain absolute equality, but fall short of it?
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 75a (tr. Jowett)

Think of a geometry lesson. No line you draw is perfectly straight and no circle is perfectly round, yet the theorems are about perfect lines and circles, and everyone who follows the proof understands what is meant. Plato's suggestion is that the object of geometry, and of ethics, is the perfect thing that the drawings imitate.

### What a Form is

Pieced together from the middle dialogues, the Forms are:

| Feature | Forms (Beauty itself, Equality itself, Justice itself) | Sensible things (a beautiful face, two equal sticks, a just act) |
|---|---|---|
| Change | eternal and unchanging | come to be and pass away |
| Purity | purely and unqualifiedly what they are | F in one respect, not-F in another |
| How known | by thought (*noēsis*) | by the senses, yielding opinion (*doxa*) |
| Relation | the original, the paradigm | copies; they "participate in" or "imitate" the Form |
| Being | fully real | "between being and not-being" (*Republic* 5, 478d) |

Two technical words matter. A sensible thing **participates in** (*metechei*) a Form: Helen is beautiful by sharing in Beauty. And the Forms are **separate** (*chōris*): they exist independently of their instances, and would exist if nothing in the world were beautiful. That separation is the theory's boldest and most controversial claim. Aristotle accepted that universals are real but put them *in* things, and the disagreement between the two of them launched what medieval philosophers called the problem of universals (see [Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)).

At the top of the hierarchy, in the *Republic*, stands the **Form of the Good**, which makes the other Forms intelligible as the sun makes visible things visible. More on that below.

## The *Meno*: can virtue be taught?

The *Meno* opens with no preamble at all. Meno, a rich young Thessalian and a student of Gorgias, asks Socrates whether virtue can be taught. Socrates says he does not even know what virtue is. Meno offers definitions, Socrates refutes them, and Meno compares him to the stingray whose touch numbs (we met this "torpedo" in [Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md)). Then Meno strikes back: how can you search for what you do not know, and how would you recognize it if you found it? Socrates restates the point as a formal dilemma, now known as **Meno's paradox** or the paradox of inquiry:

> You argue that a man cannot enquire either about that which he knows, or about that which he does not know; for if he knows, he has no need to enquire; and if not, he cannot; for he does not know the very subject about which he is to enquire
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 80e (tr. Jowett)

It sounds like a sophistical trick, but it bites. How do you search for something you cannot yet recognize? If you can recognize it when you find it, don't you already in some sense know it?

### Recollection

Socrates' answer is startling. He reports what "priests and priestesses" have said:

> The soul, then, as being immortal, and having been born again many times, and having seen all things that exist, whether in this world or in the world below, has knowledge of them all; and it is no wonder that she should be able to call to remembrance all that she ever knew about virtue, and about everything; for as all nature is akin, and the soul has learned all things; there is no difficulty in her eliciting or as men say learning, out of a single recollection all the rest, if a man is strenuous and does not faint; for all enquiry and all learning is but recollection.
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 81c–d (tr. Jowett)

This is the doctrine of **recollection** (*anamnēsis*). Learning is not loading new information into an empty container. It is recovering what the soul already knows but has forgotten. The paradox dissolves because there is a third state between knowing and not knowing: knowing latently, without being able to say what you know.

### The demonstration with the slave boy

Socrates calls over a slave boy who has never studied geometry and draws a square in the sand, two feet on a side, four square feet in area. How long must the side be to make a square of double the area, eight square feet?

The boy answers confidently: double the side, four feet. Socrates draws it; the new square has area sixteen, four times the original. Three feet, then? That gives nine. Now the boy is stuck, and Socrates turns to Meno:

> Do you see, Meno, what advances he has made in his power of recollection? He did not know at first, and he does not know now, what is the side of a figure of eight feet: but then he thought that he knew, and answered confidently as if he knew, and had no difficulty; now he has a difficulty, and neither knows nor fancies that he knows.
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 84a–b (tr. Jowett)

The "torpedo's shock" has done him no harm; it has made him want to learn. Then Socrates draws the diagonal of the original square and builds a square on it. The boy agrees, step by step, that it contains four half-squares of the original, eight square feet in all. The side of the doubled square is the diagonal.

Socrates insists he has taught nothing, only asked. Readers have objected ever since that the questions are leading. Even so, the scene shows something real: the boy comes to *see* why the diagonal works, and that seeing is not the same as being told. Whether we explain it by a past life, by innate structures of the mind (as Leibniz and later Chomsky did; see [Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)), or by the boy's capacity to recognize a valid proof, the *Meno* raises the question of **a priori knowledge**: knowledge that does not come from experience.

Socrates himself admits he is "not altogether confident" of some of this, but not of the main point:

> that we shall be better and braver and less helpless if we think that we ought to enquire, than we should have been if we indulged in the idle fancy that there was no knowing and no use in seeking to know what we do not know;—that is a theme upon which I am ready to fight, in word and deed, to the utmost of my power.
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 86b–c (tr. Jowett)

That is a pragmatic argument, not a proof. Whatever the truth about past lives, the *belief* that inquiry can succeed makes us better inquirers.

### Knowledge versus true opinion

Near the end the dialogue makes a distinction that epistemology still uses. A man with a true opinion about the road to Larissa will guide you there as well as a man who knows the road. So why is knowledge worth more? Socrates answers with the self-moving statues of the legendary craftsman Daedalus, which run away unless tied down:

> Now this is an illustration of the nature of true opinions: while they abide with us they are beautiful and fruitful, but they run away out of the human soul, and do not remain long, and therefore they are not of much value until they are fastened by the tie of the cause; and this fastening of them, friend Meno, is recollection, as you and I have agreed to call it. But when they are bound, in the first place, they have the nature of knowledge; and, in the second place, they are abiding.
>
> — Plato, *Meno* 97e–98a (tr. Jowett)

Knowledge is true opinion *tied down by an account of why it is true*. Here is the ancestor of the definition of knowledge as "justified true belief," which Plato examines again in the *Theaetetus* and which Edmund Gettier attacked in 1963 (see [Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)). The dialogue ends, characteristically, without deciding whether virtue can be taught. If it is knowledge it should be teachable, yet there seem to be no teachers of it. Perhaps good statesmen have had only true opinion, "by divine dispensation."

## The *Phaedo*: the soul and its immortality

The *Phaedo* is set in Socrates' prison cell on his last day. His friends, among them two Pythagorean-trained Thebans, Simmias and Cebes, are grieving, and Socrates is calm: the philosopher, he says, has spent his whole life practicing for death (we quoted that line in [Chapter 1](01-what-is-philosophy.md)). The reason is that the body gets in the way of thinking:

> For the body is a source of endless trouble to us by reason of the mere requirement of food; and is liable also to diseases which overtake and impede us in the search after true being: it fills us full of loves, and lusts, and fears, and fancies of all kinds, and endless foolery, and in fact, as men say, takes away from us the power of thinking at all.
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 66b–c (tr. Jowett)

This is **dualism**: soul (*psychē*) and body are different things, and the soul is the real self, a view that runs through Augustine to Descartes ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). But "men are apt to be incredulous," Cebes says; they fear the soul is scattered like smoke at death. Socrates offers four arguments.

1. **The cyclical argument** (70c–72e). Opposites come from opposites (waking from sleeping), so the living come from the dead as the dead from the living; otherwise everything would end up dead.
2. **The argument from recollection** (72e–77a). Our knowledge of Equality itself cannot come from the senses, so the soul had it before birth.
3. **The affinity argument** (78b–84b). The things that can be scattered are composite, changing and visible. The Forms are simple, unchanging and invisible. The soul is more like the Forms, "in the very likeness of the divine, and immortal, and intellectual, and uniform, and indissoluble, and unchangeable" (80b).

4. **The final argument** (102a–107b). Some things carry an opposite essentially: fire is always hot, three always odd, and neither can admit the contrary. The soul brings life to whatever it occupies, so it cannot admit death. It is deathless, and therefore (Socrates adds, too quickly) imperishable.

Each argument has a weak point, and the *Phaedo* says so itself. The best objection comes from Simmias. Perhaps the soul is like the **attunement** (*harmonia*) of a lyre:

> Suppose a person to use the same argument about harmony and the lyre—might he not say that harmony is a thing invisible, incorporeal, perfect, divine, existing in the lyre which is harmonized, but that the lyre and the strings are matter and material, composite, earthy, and akin to mortality?
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 85e–86a (tr. Jowett)

Yet when the lyre breaks, the attunement perishes with it. Being invisible does not prove independence. This is, in embryo, the view that the mind is a function or organization of the body, which is the core of modern **functionalism** ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)). Socrates' reply is that an attunement cannot oppose the strings it belongs to, while the soul often opposes the body's desires. That anticipates the divided soul of the *Republic*.

Then the poison, and the numbness creeping up from Socrates' feet:

> He was beginning to grow cold about the groin, when he uncovered his face, for he had covered himself up, and said—they were his last words—he said: Crito, I owe a cock to Asclepius; will you remember to pay the debt? The debt shall be paid, said Crito; is there anything else? There was no answer to this question
>
> — Plato, *Phaedo* 118a (tr. Jowett)

A cock was the customary offering to Asclepius, god of healing, by someone who had recovered from an illness. Nietzsche read the line as Socrates' confession that life itself is a sickness (see [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md)). Others read it as plain piety, or gratitude that his friends had been cured of their fear.

## The *Republic*

The *Republic* is the most read philosophy book ever written, and probably the most argued about. Its question is the one Thrasymachus forced in Book 1 ([Chapter 4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md)): is justice worth having for its own sake, or is it just a deal the weak make to protect themselves? In Book 2 Glaucon and Adeimantus restate that challenge in its strongest form. They ask Socrates to show that the just person is happier than the unjust one even if the unjust one has a perfect reputation and the just one is crucified. Glaucon's thought experiment for this is the Ring of Gyges, which makes its wearer invisible; we met it in [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md), and [the Ethics course, Chapter 1](../learn-ethics/01-what-is-ethics.md) builds a whole chapter on it. Socrates' reply takes the remaining eight books.

### The city and the soul

Justice exists in a city and in a person, so Socrates proposes to read the larger case first and the smaller by analogy. His "city in speech" begins as a simple community of producers (fit only for pigs, Glaucon scoffs). Admit luxuries and it needs soldiers, and the soldiers need rulers. The result is three classes:

| Class | Function | Virtue | Part of the soul | Metal in the myth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Rulers (guardians proper) | deliberate for the whole | wisdom | reason (*logistikon*) | gold |
| Auxiliaries (soldiers) | enforce, defend | courage | spirit (*thumoeides*) | silver |
| Producers (farmers, craftsmen, traders) | supply needs | (moderation, shared by all) | appetite (*epithumētikon*) | bronze and iron |

To keep this order stable, Socrates proposes what Jowett translates as "one royal lie." It is better known as the **noble lie**, a founding myth told to all the citizens:

> Citizens, we shall say to them in our tale, you are brothers, yet God has framed you differently. Some of you have the power of command, and in the composition of these he has mingled gold, wherefore also they have the greatest honour; others he has made of silver, to be auxiliaries; others again who are to be husbandmen and craftsmen he has composed of brass and iron; and the species will generally be preserved in the children. But as all are of the same original stock, a golden parent will sometimes have a silver son, or a silver parent a golden son.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 3, 415a–b (tr. Jowett)

Notice that the myth allows mobility both ways, and rulers are warned not to be "pitiful" toward their own children if those children are made of brass. Notice too that it is a lie, consciously told by the rulers for the good of the ruled. Whether that is wise statecraft or the template for propaganda is one of the questions the *Republic* leaves you with.

### The three parts of the soul

The city has three parts, so does the soul? Socrates' argument rests on a principle close to the law of non-contradiction ([Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md)): the same thing cannot act or be acted on in opposite ways, in the same part and in relation to the same thing, at the same time (436b). A thirsty man who refuses to drink, because he knows the water is bad, is pulled two ways at once. So something in him wants to drink and something else forbids it: appetite and reason. Then there is a third thing, anger, which can side with reason against appetite. Socrates' example is unforgettable:

> The story is, that Leontius, the son of Aglaion, coming up one day from the Piraeus, under the north wall on the outside, observed some dead bodies lying on the ground at the place of execution. He felt a desire to see them, and also a dread and abhorrence of them; for a time he struggled and covered his eyes, but at length the desire got the better of him; and forcing them open, he ran up to the dead bodies, saying, Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 4, 439e–440a (tr. Jowett)

Leontius is angry at his own eyes. The part of him that is disgusted with himself is neither the appetite (which wants to look) nor cool calculation. It is **spirit**, the seat of pride, shame and indignation. Anyone who has doom-scrolled through a disaster while despising themselves for it knows Leontius.

In the *Phaedrus* Plato turns this psychology into a picture: the soul as "a pair of winged horses and a charioteer," where "one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed; and the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him" (*Phaedrus* 246a–b). Freud's ego, superego and id are a distant descendant, though Freud drew the lines differently.

### Justice as inner harmony

Now Socrates can answer Glaucon. Justice in the city is each class doing its own work and not meddling in the others'. Justice in the soul is the same: reason rules, with spirit as its ally, and appetite obeys. Justice turns out to be less a matter of outward behavior than of the condition of the soul:

> But in reality justice was such as we were describing, being concerned however, not with the outward man, but with the inward, which is the true self and concernment of man: for the just man does not permit the several elements within him to interfere with one another, or any of them to do the work of others,—he sets in order his own inner life, and is his own master and his own law, and at peace with himself
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 4, 443c–d (tr. Jowett)

If that is what justice is, the answer to Glaucon follows: injustice is a kind of civil war inside the soul, and nobody is happy in a state of civil war. Justice is to the soul what health is to the body. Has Socrates changed the subject? Critics, starting with David Sachs in 1963, have said so. Glaucon asked about *conventional* justice (not stealing, keeping promises), and Socrates answered about *psychic* harmony. He needs the two to line up, and it is not obvious that a well-ordered soul could never cheat. [The Ethics course, Chapter 5](../learn-ethics/05-ancient-virtue-ethics.md) takes up this "Sachs problem" in detail.

### The three waves: women, family, philosopher-kings

In Book 5 Socrates braces for three "waves" of ridicule. The first is that women of the guardian class should receive the same education and do the same jobs as men, soldiering and ruling included:

> And if so, my friend, I said, there is no special faculty of administration in a state which a woman has because she is a woman, or which a man has by virtue of his sex, but the gifts of nature are alike diffused in both; all the pursuits of men are the pursuits of women also, but in all of them a woman is inferior to a man.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 5, 455d–e (tr. Jowett)

The sentence has a sting in its tail: Plato thinks women on average weaker, but sex irrelevant to *which* work a person is fit for. In an Athens where citizen women could not own property, that was radical. The second wave abolishes private families and property for the guardians. The third and biggest:

> Until philosophers are kings, or the kings and princes of this world have the spirit and power of philosophy, and political greatness and wisdom meet in one, and those commoner natures who pursue either to the exclusion of the other are compelled to stand aside, cities will never have rest from their evils,—nor the human race, as I believe,—and then only will this our State have a possibility of life and behold the light of day.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 5, 473c–e (tr. Jowett)

Socrates defends the idea with the parable of the **ship of state**: the sailors (politicians) fight over the helm and drug the strong but "a little deaf" owner (the people), while the true navigator (the philosopher) is dismissed as "a star-gazer." It is an argument from expertise: we let trained pilots steer ships, so why let the untrained steer the state? Dialogue 2 below tests it, and [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md) follows the debate into modern democratic theory.

### The sun, the line and the cave

What must the philosopher-kings know? Above all, the **Form of the Good**. Socrates will describe it only through images, and he offers three, the most famous passage in Western philosophy.

**The sun.** As the sun makes visible things visible and also makes them grow, so the Good makes the Forms knowable and gives them their very being:

> In like manner the good may be said to be not only the author of knowledge to all things known, but of their being and essence, and yet the good is not essence, but far exceeds essence in dignity and power.
>
> Glaucon said, with a ludicrous earnestness: By the light of heaven, how amazing!
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 6, 509b–c (tr. Jowett)

**The line.** Next, Socrates asks Glaucon to "take a line which has been cut into two unequal parts, and divide each of them again in the same proportion" (509d). The lower part stands for the visible world, the upper for the intelligible. Laid out from bottom to top:

| Section | Objects | State of mind | In the cave |
|---|---|---|---|
| D (highest) | Forms, culminating in the Good, grasped without hypotheses | understanding (*noēsis*) | the real things outside, and the sun |
| C | mathematical objects, reasoned about from assumptions, using diagrams | thought (*dianoia*) | reflections and shadows outside |
| B | animals, plants, artifacts | belief (*pistis*) | the statues carried along the wall |
| A (lowest) | shadows, reflections, images | imagining (*eikasia*) | shadows on the cave wall |

The lower half (A and B) is opinion, *doxa*, and the upper half (C and D) is knowledge. Mathematics sits on the knowledge side but below the top. The mathematician reasons validly from assumptions (that there are odd and even numbers, three kinds of angle) and does not ask what justifies them. **Dialectic**, philosophy proper, climbs past every assumption to a first principle that needs none, and then comes back down.

**The cave.** Finally the allegory itself, at the opening of Book 7:

> And now, I said, let me show in a figure how far our nature is enlightened or unenlightened:—Behold! human beings living in a underground den, which has a mouth open towards the light and reaching all along the den; here they have been from their childhood, and have their legs and necks chained so that they cannot move, and can only see before them, being prevented by the chains from turning round their heads. Above and behind them a fire is blazing at a distance, and between the fire and the prisoners there is a raised way; and you will see, if you look, a low wall built along the way, like the screen which marionette players have in front of them, over which they show the puppets.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 7, 514a–b (tr. Jowett)

"You have shown me a strange image, and they are strange prisoners," says Glaucon. "Like ourselves," Socrates replies. The prisoners see only shadows of puppets, hear echoes they attribute to the shadows, and hold contests over who can best predict which shadow comes next. Suppose one is freed and dragged up into the daylight. The light hurts, he wants to turn back, and at first the shadows seem truer than the objects. Only gradually can he look at reflections, then things, then the stars, and finally the sun. Now suppose he goes back down:

> And if there were a contest, and he had to compete in measuring the shadows with the prisoners who had never moved out of the den, while his sight was still weak, and before his eyes had become steady … would he not be ridiculous? Men would say of him that up he went and down he came without his eyes; and that it was better not even to think of ascending; and if any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 7, 516e–517a (tr. Jowett)

That last sentence is about Socrates. Plato then decodes the image himself: "the prison-house is the world of sight, the light of the fire is the sun," and the journey upward is "the ascent of the soul into the intellectual world" (517b). The cave has at least three layers of meaning.

- **Metaphysical.** There are grades of reality. The sensible world stands to the Forms as shadows stand to the things that cast them.
- **Epistemological.** There are grades of cognition, mapped onto the line, and moving up them is slow and painful.
- **Political and educational.** The freed prisoner must go back down and rule, though he would rather stay in the light. And the people in the cave may kill him for his trouble.

The educational lesson follows at once. Some teachers claim to put knowledge into the soul "like sight into blind eyes," but "the power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already" (518c), and the whole soul must be turned from becoming toward being. Education is *periagōgē*, "turning around," not filling a vessel: recollection again, without the past lives.

The curriculum follows: mathematics, astronomy and harmonics to draw the mind toward the intelligible, five years of dialectic from age thirty, fifteen years of practical service back in the cave, and only at fifty a turn at ruling, taken reluctantly:

> Whereas the truth is that the State in which the rulers are most reluctant to govern is always the best and most quietly governed, and the State in which they are most eager, the worst.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 7, 520d (tr. Jowett)

Whatever you think of philosopher-kings, that sentence is worth remembering at election time.

### The decline of constitutions

Books 8 and 9 tell how the ideal city would decay, through a sequence of constitutions each matched to a type of soul: **timocracy** (rule by the honor-loving, like Sparta), **oligarchy** (rule by the rich), **democracy**, and finally **tyranny**. Plato's portrait of democracy is a satire with real bite. Everyone is free to do as he likes, offices go by lot, and anyone who claims to be the people's friend is honored:

> These and other kindred characteristics are proper to democracy, which is a charming form of government, full of variety and disorder, and dispensing a sort of equality to equals and unequals alike.
>
> — Plato, *Republic* 8, 558c (tr. Jowett)

Democracy's excess of freedom, he argues, breeds a demagogue who becomes a tyrant, and the tyrannical soul, ruled by its most lawless appetite, is the most miserable of all. Book 10 attacks poetry as imitation "thrice removed" from truth and ends with the Myth of Er, in which souls choose their next lives.

Is the *Republic* totalitarian? Karl Popper, writing in wartime exile, called Plato the first great enemy of the "open society." Defenders reply that the book is about the soul as much as the city (a "pattern" laid up in heaven for the individual, 592b) and that the late *Laws* is far more moderate. Read Books 2 to 5 and decide.

## The *Symposium*: the ladder of love

The *Symposium* is a drinking party at which each guest gives a speech in praise of Eros, the god of love. The comic poet Aristophanes tells the best-known story: humans were once double creatures, rolling around like cartwheels, until Zeus sliced them in half, and ever since each of us has searched for our other half:

> And the reason is that human nature was originally one and we were a whole, and the desire and pursuit of the whole is called love.
>
> — Plato, *Symposium* 192e–193a (tr. Jowett)

Every "soulmate" in modern romance descends from this joke.

Socrates goes higher. He learned about love, he says, from a priestess, Diotima. Love is desire for what we lack, ultimately for "the everlasting possession of the good," pursued by "birth in beauty": children, poems, laws, ideas. The lover climbs from one beautiful body to all bodily beauty, then to beautiful souls, practices and laws, to the beauty of knowledge, and at last to Beauty itself:

> He who has been instructed thus far in the things of love, and who has learned to see the beautiful in due order and succession, when he comes toward the end will suddenly perceive a nature of wondrous beauty … a nature which in the first place is everlasting, not growing and decaying, or waxing and waning; secondly, not fair in one point of view and foul in another … but beauty absolute, separate, simple, and everlasting, which without diminution and without increase, or any change, is imparted to the ever-growing and perishing beauties of all other things.
>
> — Plato, *Symposium* 210e–211b (tr. Jowett)

The lover is to "begin from the beauties of earth and mount upwards for the sake of that other beauty, using these as steps only" (211c). This is the origin of "Platonic love," and it is the theory of Forms seen from the side of desire. But are the people we actually love just rungs to be climbed past? Gregory Vlastos argued in 1969 that this is Plato's blind spot: he cannot account for loving a whole individual for that person's own sake. The dialogue seems to know it. Right after Socrates sits down, the drunk Alcibiades crashes in to give a furious, funny, heartbroken speech about loving one particular man, Socrates himself.

## Plato against Plato: the critics of the Forms

### The *Parmenides*

The most penetrating early critique of the Forms was written by Plato. In the *Parmenides* a very young Socrates presents the theory to the great Eleatic ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)) and is taken apart. Which things have Forms? Socrates is sure about Justice and Beauty, unsure about Man and Fire, and embarrassed by the lowly cases:

> And would you feel equally undecided, Socrates, about things of which the mention may provoke a smile?—I mean such things as hair, mud, dirt, or anything else which is vile and paltry; would you suppose that each of these has an idea distinct from the actual objects with which we come into contact, or not?
>
> — Plato, *Parmenides* 130c–d (tr. Jowett)

Parmenides tells him he is "still young" and that philosophy will one day teach him not to despise "even the meanest things." Then comes the most famous objection, later nicknamed the **Third Man**. If many large things are large because they share in one Form, Largeness, then consider the large things *together with* Largeness itself. Largeness is also large, by self-predication. So there must be a further Form by virtue of which all of them are large:

> Then another idea of greatness now comes into view over and above absolute greatness, and the individuals which partake of it; and then another, over and above all these, by virtue of which they will all be great, and so each idea instead of being one will be infinitely multiplied.
>
> — Plato, *Parmenides* 132a–b (tr. Jowett)

The argument relies on two assumptions: that the Form of F is itself F ("self-predication"), and that nothing is F by virtue of itself ("non-identity"). Together they generate an infinite regress, the pattern of argument from [Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md). Since Vlastos's 1954 paper, scholars have debated whether Plato saw which premise to drop and whether he dropped it. The late dialogues (*Sophist*, *Philebus*) keep Forms but treat them more as kinds and their relations ("the greatest kinds": being, sameness, difference, motion, rest). Whether Plato ever answered the *Parmenides*, or simply moved on, is still disputed.

### Aristotle and the Cynics

Aristotle pressed the critique for a lifetime. His objections fill [Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md), and in the *Nicomachean Ethics* he prefaces his attack on the Form of the Good with a famous apology:

> Still perhaps it may appear better, nay to be our duty where the safety of the truth is concerned, to upset if need be even our own theories, specially as we are lovers of wisdom: for since both are dear to us, we are bound to prefer the truth.
>
> — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* 1.6, 1096a14–17 (tr. Chase)

The proverb *amicus Plato, sed magis amica veritas* ("Plato is a friend, but truth is a greater friend") is the medieval Latin descendant of that sentence.

The Cynic Diogenes ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) preferred ridicule. According to Diogenes Laertius, the Academy had defined man as a featherless two-footed animal:

> Plato defined man thus: "Man is a two-footed, featherless animal," and was much praised for the definition; so Diogenes plucked a cock and brought it into his school, and said, "This is Plato's man." On which account this addition was made to the definition, "With broad flat nails."
>
> — Diogenes Laertius 6.40 (tr. C. D. Yonge)

The joke has a philosophical edge. Definitions by genus and differentia were the Academy's working method (the *Sophist* and *Statesman* are full of them), and a single counterexample sinks a definition, as every student of Socrates knew.

### Nietzsche

Two thousand years later Friedrich Nietzsche turned the critique into a diagnosis: the two-world picture, a true world of Being behind a false world of becoming and the body, was a symptom of exhaustion with life, and Christianity, "Platonism for the 'people'" (*Beyond Good and Evil*, Preface), spread it. His attack on the "true world" returns in [Chapter 15](15-nietzsche.md) and is a major thread in [the Nihilism course, Chapter 2](../learn-nihilism/02-ancient-roots.md).

## Plato's long afterlife

Within a century the Academy itself turned skeptical, taking up the questioning side of Socrates ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)). Plotinus (third century CE) rebuilt Plato's system around the Good, now called the One, and through him Plato reached Augustine, Boethius and the theologians of three religions ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)). Today "Platonism" in the philosophy of mathematics names the view that numbers and sets exist independently of us and are discovered, not invented; Frege and Gödel held versions of it ([Chapter 18](18-the-analytic-turn.md)). The *Meno*'s question about what knowledge adds to true belief is where analytic epistemology still begins ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)), and every argument for rule by experts has a Platonic ancestor ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)).

## Living it

Plato's thought is unusually practical once you see what he was after: a life steered by understanding rather than by shadows. Some ways to try it:

- **Name your shadows.** Pick one belief you hold firmly on a contested matter: a diet, a political claim, a view about someone you know. Ask where it came from. If the honest answer is "an image of an image" (a headline about a summary of a study), you are at the bottom of the line. Decide whether to climb, or to hold the belief more loosely.
- **Tie down a true opinion.** Take something you believe that happens to be true, for example that the earth goes round the sun. Can you give the reason? If not, find it and explain it to someone. That is the Daedalus exercise: turning a runaway true opinion into knowledge.
- **Watch for Leontius.** For a week, notice moments when part of you wants something and another part is angry at the wanting. Plato's psychology says spirit is reason's natural ally. Can you recruit your self-respect to the side of your considered judgment?
- **Go back into the cave.** The freed prisoner's duty is to return. If you learn something that changes how you see, explain it, patiently, to someone still watching the wall. Expect resistance, and remember how the shadows looked to you before you turned around.

## Summary

- Plato (c. 427–347 BCE) founded the Academy and wrote dialogues, never speaking in his own voice. His views are inferences from dramatic texts, usually divided into early, middle and late.
- The theory of **Forms** posits eternal, unchanging, separate realities (Beauty itself, Equality itself, the Good) that sensible things "participate in" and imperfectly copy. It answers both the Socratic demand for definitions and the Heraclitean worry that the changing world cannot be known.
- The *Meno* poses the paradox of inquiry and answers it with **recollection**, illustrated by the slave boy's geometry. It also distinguishes **knowledge** from **true opinion**: knowledge is true opinion "fastened by the tie of the cause."
- The *Phaedo* defends the soul's immortality with four arguments (cyclical, recollection, affinity, final). Simmias' **attunement objection** anticipates functionalism.
- The *Republic* answers Glaucon's challenge by mapping a three-class city onto a **three-part soul** (reason, spirit, appetite). Justice is each part doing its own work, a harmony that makes the just person happiest.
- Book 5's **three waves** are equal roles for women guardians, no private families for the guardians, and **philosopher-kings**. The ship of state parable argues for rule by expertise.
- The **sun**, the **divided line** and the **cave** present the Good as the source of being and knowledge, rank imagining, belief, thought and understanding, and picture education as turning the whole soul toward the light.
- The *Symposium*'s **ladder of love** rises from one beautiful body to Beauty itself. Critics ask whether it leaves the individual beloved behind.
- Plato criticized his own theory in the *Parmenides* (the **Third Man** regress). Aristotle, the Cynics, Popper and Nietzsche pressed the attack from different directions. Platonism survives in theology, the philosophy of mathematics, epistemology and political theory.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether you are in the cave

**Socrates:** You carry a small glowing tablet everywhere, and you spend some hours each day watching images pass across it: people you have never met, places you have never been, quarrels about events you did not see. Tell me, are you a prisoner in the cave?

**You:** No. The cave is a metaphor for people who never question appearances. I know my feed is curated, and I can always look things up.

**Socrates:** You can look things up. On what?

**You:** On the same device, I suppose. Search results, articles, sometimes the original sources.

**Socrates:** So when you doubt one shadow, you check it against another shadow cast on the same wall?

**You:** That's unfair. Some sources are reliable. A peer-reviewed study is not a rumor.

**Socrates:** Agreed, and that is a real distinction, like the one between a shadow and the statue that casts it. But how many of the studies you cite have you read, and how many have you understood well enough to say why their conclusions hold?

**You:** Honestly, very few. I rely on people who have. Nobody can check everything, and trusting experts is rational.

**Socrates:** I think so too. But then you hold true opinions, at best, that are tied down by someone else's reasons and not your own. Do you remember what happens to Daedalus' statues when they are not tied down?

**You:** They run away. So my beliefs can be talked out of me by the next persuasive shadow.

**Socrates:** And in the cave, which prisoners won the honors?

**You:** The ones best at predicting which shadow came next. I suppose that's what people who are good at the feed are rewarded for: guessing the next trend.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps the question is not whether you are in the cave, since everyone begins there, but whether you have ever turned your head. Is there anything you understand from the inside, so that you could defend it against a clever objector without looking anything up?

**You:** A few things. My own work. Some mathematics. How my family argues.

**Socrates:** Then you know what the climb feels like. You cannot make it everywhere; not even the philosopher-kings could. Perhaps the practical lesson is this: know a few things truly, know which of your other beliefs are only borrowed, and hold the borrowed ones with the looseness they deserve.

*What we learned:* The cave is not only about naive people. It describes anyone whose beliefs are images of images. Relying on experts is reasonable, but it gives you true opinion, not knowledge. The Platonic remedy is not omniscience. It is to turn around in some domains, to know the difference between your understood and your borrowed beliefs, and to calibrate your confidence to match.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether the experts should rule

**Socrates:** When you are ill, do you put your treatment to a vote among your neighbors, or go to a doctor?

**You:** A doctor, obviously.

*Your turn: answer Socrates in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Socrates:** And on a ship, do you want the passengers to elect the captain each morning, or a trained navigator at the helm? Governing a city is surely harder than steering a ship.

**You:** I see where this is going. But "harder" is the problem. The doctor and I agree on the goal: my health. In politics people disagree about the goal itself. A doctor can't tell me whether I should value liberty over equality.

**Socrates:** You think there is no knowledge of the good, then, only preference?

**You:** I didn't say that. Maybe there is. But even if some people know it better, who picks them? Every ruler in history has claimed to be the wise one.

**Socrates:** A fair blow. Our city tested its rulers for fifty years before trusting them. Would you accept rulers who had passed such tests?

**You:** Who designs the tests? The current rulers. That's how you get a self-perpetuating caste, gold parents passing gold to their own children whatever the myth says.

**Socrates:** So your first objection is that no one can be trusted to identify the wise. Have you a second?

**You:** Yes. Even a wise ruler doesn't know my life from the inside. A doctor can read my blood test, but only I know whether the side effects are worth it to me. Voting is partly how people report what is happening to them.

**Socrates:** So democracy is a way of gathering knowledge, many people each knowing a little? An interesting thought, which I did not consider. But do the many in your city actually pool their knowledge, or do they shout at shadows?

**You:** Often they shout. I'll grant that democracy can be the noisy ship you described.

**Socrates:** Then perhaps we agree on more than it seemed. You want experts to advise, the many to decide, and some way to keep both honest. I wanted rulers who do not want to rule. Is there any arrangement in your city that selects for people reluctant to hold power?

**You:** Not really. Our elections reward people who want it most.

**Socrates:** Then that is a problem worth your thought, whatever you decide about kings.

*What we learned:* The argument from expertise is strong where ends are agreed, as in medicine and navigation, and weaker where the ends themselves are disputed. Democrats can answer Plato that no one can be trusted to select the wise, that citizens know things about their own lives that experts do not, and that many partial knowers may outperform a few experts. Plato's sharpest challenge survives these replies: systems that reward the desire for power tend to attract the wrong people.

### Dialogue 3 — On whether the number seven exists

**Parmenides:** Young friend, they tell me you are a mathematician. When you prove that there is no largest prime number, what is your proof about?

**You:** About numbers. The primes.

*Your turn: answer Parmenides in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Parmenides:** And are the numbers things you can see or touch?

**You:** No. I can see the numeral "7" written in chalk, but erase it and the number is still prime.

**Parmenides:** Then the number is not the chalk. Is it a thought in your head?

**You:** It can't be that either. If it were my thought, it would die with me, and seven would stop being prime. And your seven and mine would be two different numbers.

**Parmenides:** So it is neither in the world of the senses nor in any mind, and it does not change. You are sounding very like young Socrates with his Forms.

**You:** Maybe Plato was right about mathematics, even if he was wrong about mud.

**Parmenides:** Then let me ask you what I asked him. How do *you*, a creature of flesh living in time, come to know a thing outside space and time that never acts on anything?

**You:** By proof. I reason my way there.

**Parmenides:** But reasoning is a process in your body. How does a process in your body come to be reliably about objects that cannot touch it? When you see a tree, light travels from the tree to your eye. What travels from the number seven to you?

**You:** Nothing, I suppose. That's strange. But the proofs work. Bridges stand up because the mathematics is right.

**Parmenides:** Is that an argument that the numbers exist, or only that the mathematics is useful?

**You:** If it's indispensable to our best science, and we believe the science, don't we have to believe in what the mathematics talks about?

**Parmenides:** A clever reply. Some in your time make it. Others say the numbers are useful fictions, like the characters in a story we all agree to tell the same way. Which troubles you more: numbers that exist where nothing can reach them, or truths that are true about nothing?

**You:** Both. I'm not ready to choose.

**Parmenides:** Good. Young Socrates was not ready either, and I told him that he had taken up the Forms too soon, before he had trained himself in argument. Train, and come back.

*What we learned:* Mathematical Platonism is attractive because mathematical truths seem objective, timeless and independent of minds and marks. Its central difficulty, pressed by Paul Benacerraf in 1973, is epistemic: how could we know abstract objects that cause nothing? The "indispensability argument" (Quine, Putnam) and fictionalism are two modern responses. The *Parmenides* models the right attitude: take the theory seriously enough to attack it.
