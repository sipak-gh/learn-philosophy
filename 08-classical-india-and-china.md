# Chapter 8 — Classical India and China

In the same few centuries that produced Heraclitus, Socrates and Aristotle, people far from Greece were asking the same kinds of questions, and some answers that Greece never thought of. In northern India, forest teachers asked what the self is and whether it is identical with the ground of all being; a prince left his palace to find the cause of suffering, and came back teaching that there is no self at all. In China, as the old Zhou kingdom broke into warring states, a minor official named Kong Qiu taught that society could be repaired by ritual and humaneness. His critics answered that we should love everyone equally, or that human nature is bad and must be straightened like warped wood, or that all such moralizing is a symptom of the disease it claims to cure.

The German philosopher Karl Jaspers called this period, roughly 800 to 200 BCE, the "Axial Age," because so much of human thought seems to turn on it. The label is debatable, and so is the coincidence, but the fact is not: three civilizations, with almost no contact, each developed a tradition of reasoned argument about reality, knowledge and the good life. This chapter surveys the classical Indian tradition, from the Upanishads through the orthodox schools (*darśanas*), the Buddha and Nāgārjuna, and the classical Chinese tradition: Confucius, Mozi, Mencius, Xunzi, Laozi and Zhuangzi. The ethics of these traditions gets a full chapter in [the Ethics course, Chapter 7](../learn-ethics/07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md); here the emphasis falls on metaphysics, knowledge and argument.

## A word about comparison

Two mistakes are easy to make here. The first is to treat "Eastern thought" as a single mystical block, set against a rational "West." That is false. India had formal logic, atomism, materialism, skepticism and centuries of technical debate about perception and inference; China had logicians, utilitarians and hard-nosed political realists. The second mistake is the reverse: to translate everything into familiar Greek categories, so that the Buddha becomes a Humean and Zhuangzi a Pyrrhonist, and nothing new is learned. The parallels are real, and this chapter points them out, but each tradition asked its questions for its own reasons.

The texts also differ in form. Few are treatises. The Upanishads are dialogues and riddles attached to ritual manuals; the Buddhist sermons were memorized and recited for centuries before being written down; the *Analects* is a collection of remarks by Confucius and his disciples; Zhuangzi writes fables and jokes. Greek philosophy also began in poems (Parmenides) and dialogues (Plato), so this is not a reason to deny these texts the name of philosophy. It is a reason to read them slowly, as [Chapter 1](01-what-is-philosophy.md) recommended, asking what question each passage answers and what argument, if any, supports the answer.

All quotations below are from nineteenth- and early twentieth-century translations, which are in the public domain. Modern translations are often more accurate, and the procure-yourself titles above are the place to start.

## India: the Upanishads

The oldest Indian texts are the *Vedas*, hymns and ritual formulas composed from about 1500 BCE. Around 800 BCE, at the end of the Vedic corpus, appear the *Upanishads* ("sitting down near," that is, secret teachings received at the feet of a teacher). They turn from the outer ritual to its inner meaning, and from the gods to a single question: what is the ultimate reality, and what am I?

The most dramatic is the *Katha Upanishad*. A boy, Nachiketas, is sent by his angry father to the house of Yama, the god of death. Yama, having kept him waiting, owes him three wishes. The third is the question that has haunted every chapter of this course so far:

> Nachiketas said: There is this doubt regarding what becomes of a man after death. Some say he exists, others that he does not exist. This knowledge I desire, being instructed by thee. Of the boons this is the third boon.
>
> — *Katha Upanishad* 1.1.20 (tr. Paramananda)

Death tries to buy him off with sons, cattle, gold, long life and "these lovely maidens with their chariots and musical instruments." The boy refuses: "Keep thou thy chariots, dance and music." Satisfied, Death begins the teaching with a distinction that Plato would have recognized:

> The good is one thing and the pleasant another. These two, having different ends, bind a man. It is well with him who chooses the good. He who chooses the pleasant misses the true end.
>
> — *Katha Upanishad* 1.2.1 (tr. Paramananda)

The answer to the question about death is the doctrine of the **Ātman**, the true self. It is not the body, the senses or even the mind, but the witness behind them, and it is untouched by death: "This Self is never born, nor does It die." The Upanishad pictures the person as a chariot:

> Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins.
>
> — *Katha Upanishad* 1.3.3 (tr. Paramananda)

Compare Plato's charioteer in the *Phaedrus* ([Chapter 5](05-plato.md)). Remember this chariot; the Buddhists will take it apart.

The second great doctrine is the identity of this inner self with **Brahman**, the ground of all being. In the *Chāndogya Upanishad* the sage Uddālaka Āruṇi teaches his son Śvetaketu, a conceited young man who has just finished twelve years of study. Everything made of clay, he says, is known by knowing clay; "the change is a mere matter of words, nothing but a name, the truth being that all is clay." Then he asks the boy to dissolve salt in water and taste it:

> 'Taste from the middle. How is it?' 'It is salt.' 'Taste from the bottom, how is it?' 'It is salt.' ... The father said, 'Here also in this body you do not perceive the Real, but there it is. That subtle being by which this universe subsists, it is the Real, it is the Âtman and thou, Svetaketu, art It.'
>
> — *Chāndogya Upanishad* 6.13, as translated in Charles Eliot, *Hinduism and Buddhism*, vol. 1

*Tat tvam asi*, "That art thou," became the central formula of later Vedānta. Like the Eleatics ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)), Uddālaka argues that the many are appearances of one underlying reality, and he even uses a Parmenidean argument: "how could that which is be born of that which is not?" Unlike Parmenides, he identifies that reality with the self.

What can be said about Brahman? Nothing positive, according to the sage Yājñavalkya in the *Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upanishad*. Any description picks out some limited thing, and Brahman is not a limited thing. So the only way to indicate it is by negation: *neti neti*, "not this, not this." Dasgupta summarizes:

> Positive definitions were impossible. They could not point out what the Brahman was like in order to give an utterance to that which was unutterable, they could only say that it was not like aught that we find in experience.
>
> — Surendranath Dasgupta, *A History of Indian Philosophy*, vol. 1, ch. 3

The Upanishads also contain, in early form, three ideas that almost every later Indian school shared: **karma** (actions have consequences that carry over into future lives), **saṃsāra** (the cycle of rebirth, experienced as bondage), and **mokṣa** (liberation from that cycle through knowledge). Indian philosophy was therefore, like Hellenistic philosophy, explicitly soteriological: its aim was not only truth but release. The schools differed about what knowledge liberates and what, if anything, is liberated.

## The darśanas: orthodox and heterodox

Over the following centuries the arguments hardened into schools, each with a foundational text of terse aphorisms (*sūtras*) and a long tradition of commentary. A *darśana* ("view," from the root "to see") is a system. Indian doxographers classified them by their attitude to the Vedas:

> The Hindus classify the systems of philosophy into two classes, namely, the _nâstika_ and the _âstika_. The nâstika (_na asti_ "it is not") views are those which neither regard the Vedas as infallible nor try to establish their own validity on their authority.
>
> — Surendranath Dasgupta, *A History of Indian Philosophy*, vol. 1, ch. 4

The six **orthodox** (*āstika*) systems are usually grouped in pairs:

| School | Traditional founder | Core idea |
|---|---|---|
| **Sāṃkhya** | Kapila | Reality is two: pure consciousness (*puruṣa*) and matter (*prakṛti*). Liberation is consciousness recognizing that it is not matter. |
| **Yoga** | Patañjali | The practical discipline of Sāṃkhya: stilling the fluctuations of the mind to isolate consciousness. |
| **Nyāya** | Gautama (Akṣapāda) | Logic and epistemology: the valid means of knowledge (*pramāṇas*) and the rules of debate. |
| **Vaiśeṣika** | Kaṇāda | Atomism and a theory of categories: substance, quality, motion, universal, particularity, inherence. |
| **Mīmāṃsā** | Jaimini | The interpretation of Vedic ritual commands; a defense of the Vedas as eternal and authorless. |
| **Vedānta** | Bādarāyaṇa | Systematic interpretation of the Upanishads. Its most famous form, Śaṅkara's Advaita, holds that only Brahman is real. |

The **heterodox** (*nāstika*) schools are the Buddhists, the Jains and the Cārvākas.

Some of these deserve a closer look. **Nyāya** developed a theory of inference that invites comparison with Aristotle's syllogism ([Chapter 2](02-logic-and-argument.md), [Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md)). Its standard example has five members: (1) the hill is on fire; (2) because it has smoke; (3) wherever there is smoke there is fire, as in a kitchen; (4) this hill has smoke; (5) therefore it is on fire. The structure differs from Aristotle's in two telling ways. It is laid out for *persuading someone in a debate*, so it states the conclusion first and repeats it at the end. And the general rule always comes with an example, the kitchen, because the Naiyāyikas held that a universal is known through observed instances. Indian logic was always also epistemology: the question is not only what follows from what, but how we come to know.

**Advaita Vedānta**, systematized by Śaṅkara around 700 CE, pushed the Upanishadic identity to its limit. If Brahman alone is real and "That art thou," then the world of many things and many selves is *māyā*, an appearance superimposed on Brahman through ignorance, as a coiled rope in twilight is mistaken for a snake. Liberation is not going anywhere; it is seeing that the snake was always a rope. Compare Parmenides again, and the Platonic cave.

The **Cārvākas** (also called Lokāyata, "worldly ones") were the materialists of India, and their opponents preserved their views mainly to refute them. They accepted perception as the only valid source of knowledge, rejected inference to anything unseen, and therefore rejected karma, rebirth, gods and the authority of the Vedas. A fourteenth-century survey of all the systems, Mādhava's *Sarva-Darśana-Saṃgraha*, begins with them and quotes their verses:

> If a beast slain in the Jyotishtoma rite will itself go to heaven,
> Why then does not the sacrificer forthwith offer his own father?
>
> — Cārvāka verses attributed to Bṛhaspati, in Mādhava, *Sarva-Darśana-Saṃgraha* 1 (tr. Cowell and Gough)

and, a little later, "When once the body becomes ashes, how can it ever return again?" Their practical refrain could have been carved over the gate of Epicurus' Garden ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)): "While life is yours, live joyously." Their epistemological argument was sharper than their ethics: every inference depends on a universal connection (where there is smoke, there is fire), but we perceive only particular cases, never the universal, so how can inference be justified? Hume would ask the same question about induction two thousand years later ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)).

## The Buddha

Siddhārtha Gautama (fifth century BCE; traditional dates 563–483 BCE) was born into a ruling family in the foothills of the Himalayas. The legend says that his father shielded him from every sight of suffering until, on chariot rides outside the palace, he saw an old man, a sick man, a corpse and a wandering ascetic. He left home, studied with meditation teachers, nearly starved himself as an ascetic, and finally, sitting under a tree at Bodh Gaya, attained awakening (*bodhi*). "Buddha" means "the awakened one."

His first sermon, at the Deer Park near Benares, began by rejecting two extremes, self-indulgence and self-mortification, in favor of a **middle way**. Then came the **four noble truths**, which have the shape of a medical diagnosis, as the Hellenistic schools' teachings did. In the Dhammapada's compressed form:

> He who takes refuge with Buddha, the Law, and the Church; he who, with clear understanding, sees the four holy truths:--
>
> Viz. pain, the origin of pain, the destruction of pain, and the eightfold holy way that leads to the quieting of pain
>
> — *Dhammapada* 190–191 (tr. Müller)

(Müller's "the Church" translates *saṅgha*, the community of monks.) The disease is *dukkha*, usually translated "suffering" or "pain" but closer to "unsatisfactoriness": even pleasant things fail to satisfy, because they change and end. Its cause is craving (*taṇhā*, literally "thirst"). Its cure is the extinction of craving, *nirvāṇa* ("blowing out"). And the treatment is the eightfold path of right view, intention, speech, action, livelihood, effort, mindfulness and concentration.

### Not-self

The Buddha's most radical teaching was **anattā**, not-self. The Upanishads said that behind the body, senses and mind stands the true Self, the lord of the chariot. The Buddha examined each candidate (body, feelings, perceptions, mental formations, consciousness, the five "aggregates" or *skandhas*) and found that each is impermanent, and that whatever is impermanent cannot be a permanent self. There is no further thing, behind or beyond the aggregates, that owns them. When the tempter Māra challenged the nun Vajirā to say who made this "being," she replied with the same image the *Katha Upanishad* used, turned the other way:

> Here is a mere heap of _sankhâras_: there is no 'being.' As when various parts are united, the word 'chariot' is used (to describe the whole), so when the _skandhas_ are present, the word 'being' is commonly used.
>
> — Vajirā, in the *Saṃyutta Nikāya*, as translated in Charles Eliot, *Hinduism and Buddhism*, vol. 1

A chariot is real enough to ride in, but "chariot" names an arrangement of wheels, axle and frame, not an extra thing in addition to them. Likewise "I" names an arrangement of bodily and mental processes. The fifth-century commentator Buddhaghosa put the conclusion in verse:

> Misery only doth exist, none miserable;
> No doer is there, naught but the deed is found;
> Nirvana is, but not the man that seeks it;
> The path exists but not the traveller on it.
>
> — Buddhaghosa, *Visuddhimagga* 16, as quoted in Charles Eliot, *Hinduism and Buddhism*, vol. 1

This is strikingly close to David Hume's "bundle theory" of the self ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)) and to modern reductionist accounts of personal identity such as Derek Parfit's ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)). The difference is the motive. For Hume, the absent self was a puzzle; for the Buddha it is the cure. Craving and fear cling to a self. See that there is no self to cling to, and the grip loosens.

The obvious objections were raised in ancient India too. If there is no self, what is reborn? What carries karma from one life to the next? Who is liberated? The Buddhist answer is **dependent origination** (*pratītya-samutpāda*): each state arises in dependence on previous states, in a causal stream, as one candle flame lights another. Nothing passes over, yet the later flame is not unconnected to the earlier one. Whether that answer can carry the moral weight of responsibility is still argued.

### Impermanence and the mind

Three "marks" characterize all conditioned things: impermanence, unsatisfactoriness and not-self. The Dhammapada states them in three verses with the same refrain:

> 'All created things perish,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way to purity.
>
> 'All created things are grief and pain,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity.
>
> 'All forms are unreal,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity.
>
> — *Dhammapada* 277–279 (tr. Müller)

The emphasis on the mind is equally central. The collection opens: "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts." If a man acts "with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage." Epictetus' claim that we are disturbed not by things but by our judgments ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) is a close cousin.

### Questions not worth answering

The Buddha was famously reluctant to answer certain metaphysical questions: whether the world is eternal or not, infinite or not, whether the soul is the same as the body, whether an enlightened person exists after death. A monk named Māluṅkyāputta threatened to quit unless he got answers. The Buddha replied with a parable:

> Now suppose a man were wounded by a poisoned arrow and his friends called in a physician to dress his wound. What if the man were to say, I shall not have my wound treated until I know what was the caste, the family, the dwelling-place, the complexion and stature of the man who wounded me … The man would never learn all that, because he would die first.
>
> — *Cūḷa-Māluṅkya Sutta* (*Majjhima Nikāya* 63), as translated in Charles Eliot, *Hinduism and Buddhism*, vol. 1

Notice the logical form of the question about the enlightened person. Indian logicians recognized four possibilities, the "tetralemma" (*catuṣkoṭi*): he exists after death; he does not; he both does and does not; he neither does nor does not. The Buddha rejected all four. Is that a contradiction, a refusal, or a claim that the question presupposes something false (a self that could either survive or not)? The third reading is the most common, and it leads straight to Nāgārjuna. The ethics that grew from these teachings, compassion, non-harming and the precepts, are discussed in [the Ethics course, Chapter 7](../learn-ethics/07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md).

## Nāgārjuna and emptiness

About six centuries after the Buddha, Buddhist scholastics (the *Abhidharma* schools) had analyzed persons and things into momentary elements called *dharmas*, which they treated as the real building blocks of experience, each with its own intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*). The self was empty, they said, but the elements were real.

Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE), the founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school, applied the not-self argument to everything. Nothing whatever has intrinsic nature, because everything exists only in dependence on causes, parts and the concepts by which we pick it out. That lack of intrinsic nature is **emptiness** (*śūnyatā*), and Nāgārjuna's most famous verse identifies it with dependent origination itself.

His method is relentlessly dialectical. He takes a concept that seems indispensable (causation, motion, time, the self) and shows that every way of making it intrinsically real leads to contradiction. His treatise opens with causation. Dasgupta paraphrases the argument as Candrakīrti, Nāgārjuna's greatest commentator, presents it:

> All origination is false, for a thing can neither originate by itself nor by others, nor by a co-operation of both nor without any reason. For if a thing exists already it cannot originate again by itself.
>
> — Surendranath Dasgupta, *A History of Indian Philosophy*, vol. 1, ch. 5

If it came from something else, anything could come from anything ("from light we could have darkness!"); if from both, it inherits both problems; if from nothing, things would arise at random. Notice the four-cornered form again, now used as a tool of refutation.

Does this mean nothing exists? Nāgārjuna insisted not. He distinguished two truths: **conventional truth**, in which tables, persons and causes exist and function perfectly well, and **ultimate truth**, which is that none of them exists by its own nature. Emptiness is not a deeper substance, nor nothingness; even emptiness is empty. Dasgupta's own heading for the school was "Nihilism," which shows how often Nāgārjuna has been read that way, and how much is at stake in the reading. The [nihilism course, Chapter 2](../learn-nihilism/02-ancient-roots.md) examines the charge in detail. The parallels with Greek skepticism are notable: some scholars think Pyrrho's encounter with Indian sages ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) explains Pyrrhonism's form. Both use opposed arguments to release the mind from fixed views and promise peace as the result. The difference is that Nāgārjuna presents his dialectic as the path to an insight; Sextus claims no insight at all.

## China: the Hundred Schools

Classical Chinese philosophy grew in a political emergency. The Zhou dynasty, which had ruled since about 1046 BCE, decayed into rival states, and from 475 to 221 BCE these states fought the long series of wars known as the Warring States period, until Qin unified China by force. Rulers competed for advisers who could tell them how to bring order, and wandering scholars competed to be hired. The result was the "Hundred Schools of Thought." Their central question was practical: how should we live together, and how should a state be governed? Metaphysics entered mainly as it bore on that question, through the concept of *Tian* (Heaven, the cosmic order) and *Dao* (the Way).

## Confucius: humaneness and ritual

Kong Qiu, honored as Kongzi or Kong Fuzi, Latinized by Jesuit missionaries as "Confucius" (551–479 BCE), was a minor official from the state of Lu who never achieved the political influence he sought. He taught a circle of disciples, who collected his sayings into the *Analects* (*Lunyu*). He presented himself as a conservative, not an innovator: "A transmitter and not a maker, believing in and loving the ancients." In fact he transformed the tradition he claimed to transmit.

Two concepts organize his thought. **Ren** (仁), which Legge translates "perfect virtue" and others "humaneness" or "benevolence," is the full excellence of a human being in relation to others. **Li** (禮), "propriety" or "ritual," is the whole body of rites, manners and customs, from sacrifices to ancestors to the way you greet a guest. The relation between them is the central puzzle of Confucian ethics:

> Yen Yuan asked about perfect virtue. The Master said, 'To subdue one's self and return to propriety, is perfect virtue. If a man can for one day subdue himself and return to propriety, all under heaven will ascribe perfect virtue to him. Is the practice of perfect virtue from a man himself, or is it from others?'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* 12.1 (tr. Legge)

The Cynic Diogenes ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)) thought conventions were counterfeit coins. Confucius thought they were the medium in which human goodness is expressed and learned. A bow is just a bend at the waist, but performed sincerely, it is respect made visible, and performing it shapes the feeling. Ritual without *ren* is empty; *ren* without ritual has no form. The modern philosopher Herbert Fingarette argued that Confucius had discovered the "magic" of ritual: how much of human life, from promises to apologies, consists in acts that do something just by being performed in the right way.

When a disciple asked for a single word to live by, Confucius offered **reciprocity** (*shu*):

> Tsze-kung asked, saying, 'Is there one word which may serve as a rule of practice for all one's life?' The Master said, 'Is not RECIPROCITY such a word? What you do not want done to yourself, do not do to others.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* 15.23 (tr. Legge)

This "silver rule," stated negatively, predates the Gospel version by five centuries.

Confucius' politics rested on moral example. Coercion produces outward compliance; virtue produces inner transformation:

> The Master said, 'If the people be led by laws, and uniformity sought to be given them by punishments, they will try to avoid the punishment, but have no sense of shame.
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* 2.3 (tr. Legge)

If they are led by virtue and ritual, the passage continues, they will have a sense of shame and become good. And the first step in government is to **rectify names** (*zhengming*): to make words fit realities, so that a "ruler" really rules and a "father" really acts as a father. "If names be not correct, language is not in accordance with the truth of things. If language be not in accordance with the truth of things, affairs cannot be carried on to success." A society that calls aggression "defense" and flattery "advice" has lost its grip on its own affairs.

Confucius was, in his way, a Socratic figure: a teacher without a book, more concerned with how to live than with cosmology, and candid about the limits of knowledge. "When you know a thing, to hold that you know it; and when you do not know a thing, to allow that you do not know it;-- this is knowledge." Asked about death, he replied: "While you do not know life, how can you know about death?" His own summary of his life is the best short description of moral education in any tradition:

> The Master said, 'At fifteen, I had my mind bent on learning. 'At thirty, I stood firm. 'At forty, I had no doubts. 'At fifty, I knew the decrees of Heaven. 'At sixty, my ear was an obedient organ for the reception of truth. 'At seventy, I could follow what my heart desired, without transgressing what was right.'
>
> — Confucius, *Analects* 2.4 (tr. Legge)

At seventy, the rules and the desires coincide. Aristotle's virtuous person, who takes pleasure in acting well ([Chapter 6](06-aristotle.md)), is the nearest Greek parallel.

## Mozi: impartial care

The first great critic of Confucianism was Mo Di, or Mozi (c. 470–391 BCE), perhaps a craftsman, who led a disciplined organization of followers that also served as a corps of defensive military engineers, hiring out to besieged cities. Confucians emphasized graded love: you owe more to your father than to a stranger. Mozi argued that this partiality is the root of the world's disorder. Thieves love their own households and rob others; states love their own state and attack others. The remedy is **impartial caring** (*jian ai*): regard others' families as your own, and others' states as your own.

Mozi defended this with what may be the first explicitly consequentialist argument in philosophy. The standard for judging any doctrine is whether it benefits the people: does it make them wealthy, populous and orderly? By that test, he argued, elaborate Confucian funerals and three-year mourning periods waste resources; expensive court music is a luxury the poor pay for; and offensive war is the greatest of crimes, theft and murder on a mass scale. He also built a theory of argument with three tests for any claim: its basis in the deeds of ancient sages, its fit with the evidence of the people's eyes and ears, and its usefulness in practice.

The Confucians answered fiercely. Mencius lumped Mozi with the egoist Yang Zhu as the twin dangers of the age:

> But Yang Chu's egoism excludes the claim of a sovereign, while Mo Ti's universal altruism leaves out the claim of a father. And he who recognises the claim of neither sovereign nor father is a brute beast.
>
> — Mencius 3B9, as translated in Herbert A. Giles, *A History of Chinese Literature*, Book 1

The debate between impartial and partial morality never ended. It reappears in the argument between utilitarians, who say each person counts for one, and their critics, who say a morality that does not let you prefer your own child is inhuman. The [ethics course, Chapter 7](../learn-ethics/07-confucian-buddhist-and-other-traditions.md), develops the Mohist argument further.

## Mencius and Xunzi: is human nature good?

Mencius (Mengzi, c. 372–289 BCE) was the great defender of Confucius a century after his death, and his central claim was that **human nature is good**. He did not mean that people always act well. He meant that everyone has innate "sprouts" of virtue, spontaneous feelings of compassion, shame, deference and approval of right, which grow into full virtues if cultivated and wither if neglected. His most famous illustration: anyone who suddenly sees a child about to fall into a well feels alarm and compassion, not because he wants credit with the parents or fears a bad reputation, but immediately.

He used the same strategy on a king who had spared an ox being led to sacrifice, because he "could not bear its frightened appearance," and ordered a sheep substituted. The people thought the king stingy. Mencius told him that his compassion was real and only needed extending: "You saw the bull, and had not seen the sheep." If the king could feel for an ox, he could feel for his suffering people. Ethics for Mencius is the cultivation and extension of feelings we already have.

His debate with the philosopher Gaozi, who held that human nature is neither good nor bad, shows his style of argument by analogy:

> Kao Tzŭ said, 'Human nature is like rushing water, which flows east or west according as an outlet is made for it. …' Mencius replied, 'Water will indeed flow indifferently towards the east or west; but will it flow indifferently up or down? It will not; and the tendency of human nature towards good is like the tendency of water to flow down.
>
> — Mencius 6A2, as translated in Herbert A. Giles, *A History of Chinese Literature*, Book 1

Is this an argument or a metaphor? Both sides use images, and the analogy proves nothing by itself; but it changes the question. The issue is not whether nature determines conduct, but which direction it inclines when left unobstructed.

Xunzi (c. 310–235 BCE), the last great classical Confucian, turned Mencius upside down. Giles calls the passage one "which hardly carries conviction," but it has convinced a great many readers since:

> By nature, man is evil. If a man is good, that is an artificial result. For his condition being what it is, he is influenced first of all by a desire for gain. Hence he strives to get all he can without consideration for his neighbour.
>
> — Xunzi, ch. 23, as translated in Herbert A. Giles, *A History of Chinese Literature*, Book 2

For Xunzi, goodness is like a straight board: wood is crooked by nature and must be steamed and pressed against a frame. Ritual and teaching are that frame, invented by the ancient sages precisely because people left to themselves fight over scarce goods. Notice that Xunzi is still a Confucian: both he and Mencius prize ritual, learning and the sages. They disagree about whether ritual draws out a nature that is already good or imposes order on one that is not. That disagreement anticipates the European argument between Hobbes, for whom human beings in a state of nature live in a war of all against all, and Rousseau, for whom society corrupts natural goodness ([Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)). Xunzi's students included Han Fei, the theorist of the Legalist school, whose harsh doctrine of rewards and punishments the Qin dynasty adopted to unify China.

## Laozi and the Dao

The Daoist classic, the *Daodejing* (*Tao Teh King*, "Classic of the Way and its Power"), is attributed to Laozi, "the Old Master," an archivist said to have been an older contemporary of Confucius. He is almost certainly legendary, and the text as we have it was probably compiled in the fourth or third century BCE. Its eighty-one short chapters of rhythmic verse have made it one of the most translated books in the world. It begins with a warning about language:

> The Tao that can be trodden is not the enduring and unchanging Tao. The name that can be named is not the enduring and unchanging name.
>
> — Laozi, *Tao Teh King* 1 (tr. Legge)

*Dao* means "way" or "path," and by extension a method or teaching; every school had its *dao*. Laozi uses the word for the source and pattern of all things, prior to names and distinctions, which cannot be captured in words. The *via negativa* of the Upanishads (*neti neti*) and of Christian mystics ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)) is the closest analogue.

The book's characteristic move is to reverse ordinary valuations. Where we prize the full, the hard and the active, it prizes the empty, the soft and the yielding:

> The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends. Clay is fashioned into vessels; but it is on their empty hollowness, that their use depends.
>
> — Laozi, *Tao Teh King* 11 (tr. Legge)

Water, which is soft and seeks the lowest place, wears away stone: "There is nothing in the world more soft and weak than water, and yet for attacking things that are firm and strong there is nothing that can take precedence of it." The political ideal is **wu wei**, "non-action" or effortless action: the sage ruler governs by not interfering, like a cook frying a small fish who knows that too much stirring breaks it. And the moral vocabulary that Confucians prized is, for Laozi, a symptom of the loss it laments:

> When the Great Tao (Way or Method) ceased to be observed, benevolence and righteousness came into vogue. (Then) appeared wisdom and shrewdness, and there ensued great hypocrisy.
>
> — Laozi, *Tao Teh King* 18 (tr. Legge)

People talk about filial piety only when families have stopped being harmonious; they praise loyal ministers only when the state is in disorder. A society that needs moral teachers has already gone wrong.

## Zhuangzi: the butterfly and the cook

Zhuangzi (c. 369–286 BCE) is the wittiest philosopher of the ancient world. His book, of which the first seven "inner chapters" are probably his own, is a collection of fables, dialogues, paradoxes and jokes. Its most famous passage is a dream:

> Once upon a time, I, Chuang Tzŭ, dreamt I was a butterfly, fluttering hither and thither, to all intents and purposes a butterfly. I was conscious only of following my fancies as a butterfly, and was unconscious of my individuality as a man. Suddenly, I awaked, and there I lay, myself again. Now I do not know whether I was then a man dreaming I was a butterfly, or whether I am now a butterfly dreaming I am a man.
>
> — Zhuangzi, ch. 2 (tr. Giles)

Descartes would use the dream to raise a skeptical problem that he then set out to solve ([Chapter 11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md)). Zhuangzi seems content to leave it open. His point is that perspectives are many, and none is privileged. In the same chapter, a sage named Wang I asks whose habitat is right, absolutely:

> If a man sleeps in a damp place, he gets lumbago and dies. But how about an eel? And living up in a tree is precarious and trying to the nerves;--but how about monkeys? Of the man, the eel, and the monkey, whose habitat is the right one, absolutely?
>
> — Zhuangzi, ch. 2 (tr. Giles)

Sextus' first mode, based on "the differences in animals" ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)), uses the same move to the same end: there is no neutral standpoint. Zhuangzi was also arguing against the Confucians and Mohists, who each declared their own "this" right and the other's "that" wrong. From the standpoint of the Dao, he suggests, such disputes look like the monkeys in his fable who were furious when told they would get three chestnuts in the morning and four at night, and delighted when told they would get four in the morning and three at night.

Zhuangzi is not simply a skeptic, though. His fables of skill show what living in accord with the Dao is like. The cook of Prince Hui carves an ox so smoothly that his movements are like a dance. Asked his secret, he explains that he no longer sees the ox with his eyes:

> "A good cook changes his chopper once a year,--because he cuts. An ordinary cook, once a month,--because he hacks. But I have had this chopper nineteen years, and although I have cut up many thousand bullocks, its edge is as if fresh from the whetstone.
>
> — Zhuangzi, ch. 3 (tr. Giles)

The blade, having no thickness, passes through the spaces between the joints. The prince says he has learned from this "how to take care of my life." The lesson is not passivity but a kind of mastery that has become spontaneous, like Confucius at seventy, reached by a different road.

Zhuangzi's most famous argument is with his friend, the logician Hui Shi, on a bridge over the river Hao:

> "You not being a fish yourself," said Hui Tzŭ, "how can you possibly know in what consists the pleasure of fishes?"
>
> "And you not being I," retorted Chuang Tzŭ, "how can you know that I do not know?"
>
> — Zhuangzi, ch. 17 (tr. Giles)

Hui Shi presses: then by the same reasoning you cannot know the fish. Zhuangzi answers by going back to the original question: "You asked me how I knew in what consists the pleasure of fishes. Your very question shows that you knew I knew. … I knew it from my own feelings on this bridge." Is this a sophism, a joke about the word "how," or a serious point that knowledge of other minds starts from shared embodied engagement rather than proof? Readers still disagree, which may be Zhuangzi's intention.

Finally, death. When Zhuangzi's wife died, Hui Shi found him drumming on a bowl and singing, and was scandalized. Zhuangzi explained that at first he grieved, but then reflected that she had come from formlessness and returned to it, "passing from one phase to another like the sequence of spring, summer, autumn, and winter." To weep would "proclaim myself ignorant of these natural laws." Compare Epicurus' "death is nothing to us" ([Chapter 7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md)): both seek release from the fear of death, but Epicurus argues from the end of sensation, while Zhuangzi rests on belonging to a larger process of transformation.

## What comparison teaches

Set side by side, the three traditions converge on surprising points. All three produced a philosophy of the self (Ātman, the Platonic soul) and a critique of it (*anattā*, Hume much later). All three produced skeptical arguments from the relativity of perception (Sextus, Zhuangzi, Nāgārjuna's dialectic). All three treated philosophy as a cure: for suffering, for disorder, for fear. And all three asked whether goodness is natural or imposed (Mencius and Xunzi; Rousseau and Hobbes; Socrates against the Sophists).

The differences are equally instructive. Indian philosophy is oriented toward liberation from a cycle of rebirth, so its debates about the self have stakes that Greek debates rarely had. Chinese philosophy is oriented toward social and political harmony, so it asks how people become good together, through ritual, example and institutions, more than it asks what the good is in the abstract. Greek philosophy has the most developed tradition of argument for its own sake, and arguably the strongest interest in nature for its own sake, which is one reason natural science grew from it. But the Nyāya logicians argued about the structure of inference as carefully as any Greek, and the later Mohists wrote a treatise on logic and optics.

Whether "philosophy" is the right word for all this was once seriously disputed in Western universities, many of which still teach only the Greek line. The question is answered by reading the texts. Arguments are given, objections raised and answered, concepts analyzed and distinctions drawn. If that is not philosophy, nothing in this course is.

## Living it

- **Ask the third boon.** Nachiketas refused wealth and pleasure in exchange for an answer. Write down the question you would ask Death, then the things that, honestly, you would accept instead. What does the list show about what you value?
- **Look for the self.** Sit quietly for five minutes and look for the "I" that is having your experiences. Notice what you find: sensations, thoughts, feelings, images. Do you find a further thing that has them? Hume and the Buddha reported that they did not.
- **Practice reciprocity, negatively.** For one day, before any act that affects another person, ask Confucius' question: would I want this done to me? The negative form is easier to apply than the positive one, and catches more.
- **Rectify one name.** Find a word you use loosely that matters: "friend," "busy," "emergency," "love." Write down what it should mean, and notice where your use of it does not fit the reality.
- **Cut like the cook.** Choose a task you do clumsily and practice it until you can find the "spaces between the joints." Notice the difference between forcing and flowing, and when effort becomes effortless.

## Summary

- From about 800 to 200 BCE, India, China and Greece each developed traditions of reasoned argument about reality, knowledge and life. Comparison is valuable but should not erase differences or reduce one tradition to another.
- The **Upanishads** teach that the true self (Ātman) is untouched by death and identical with Brahman, the ground of all being: "That art thou." Brahman can be indicated only by negation, *neti neti*. Karma, rebirth and liberation frame almost all later Indian thought.
- The **darśanas** divide into six orthodox systems (Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta) and the heterodox Buddhists, Jains and Cārvākas. Nyāya developed a five-membered theory of inference; Advaita Vedānta held the world to be an appearance of Brahman; the Cārvākas were materialists who accepted only perception.
- **The Buddha** taught the middle way and the four noble truths: suffering, its cause in craving, its cessation, and the eightfold path. His doctrine of **not-self** analyzes the person into changing aggregates, as "chariot" names an arrangement of parts. He set aside metaphysical questions that do not lead to liberation, as in the parable of the poisoned arrow.
- **Nāgārjuna** argued that everything is empty of intrinsic nature because everything arises dependently, and distinguished conventional from ultimate truth. Whether this is nihilism is disputed.
- **Confucius** taught *ren* (humaneness) expressed through *li* (ritual), the rule of reciprocity, government by example, and the rectification of names. **Mozi** countered with impartial caring judged by its benefits, an early form of consequentialism.
- **Mencius** held that human nature is good, with sprouts of virtue that need cultivation; **Xunzi** held that it is bad and must be straightened by ritual and teaching.
- **Laozi** taught a nameless Dao, the value of emptiness and softness, and *wu wei*; moral vocabulary arises when the Way is lost. **Zhuangzi** used fables and paradox to undermine fixed perspectives (the butterfly, the eel and the monkey, the fish), and to show skill and acceptance of death as living in accord with the Dao.

**You:** Fine. Suppose I'm a chariot, a bundle of processes. The sadness is still real. It still hurts.

**Vajirā:** I have not said it is unreal. I have said there is no owner behind it. What hurts more: sadness, or "my sadness, which says something about me, which I must defend or escape"?

**You:** The second, probably. The first is weather. The second is a story.

**Vajirā:** Then you have found the use of the teaching. The sadness will pass, as all conditioned things pass. The story keeps calling it back.

**You:** But if there's no one here, who's responsible for what I did yesterday? Who keeps promises?

**Vajirā:** The stream that made the promise is continuous with the stream that keeps it, as one flame lights another. That is enough for promises. It is not enough for pride.

*What we learned:* The not-self argument says introspection finds only changing processes, never an owner. It need not deny that experiences are real or that persons exist conventionally. Its practical claim is that much suffering comes from treating experiences as possessions of a fixed self. The hard question it leaves is whether causal continuity is enough to ground responsibility.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether goodness is natural

**Xunzi:** You say people are basically good. Leave two hungry children alone with one bowl of rice. What happens?

**You:** They might fight. But they might share, too. I've seen small children comfort each other when one is crying, without being taught.

*Your turn: answer Xunzi in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Xunzi:** Might. And which do you see more often?

**You:** Honestly, both. That's my point. There's something in them besides greed: the impulse to comfort is real, and nobody put it there.

**Xunzi:** Grant the impulse. Is it strong enough to build a state on?

**You:** Not on its own. It has to be cultivated. Mencius said it's a sprout.

**Xunzi:** And who cultivates it? Parents, teachers, rituals, laws. Then you agree with me that goodness comes from instruction.

**You:** No. A gardener doesn't create the sprout. If there were nothing there, no amount of teaching would produce compassion. You can't teach a stone to care.

**Xunzi:** Nor can you teach a crooked board to be straight without steam and a frame. The wood was capable of being straightened, yes. Is it therefore straight by nature?

**You:** The analogy cuts both ways. Wood doesn't want to be straight. Children do want to be loved and to belong. Rituals work because they give form to wants that are already there.

**Xunzi:** Then why have the sages needed ten thousand rules? If the wants were good, people would follow them without teaching.

**You:** Because we have bad wants too. Maybe human nature is mixed.

**Xunzi:** That was Gaozi's view, and Mencius mocked it. But say it is mixed. Which part should the state rely on?

**You:** Rely on the good and guard against the bad. Design institutions that don't need saints, but that make it easier for people to act on their better impulses.

**Xunzi:** That is very nearly what I said, and I would have reached it faster.

*What we learned:* Mencius and Xunzi agree that ritual and education are essential; they disagree about whether these develop a good nature or restrain a bad one. Each analogy (sprout, warped wood) captures part of the evidence. A mixed view is tempting, but it still has to decide which tendencies institutions should build on and which they should check, the question at the heart of later debates between Hobbes and Rousseau.

### Dialogue 3 — On how anyone knows what a fish enjoys

**Zhuangzi:** Look at the minnows darting about under the bridge. That is what fish enjoy.

**You:** You can't know that. You're not a fish. For all you know, they're fleeing a predator or just following chemical gradients. "Enjoy" is your word, projected onto them.

*Your turn: answer Zhuangzi in three or four sentences before opening the continuation. Then ask yourself what he will ask next.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Zhuangzi:** You are not me. How do you know that I do not know?

**You:** I don't know it for certain. But I can infer it. You don't have access to fish experience. I'm judging from general principles about knowledge.

**Zhuangzi:** And from those principles you know what I lack. Then you know something about another mind after all, though you are not inside it.

**You:** By inference from behavior and from what you're like. You're a human, like me.

**Zhuangzi:** And the fish is an animal, like you. It darts, it rests, it flees, it feeds. Is the gap between you and me so much smaller than the gap between us and the fish?

**You:** Yes. We have language. You can tell me what you feel.

**Zhuangzi:** And you believe me when I say it? People say they are happy when they are not. Language is also behavior.

**You:** All right. All knowledge of other minds is inference from the outside. But some inferences are better than others. I'm more confident about you than about the fish.

**Zhuangzi:** Good. Then we agree you do not demand certainty, only degrees. Now, when you first saw the minnows, before you began to argue, what did you see?

**You:** Fish having fun, honestly. It looked playful.

**Zhuangzi:** Then you knew it the way I knew it: from here, on the bridge. Your argument came later and told you that you could not know what you had just seen.

**You:** Maybe my first perception was a projection.

**Zhuangzi:** Maybe. And maybe your doubt is too. Whichever is true, the minnows are still darting.

*What we learned:* Hui Shi's challenge, that you cannot know another's experience without being them, would rule out knowing other human minds as well. Once certainty is given up, the question becomes how good our inferences are, and from what starting points. Zhuangzi suggests that understanding others begins in engaged perception, not in theory, and that skepticism, applied consistently, undermines itself as easily as it undermines belief.
