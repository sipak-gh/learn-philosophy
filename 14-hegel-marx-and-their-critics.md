# Chapter 14 — Hegel, Marx, and Their Critics

Kant had drawn a line around human knowledge: we know appearances, not things in themselves. His successors found the line intolerable. If the thing in itself is unknowable, how do we know it exists, or that it causes our sensations? Within twenty years a series of young German philosophers, Fichte, Schelling and above all Hegel, tried to overcome the division between mind and world by showing that reality itself is rational, a self-developing whole that comes to know itself in human thought and history. The result, **German Idealism**, is the most ambitious philosophical project of modern times. Its reception split the nineteenth century. Marx turned Hegel's dialectic of history into a theory of class struggle and revolution, with consequences that shaped the twentieth century. Schopenhauer turned Kant's thing in itself into a blind, insatiable will. Kierkegaard protested, in the name of the single individual, against any system that would dissolve a human life into world history. This chapter covers all four. Schopenhauer and Kierkegaard get fuller treatment in the nihilism course, so they are treated briefly here.

## After Kant: Fichte and Schelling

Johann Gottlieb Fichte (1762–1814) became famous overnight in 1792 when his anonymous essay on revelation was mistaken for Kant's. He argued that Kant's thing in itself was an inconsistent leftover: Kant said causality applies only within experience, yet he made the thing in itself the cause of our sensations. Remove it, and everything must be derived from the activity of the self, the "I," which "posits" itself and, in order to act and be free, posits a world that resists it, the "not-I." Fichte's idealism is ethical at its core: the world exists as the material of our duty. He is also remembered for his nationalist *Addresses to the German Nation* (1808).

Friedrich Wilhelm Joseph Schelling (1775–1854), Hegel's roommate at the Tübingen seminary along with the poet Hölderlin, objected that Fichte left nature as a mere obstacle. Schelling's *Naturphilosophie* treated nature as visible spirit and spirit as invisible nature, two expressions of an **Absolute** in which subject and object are identical. Hegel later mocked Schelling's Absolute, in which all distinctions vanish, as "the night in which all cows are black." The Romantics (the Schlegels, Novalis) shared this vision of a living, self-organizing nature, and through Coleridge and Emerson it reached English and American thought.

## Hegel: the system

Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) was a slow starter: a private tutor, then an unpaid lecturer at Jena, where he finished the *Phenomenology of Spirit* in 1806 as Napoleon's army took the city (he wrote to a friend that he had seen the Emperor, "this world-soul," riding out of the city to reconnoiter). He was a newspaper editor and then a headmaster in Nuremberg, where he wrote the *Science of Logic* (1812–16), and finally professor at Berlin from 1818, where he dominated German intellectual life until he died in a cholera epidemic. His lectures on history, art, religion and the history of philosophy were published posthumously from students' notes.

Hegel's prose is notoriously difficult, but the basic ambitions can be stated plainly:

1. **Against Kant's limits.** Kant tried to examine our faculty of knowledge before using it. Hegel thought this incoherent:

> But to seek to know before we know is as absurd as the wise resolution of Scholasticus, not to venture into the water until he had learned to swim.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *The Logic of Hegel* §10 (tr. William Wallace)

To draw a limit to thought, you must already think beyond it. The only critique of knowledge is knowledge itself, developing and correcting itself.

2. **Reality as a developing whole.** The truth is not a set of isolated propositions but a system in which each part has its meaning through its relations to the whole. And this whole is not static; it develops through contradictions toward greater self-understanding.

3. **Spirit (*Geist*).** The subject of this development is "Spirit" or "Mind" (*Geist* means both): reason as it is realized in human consciousness, culture, institutions and history. Reality comes to know itself through us.

### Dialectic

Hegel's method is called **dialectic**, and it is widely misdescribed. Hegel almost never used the triad "thesis, antithesis, synthesis," which comes from Fichte and from popularizers. (Lee Hollander, introducing his Kierkegaard translations, describes 1830s Copenhagen, where Hegelianism was so popular that the very cobblers were using the Hegelian terminology, "with 'Thesis, Antithesis, and Synthesis.'") What Hegel describes is a process in which a concept or form of life, thought through, turns out to contain a contradiction that forces a transition to a richer concept that preserves what was true in the first. His word for this is *Aufhebung*, meaning simultaneously "cancel," "preserve" and "lift up."

The opening of the *Logic* is the standard example. The most abstract concept is **pure being**, being without any further determination:

> Pure Being makes the beginning: because it is on one hand pure thought, and on the other immediacy itself, simple and indeterminate
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *The Logic of Hegel* §86 (tr. William Wallace)

But being with no determinations at all is indistinguishable from **nothing**. And thinking the passage of being into nothing, and nothing into being, is thinking **becoming**:

> Becoming is the first concrete thought, and therefore the first notion: whereas Being and Nought are empty abstractions.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *The Logic of Hegel* §88 (tr. William Wallace)

Heraclitus ([Chapter 3](03-the-presocratics.md)) wins over Parmenides. Hegel claims dialectic is not just a method of thought but the movement of reality itself:

> Wherever there is movement, wherever there is life, wherever anything is carried into effect in the actual world, there Dialectic is at work. It is also the soul of all knowledge which is truly scientific.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *The Logic of Hegel* §81 (tr. William Wallace)

### Master and slave

The most influential passage Hegel wrote is a few pages in the *Phenomenology* on "lordship and bondage," which he summarized later in the *Philosophy of Mind*. The question is how self-consciousness arises. Hegel's answer: I become fully self-conscious only by being **recognized** by another self-consciousness. But each of two self-consciousnesses wants recognition from the other without granting it, so the encounter becomes a struggle:

> The fight of recognition is a life and death struggle: either self-consciousness imperils the other's like, and incurs a like peril for its own
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *Philosophy of Mind* §432 (tr. William Wallace)

If one kills the other, there is no one left to recognize the victor. So the struggle ends when one, preferring life to freedom, submits:

> While the one combatant prefers life, retains his single self-consciousness, but surrenders his claim for recognition, the other holds fast to his self-assertion and is recognised by the former as his superior. Thus arises the status of master and slave.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *Philosophy of Mind* §433 (tr. William Wallace)

Then the reversal. The master gets recognition, but only from someone he does not recognize as an equal, which is worthless. He consumes what the slave produces and becomes dependent. The slave, working on the world under the fear of death, shapes things and so sees his own mind embodied in them. Through discipline and labor he develops a self-consciousness the idle master lacks:

> This other, the slave, however, in the service of the master, works off his individualist self-will, overcomes the inner immediacy of appetite, and in this divestment of self and in "the fear of his lord" makes "the beginning of wisdom"—the passage to universal self-consciousness.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *Philosophy of Mind* §435 (tr. William Wallace)

The resolution is **mutual recognition**, in which each knows itself recognized by others it recognizes as free (§436). This short parable has had enormous influence: on Marx's theory of labor, on Alexandre Kojève's lectures in 1930s Paris (attended by Sartre, Lacan and Bataille), on Simone de Beauvoir's analysis of woman as "Other" ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)), on Frantz Fanon's *Black Skin, White Masks*, and on today's **politics of recognition** (Charles Taylor, Axel Honneth; [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md)). Susan Buck-Morss has argued that Hegel was thinking of the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), in which enslaved people won their freedom by fighting for it.

### History, freedom and the state

Hegel saw history as the progress of the consciousness of freedom. In the *Philosophy of History* he sketches the stages: in the ancient Orient one (the despot) was free; in Greece and Rome some were free; in the Christian-Germanic world all are known to be free, as human beings. Reason works through the passions of individuals, who pursue their own aims while unwittingly realizing a larger purpose (the "cunning of reason"). The scheme was also explicitly hierarchical, and Hegel could write:

> Whole continents, Africa and the East, have never had this idea, and are without it still.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *Philosophy of Mind* §482 (tr. William Wallace)

The dismissal of Africa as outside history in his lectures has made Hegel a central target of postcolonial critique, and a test case of whether a philosophy of universal freedom can be separated from its author's prejudices.

The *Philosophy of Right* (1820) presents the modern state as the culmination of freedom: not freedom as doing what you like, but freedom realized in rational institutions (the family, "civil society" or the market economy, and the state) in which individuals find themselves at home. Its Preface contains two of Hegel's most quoted lines. One he discusses himself in the *Logic*:

> my Philosophy of Law, p. xix, are found the propositions: What is reasonable is actual; and, What is actual is reasonable.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *The Logic of Hegel* §6 (tr. William Wallace)

Critics read this as a justification of the Prussian status quo. Hegel replied that "actual" (*wirklich*) does not mean whatever happens to exist, but what fully realizes its concept; a bad state is "existent" but not "actual." The other line is that philosophy always comes too late to tell the world what it ought to be: "the owl of Minerva spreads its wings only with the falling of the dusk." Philosophy understands an age when it is already ending. He had made the same point in his lectures on the history of philosophy:

> When Philosophy with its abstractions paints grey in grey, the freshness and life of youth has gone, the reconciliation is not a reconciliation in the actual, but in the ideal world.
>
> — G. W. F. Hegel, *Lectures on the History of Philosophy*, Vol. 1, Introduction (tr. E. S. Haldane)

At the summit of the system is **Absolute Spirit**, where Spirit knows itself in three forms: art (in sensuous form), religion (in pictorial representation) and philosophy (in concepts). Hegel's lectures on aesthetics include the thesis that art, in its highest vocation, belongs for us to the past: it no longer satisfies our deepest needs as it did for the Greeks.

### Right and Left Hegelians

After Hegel's death his school split. The "Right" or "Old" Hegelians read him as a defender of Christianity and the Prussian state. The "Left" or "Young" Hegelians (David Friedrich Strauss, Bruno Bauer, Feuerbach, Max Stirner, the young Marx) used his dialectic against both: if history is the progress of freedom, existing religion and politics are stages to be overcome. Strauss's *Life of Jesus* (1835) treated the Gospels as myth; Stirner's *The Ego and Its Own* (1844) rejected every cause, God, humanity, the state, as a "spook" haunting the only reality, the unique individual.

## Feuerbach: theology is anthropology

Ludwig Feuerbach (1804–1872) took the decisive step. In *The Essence of Christianity* (1841), translated into English by Mary Ann Evans (George Eliot) in 1854, he argued that God is a projection of the human essence:

> Thus in the first part I show that the true sense of Theology is Anthropology, that there is no distinction between the predicates of the divine and human nature, and, consequently, no distinction between the divine and human subject
>
> — Ludwig Feuerbach, *The Essence of Christianity*, Preface to the Second Edition (tr. George Eliot)

We take the best qualities of our species (reason, love, will) as infinite, project them onto an imaginary being, and then worship them as something alien:

> Consciousness of God is self-consciousness, knowledge of God is self-knowledge. By his God thou knowest the man, and by the man his God; the two are identical.
>
> — Ludwig Feuerbach, *The Essence of Christianity*, Chapter 2 (tr. George Eliot)

Feuerbach's point is not only debunking. Religion is "the dream of the human mind," and the dream shows us what we value; the task is to reclaim those qualities for humanity. This structure (human beings create something, lose sight of their authorship, and then are dominated by it) is the concept of **alienation**, and Marx applied it to economics.

## Marx: from interpreting to changing the world

Karl Marx (1818–1883) was born in Trier to a Jewish family that had converted to Lutheranism. He studied law and philosophy in Bonn and Berlin, wrote a doctoral dissertation on Democritus and Epicurus, and became a radical journalist. Expelled successively from Germany, France and Belgium, he settled in London in 1849, where he lived in poverty, researched in the British Museum Reading Room, and was supported by his friend and collaborator Friedrich Engels (1820–1895), whose family owned a cotton mill in Manchester. Engels's *The Condition of the Working Class in England* (1845) gave Marx his first picture of industrial capitalism. Volume 1 of *Capital* appeared in 1867; Engels edited the rest after Marx's death.

### Feuerbach turned into politics

Marx agreed with Feuerbach that religion is a human product, but asked *why* people need it. His answer, in the introduction to *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (published 1844), is that religion is "the sigh of the oppressed creature" and "the opium of the people": a consolation for real suffering, which will only disappear when the conditions that require it are changed. The critique of heaven becomes the critique of earth. In 1845 he jotted down eleven "Theses on Feuerbach," which Engels published in 1888. The last is the most famous:

> XI. Philosophers have only interpreted the world differently, but the point is to change it.
>
> — Karl Marx, "Theses on Feuerbach" XI, in Friedrich Engels, *Feuerbach* (tr. Austin Lewis)

### Alienated labor

In the *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*, unpublished until 1932, Marx argued that human beings are distinguished by free, conscious, creative production: we realize ourselves by transforming the world, as Hegel's slave did. Under capitalism, workers are alienated in four ways: from the **product** of their labor, which belongs to someone else and confronts them as an alien power; from the **activity** of labor, which is forced, a means to survive rather than an expression of themselves (they feel at home only when not working); from their **species-being**, their distinctively human creative nature; and from **other people**, who confront them as competitors or bosses. The manuscripts were rediscovered in the twentieth century and made the "young Marx" a humanist philosopher, admired by existentialists and Christian socialists who rejected Soviet orthodoxy.

### Historical materialism

Marx and Engels set Hegel's dialectic "right side up" (Marx said that in Hegel it was standing on its head): history is driven not by the development of Spirit but by the development of material production. The classic statement is the 1859 Preface:

> The mode of production in material life determines the general character of the social, political and spiritual processes of life. It is not the consciousness of men that determines their existence, but, on the contrary, their social existence determines their consciousness.
>
> — Karl Marx, *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy*, Author's Preface (tr. N. I. Stone)

The theory in outline:

| Concept | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Forces of production | Tools, technology, raw materials, labor power and skills |
| Relations of production | Who owns and controls the forces (lord and serf; capitalist and wage worker) |
| Base | The economic structure: relations of production |
| Superstructure | Law, politics, religion, philosophy, art, "ideology" |
| Contradiction | As the forces develop, the old relations become "fetters" on them, leading to revolution |
| Mode of production | Ancient (slave), feudal, capitalist, and (predicted) communist |

The ideas of each age serve the interests of its ruling class; *The German Ideology* says that the ruling ideas of each age have been the ideas of its ruling class. But people are not puppets of economic forces:

> Man makes his own history, but he does not make it out of the whole cloth; he does not make it out of conditions chosen by himself, but out of such as he finds close at hand. The tradition of all past generations weighs like an alp upon the brain of the living.
>
> — Karl Marx, *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Bonaparte*, I (tr. Daniel De Leon)

The same essay opens with one of Marx's best jokes: Hegel remarks that great historic facts and personages occur twice, but "He forgot to add: 'Once as tragedy, and again as farce.'" The farce was Napoleon's nephew's coup of 1851.

### The Manifesto

*The Communist Manifesto* (1848), written in a few weeks for a small society of workers, is the most widely read political pamphlet ever written. It opens:

> A spectre is haunting Europe—the spectre of Communism.
>
> — Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, Preamble (tr. Samuel Moore)

> The history of all hitherto existing societies is the history of class struggles.
>
> — Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, I (tr. Samuel Moore)

What is often missed is that the *Manifesto* contains the most powerful tribute to capitalism ever written. The bourgeoisie has created more productive power in a century than all previous generations together; it has torn apart feudal ties and:

> has left remaining no other nexus between man and man than naked self-interest, than callous "cash payment."
>
> — Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, I (tr. Samuel Moore)

> All that is solid melts into air, all that is holy is profaned, and man is at last compelled to face with sober senses, his real conditions of life, and his relations with his kind.
>
> — Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, I (tr. Samuel Moore)

This description of globalization and perpetual disruption reads as if it were written about the twenty-first century. But capitalism creates the class that will overthrow it: "What the bourgeoisie, therefore, produces, above all, is its own grave-diggers." The goal:

> we shall have an association, in which the free development of each is the condition for the free development of all.
>
> — Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, II (tr. Samuel Moore)

> The proletarians have nothing to lose but their chains. They have a world to win.
>
> — Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels, *The Communist Manifesto*, IV (tr. Samuel Moore)

### Capital

*Capital* tries to show how exploitation works even when every exchange is fair. Marx adopts the **labor theory of value** from Adam Smith and David Ricardo: the value of a commodity is determined by the socially necessary labor time required to produce it. Labor power is itself a commodity, bought at its value (what it costs to keep the worker alive and working). But a worker can produce more value in a day than the value of her wages. That difference is **surplus value**, the source of profit. Exploitation, for Marx, is not cheating; it is built into the wage relation. *Capital* also describes **commodity fetishism**: in a market economy, relations between people (who works for whom, under what conditions) appear as relations between things (prices), which seem to have a life of their own.

Marx said little about what communism would look like. Engels predicted that after the revolution, the state, which exists to enforce class rule, would gradually become unnecessary:

> the government of persons is replaced by the administration of things, and by the conduct of processes of production. The State is not "abolished." It dies out.
>
> — Friedrich Engels, *Socialism, Utopian and Scientific*, III (tr. Edward Aveling)

### Assessing Marx

The regimes that called themselves Marxist in the twentieth century did not see the state die out; they produced one-party dictatorships, famines and labor camps on an enormous scale. Defenders reply that Marx expected revolution in advanced industrial countries, not peasant societies, and never endorsed one-party rule; critics reply that a theory that dismisses individual rights as "bourgeois" and claims to know the direction of history invites exactly what happened (Karl Popper, *The Open Society and Its Enemies*, 1945; Isaiah Berlin). The labor theory of value is rejected by almost all economists. Yet Marx's questions remain central: Is work under capitalism alienating? How does economic power shape politics and ideas? Who benefits from what passes for common sense? G. A. Cohen's "analytical Marxism," the Frankfurt School's critical theory ([Chapter 23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md)), and contemporary debates about inequality and automation all continue his project. Marx's own question about work is examined further in [Chapter 22](22-political-philosophy.md).

## Schopenhauer: the world as will

Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860) despised Hegel, scheduled his Berlin lectures at the same hour as Hegel's in 1820, and lectured to an almost empty room. His *The World as Will and Idea* (1818; modern translations call it *The World as Will and Representation*) begins with Kant:

> "The world is my idea:"—this is a truth which holds good for everything that lives and knows, though man alone can bring it into reflective and abstract consciousness.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, Vol. 1, §1 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

But we also know ourselves from the inside, as **will**: striving, desire, effort. Schopenhauer concluded that the thing in itself, which Kant said was unknowable, is will: a blind, aimless striving that manifests itself in gravity, in plant growth, in animal instinct and in human desire. Since will is endless striving, and satisfaction only ends one desire before another begins, life is suffering:

> Thus its life swings like a pendulum backwards and forwards between pain and ennui.
>
> — Arthur Schopenhauer, *The World as Will and Idea*, Vol. 1, §57 (tr. R. B. Haldane and J. Kemp)

The ways out are temporary or radical: the aesthetic contemplation of art, especially music, which briefly frees us from willing; compassion, which recognizes the same will suffering in all beings and is the basis of morality; and, at the limit, the ascetic denial of the will, which Schopenhauer found in Buddhism and Hinduism (he was one of the first major European philosophers to read the Upanishads; [Chapter 8](08-classical-india-and-china.md)). He influenced Wagner, Tolstoy, Freud and, above all, the young Nietzsche. His pessimism is the subject of a full chapter in the nihilism course: [Schopenhauer and pessimism](../learn-nihilism/05-schopenhauer-and-pessimism.md).

## Kierkegaard: the single individual

Søren Kierkegaard (1813–1855) lived nearly all his life in Copenhagen (his longest absences were a few trips to Berlin), broke off his engagement to Regine Olsen in 1841 for reasons he never fully explained, and in a burst of creativity published, mostly under pseudonyms, *Either/Or* (1843), *Fear and Trembling* (1843), *The Concept of Anxiety* (1844), *Concluding Unscientific Postscript* (1846) and *The Sickness unto Death* (1849). He died at forty-two after a public campaign against the Danish state church.

His target was the Hegelian system, which in his view explained everything except the one thing that matters: how *I* should live. The system is like a palace in which the thinker lives in a shed beside it. Truth that matters for existence cannot be an objective result to be learned; it is a matter of how one relates to what one believes. "Subjectivity is truth," says the *Postscript*'s pseudonymous author, and "truth is subjectivity."

Kierkegaard describes three **stages** or spheres of existence. The **aesthetic** life seeks pleasure and interest and avoids commitment; its high point is the brilliant, melancholy and ironic voice of the "Diapsalmata" in *Either/Or*:

> Of all ridiculous things the most ridiculous seems to me, to be busy—to be a man who is brisk about his food and his work.
>
> — Søren Kierkegaard, *Either/Or*, "Diapsalmata," in *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

> Let others complain that the times are wicked. I complain that they are paltry; for they are without passion.
>
> — Søren Kierkegaard, *Either/Or*, "Diapsalmata," in *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

The aesthetic life ends in boredom and despair. The **ethical** life, represented by Judge William in *Either/Or*'s second volume, chooses itself, takes on obligations (marriage, work) and lives by universal rules. The **religious** life goes beyond the ethical through a "leap" of faith. *Fear and Trembling* tests this with the story of Abraham, commanded by God to sacrifice his son Isaac. The pseudonym Johannes de silentio refuses to soften the story:

> The fact is, the ethical expression for what Abraham did is that he wanted to murder Isaac; the religious, that he wanted to sacrifice him. But precisely in this contradiction is contained the fear which may well rob one of one's sleep.
>
> — Søren Kierkegaard, *Fear and Trembling*, in *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

If ethics is the universal, as Kant and Hegel thought, Abraham is a murderer. If he is the father of faith, there must be a "teleological suspension of the ethical," in which the single individual stands in an absolute relation to the absolute, higher than the universal. The **knight of faith** who has made this movement is invisible from outside:

> I examine his figure from top to toe to discover whether there be anywhere a chink through which the infinite might be seen to peer forth. But no! he is of a piece, all through.
>
> — Søren Kierkegaard, *Fear and Trembling*, in *Selections from the Writings of Kierkegaard* (tr. Lee M. Hollander)

In Hollander's rendering he looks like a parish beadle (in the Danish, a tax collector). He enjoys his dinner. He has given up everything in infinite resignation and received it all back "by virtue of the absurd." Kierkegaard's analysis of anxiety and despair, his emphasis on choice and the individual, and his indirect, pseudonymous style made him, decades later, the grandfather of existentialism ([Chapter 19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md)). For his account of despair see the nihilism course: [Kierkegaard and despair](../learn-nihilism/08-kierkegaard-and-despair.md).

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Metaphysical or non-metaphysical Hegel?** The traditional reading takes Hegel's Spirit as a cosmic mind, a kind of pantheistic God (Charles Taylor, Frederick Beiser). Since the 1970s, "non-metaphysical" or "post-Kantian" readers (Klaus Hartmann, Robert Pippin, Terry Pinkard) have read him as continuing Kant's project: a theory of the norms of thought and of the social practices of giving and asking for reasons. Robert Brandom has made Hegel central to analytic philosophy of language.

**The end of history.** Kojève read Hegel as saying that history ended with the modern state of mutual recognition; Francis Fukuyama's *The End of History and the Last Man* (1992) applied this to the end of the Cold War. Critics doubt that Hegel meant it and that history obliged.

**Young Marx or mature Marx?** Louis Althusser argued for an "epistemological break" around 1845 between the humanist philosopher of alienation and the scientific theorist of modes of production. Others see continuity: *Capital*'s fetishism is the 1844 alienation in economic form.

**Determinism.** Is historical materialism a technological determinism (Cohen's "functional explanation" of the superstructure by the base) or a looser claim that economic factors are fundamental "in the last instance" (Engels's late letters)? The *Brumaire* passage above supports the looser reading.

**Kierkegaard and ethics.** Does the teleological suspension of the ethical license fanaticism? Readers since Martin Buber have worried about it. Others (Edward Mooney, C. Stephen Evans) read *Fear and Trembling* as a critique of the complacent identification of ethics with social convention, written by a pseudonym who explicitly cannot understand Abraham.

## Living it

**Do a recognition audit.** Hegel says we become selves through recognition. Where do you seek recognition, and from whom? Do you recognize those people as equals whose judgment matters, or do you, like the master, want acclaim from people whose opinion you do not value? Social media counts are a convenient laboratory.

**Examine your work with Marx.** Take a week of your working life and ask the four alienation questions: Do I control or own what I produce? Is the activity itself an expression of me, or only a means? Does it use my distinctively human capacities? How does it shape my relations to coworkers? Then ask what, practically, would reduce one form of alienation. Not every answer requires a revolution.

**Spot the ideology.** Marx claims that the common sense of an era serves the interests of those with power. Choose one "obvious" belief of your society (about merit, about property, about what work deserves pay) and ask: who benefits if everyone believes this? This does not show the belief is false. It shows where to look harder.

**Kierkegaard's either/or.** Is there a commitment you are keeping open, "aesthetically," to avoid closing off options? Kierkegaard thought refusing to choose is itself a choice, and usually a choice for despair. Write down what choosing would cost and what not choosing is costing.

## Summary

- After Kant: Fichte's self-positing I and Schelling's Absolute removed the thing in itself and sought the unity of subject and object.
- Hegel: no critique of knowledge before knowing (Scholasticus and swimming); reality as a self-developing whole; Spirit; dialectic as *Aufhebung*, not "thesis–antithesis–synthesis"; being, nothing, becoming; the master–slave dialectic and mutual recognition; history as the progress of freedom (and its Eurocentrism); "what is reasonable is actual"; the owl of Minerva and philosophy's lateness; Absolute Spirit in art, religion, philosophy.
- Feuerbach: theology is anthropology; God as the projected human essence; alienation.
- Marx: Thesis XI; alienated labor; historical materialism (forces and relations of production, base and superstructure); people make history under inherited conditions; the *Manifesto*'s class struggle and its tribute to capitalism; surplus value and commodity fetishism; the withering away of the state (Engels); the record of twentieth-century communism and Marx's enduring questions.
- Schopenhauer: the world as idea and will; life swinging between pain and boredom; art, compassion and renunciation.
- Kierkegaard: against the System; truth as subjectivity; the aesthetic, ethical and religious stages; Abraham, the teleological suspension of the ethical, and the knight of faith.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether you need others' recognition

**G. W. F. Hegel:** You said you do not care what other people think of you. Is that true?

**You:** Mostly. I try to live by my own standards. Needing approval is a weakness.

*Your turn: Hegel will argue that your very sense of yourself depends on recognition from others. Before reading on, write down two or three ways in which your self-understanding might depend on other people.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I suppose my sense of being competent at my job comes from feedback, and my sense of being a good friend comes from how my friends respond.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** And your "own standards": where did you learn them?

**You:** From my parents, teachers, books, friends.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** Then even your independence from others' opinion was taught to you by others, and is maintained by the respect of those who share your standards. Would you feel the same about yourself if everyone you knew regarded you as a fool?

**You:** No. But that's different from needing approval from strangers online.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** Indeed. That is the distinction that matters. The master in my parable wanted recognition from a slave whom he did not recognize as an equal. What kind of recognition was that?

**You:** Empty. Like getting likes from accounts you think are idiots.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** And the craving is never satisfied, for the same reason. Recognition that means something comes from someone whose judgment you respect, whom you recognize in return.

**You:** So the problem is not needing recognition but seeking it in the wrong form.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** Yes. Self-consciousness is social through and through. The fantasy of a self that needs no one is the master's fantasy, and it makes him dependent on what he despises.

**You:** But there must be a place for standing alone. Kierkegaard's knight of faith, or a whistleblower who is condemned by everyone she knows.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** The whistleblower appeals to a community of recognition that does not yet exist, or to the principles her community claims to hold. She does not appeal to nothing.

**You:** Then here is my position. I was wrong to think I could be indifferent to recognition; my self-understanding depends on it. What I can do is choose whose recognition to seek, and make it mutual. And sometimes, as with the whistleblower, I may have to act for a recognition that will only come later, if at all.

**G. W. F. Hegel:** Which is how history moves. You will forgive me for saying that the slave, not the master, is the one who learns.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether your job alienates you

**Karl Marx:** What do you do for a living?

**You:** I work in software, for a large company. It pays well and the work is interesting.

*Your turn: Marx will argue that you might be alienated even if you are well paid and enjoy your work. What would alienation mean in your situation? Write two or three sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**Karl Marx:** Who owns the code you write?

**You:** The company. That's in my contract.

**Karl Marx:** Who decides what it will be used for?

**You:** Product managers, executives, ultimately shareholders. Sometimes I disagree with how a feature is used, but I can't do much about it.

**Karl Marx:** So the product of your labor belongs to others and is used for purposes you do not choose. That is the first form of alienation. Do you work because the activity expresses who you are?

**You:** Partly. But if I won the lottery, I would stop, or at least work on different things.

**Karl Marx:** Then the activity is, for you, largely a means. You are at home when you are not working, and not at home when you are.

**You:** That describes almost everyone's job. Isn't that just what work is?

**Karl Marx:** It is what work is *under these relations*. Human beings are the animals that transform the world consciously and freely. Work could be the expression of that. Under capitalism, it is bought and sold like any other commodity.

**You:** But I chose this job, freely. No one forces me.

**Karl Marx:** You chose among employers. Could you choose not to sell your labor to anyone?

**You:** Not without savings or some other income. But the company also takes risks, provides capital and pays me more than I would make alone.

**Karl Marx:** It pays you less than the value you create. Otherwise, why would it employ you?

**You:** That assumes the labor theory of value, which economists don't accept. Value also comes from the capital, the organization and the market.

**Karl Marx:** Let us set the theory aside. Grant that the company contributes something. Is the division of the product decided by you and your coworkers, or by those who own the company?

**You:** By the owners. Collective bargaining is weak in my industry.

**Karl Marx:** Then you need not accept my economics to see that you are subject to decisions about your own work in which you have no voice.

**You:** Here is where I come out. Your labor theory of value is not convincing to me. But your account of alienation describes something real: I don't control the product, the purposes or the conditions of my work, and I treat most of my working hours as a means. Whether the solution is revolution, unions, cooperatives, or working less and differently is a separate question that I need to think about.

**Karl Marx:** You have stopped interpreting and started asking how to change it. That will do for today.

</details>
