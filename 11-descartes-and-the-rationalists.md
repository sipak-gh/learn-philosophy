# Chapter 11 — Descartes and the Rationalists

On the night of November 10, 1619, a twenty-three-year-old French gentleman serving in the Bavarian army spent the day shut in a stove-heated room, thinking, and that night had three dreams that he took as a sign that he was called to rebuild all of human knowledge on a new foundation. René Descartes spent the rest of his life on that project, and the questions he posed still organize philosophy: How can I know anything for certain? What am I? How does my mind relate to my body? Can I prove that anything outside my mind exists? This chapter follows Descartes and the two greatest philosophers who tried to finish what he started, Spinoza and Leibniz, together with his most brilliant critics, from Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia to Pascal. The three are traditionally called **rationalists** because they held that reason, not sense experience, is the primary source of knowledge. Their systems are some of the boldest constructions in the history of thought: a world of two substances, a world of one, and a world of infinitely many.

Previous: [Chapter 10 — The Renaissance and the New Science](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md). Next: [Chapter 12 — The Empiricists](12-the-empiricists.md).

## What "rationalism" means

The labels **rationalism** and **empiricism** were fixed by later historians, partly under Kant's influence, and none of the philosophers in this chapter or the next called himself by either name. All of them did experiments; Descartes dissected cow eyes and Leibniz built a calculating machine. Still, the labels mark a real difference of emphasis:

| | Rationalists | Empiricists ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)) |
|---|---|---|
| Main source of knowledge | Reason, intuition, deduction | Sense experience |
| Innate ideas | Yes: God, self, substance, mathematics | No: the mind starts as a "white paper" |
| Model of knowledge | Geometry | Natural history and experiment |
| Typical metaphysics | Ambitious systems (substance, God, the soul) | Modest, often skeptical |
| Key figures | Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz (also Malebranche) | Locke, Berkeley, Hume |

The rationalists also shared a conviction that the world is **intelligible**: that its structure can be grasped by reason because it is, in some sense, rational. Leibniz turned this into a principle: nothing happens without a reason. That conviction was their glory and, Hume and Kant would argue, their downfall.

## Descartes: the search for certainty

René Descartes (1596–1650) was educated by the Jesuits at La Flèche, studied law, and then, as he says in the *Discourse*, resolved to study only "the great book of the world." He traveled and soldiered, settled in the Netherlands in 1628 for its freedom and quiet, and published his *Discourse on the Method* (1637) in French, so that people without Latin could read it. The *Meditations* followed in Latin in 1641, published with seven sets of objections from Hobbes, Gassendi, Arnauld, Mersenne and others, and his replies: an early form of peer review. In 1649 Queen Christina of Sweden summoned him to Stockholm and demanded philosophy lessons at five in the morning. He died there the next winter of pneumonia.

### A method and a provisional morality

The *Discourse* opens with a joke that is also a premise:

> Good sense is, of all things among men, the most equally distributed; for every one thinks himself so abundantly provided with it, that those even who are the most difficult to satisfy in everything else, do not usually desire a larger measure of this quality than they already possess.
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part I (tr. John Veitch)

If everyone has reason, disagreement must come from using it badly. Descartes therefore offers four rules: accept nothing as true that is not so clearly and distinctly presented that there is no occasion to doubt it; divide each difficulty into parts; proceed from the simplest to the most complex; and make complete enumerations so as to omit nothing.

While demolishing his old beliefs, he needs somewhere to live, so he adopts a **provisional morality**: obey the laws and customs of his country; act decisively even on uncertain opinions, as travelers lost in a forest should walk straight in one direction instead of circling; and a third, Stoic-flavored maxim ([the Stoicism course, Chapter 14](../learn-stoicism/14-stoicism-through-the-ages.md)):

> My third maxim was to endeavor always to conquer myself rather than fortune, and change my desires rather than the order of the world, and in general, accustom myself to the persuasion that, except our own thoughts, there is nothing absolutely in our power
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part III (tr. John Veitch)

### Meditation I: the method of doubt

The *Meditations* are written in the first person over six days, a spiritual exercise turned to epistemology. The First Meditation begins (in the 1680 translation, which keeps its period spelling):

> Some years past I perceived how many Falsities I admitted as Truths in my Younger years, and how Dubious those things were which I raised from thence; and therefore I thought it requisite (if I had a designe to establish any thing that should prove firme and permanent in sciences) that once in my life I should clearly cast aside all my former opinions, and begin a new from some First principles.
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* I (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

He will not examine beliefs one by one. He attacks their **foundations**, and he applies a strict rule: anything open to the slightest doubt will be treated as if it were false. The doubt advances in three waves.

1. **The senses sometimes deceive** (a distant tower looks round). But this does not touch close, clear cases: that I am sitting by the fire in a dressing gown.
2. **The dream argument.** But I have dreamed exactly that, "Tho all the while I am lying naked between the Sheets." There are no certain marks to distinguish waking from sleep. Yet even dreams are built from simple elements (extension, shape, number) and the truths of arithmetic hold awake or asleep.
3. **The deceiving God, or evil spirit.** Perhaps an all-powerful being makes me go wrong even when I add two and three. Descartes dramatizes this as a malicious demon:

> I will suppose, not an Infinitely perfect God, the Fountain of truth, but that some Evil Spirit which is very Powerful and crafty has used all his endeavours to deceive me; I will conceive, the Heavens, Air, Earth, Colours, Figures, Sounds, and all outward things are nothing else but the delusions of Dreams, by which he has laid snares to catch my easy beleif
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* I (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

This is the ancestor of every brain-in-a-vat and simulation scenario ([Chapter 21](21-knowledge-and-science.md)). Notice its function. Descartes does not believe in the demon; it is a device for holding a doubt in place. The doubt is **hyperbolic**: exaggerated on purpose, to find what survives even the most extravagant skepticism.

### Meditation II: I am, I exist

What survives is the doubter:

> Deceive me as much as he can, yet he can never make me not to Be, whilst I think that I am. Wherefore I may lay this down as a Principle, that whenever this sentence I am, I exist, is spoken or thought of by Me, 'tis necessarily True.
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* II (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

The famous formula *cogito, ergo sum*, "I think, therefore I am," appears in the *Discourse* (in French, *je pense, donc je suis*) and the *Principles*, not in the *Meditations*:

> I observed that this truth, I think, therefore I am (COGITO ERGO SUM), was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt, however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part IV (tr. John Veitch)

You met a version of this in Augustine's *si fallor, sum* ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)). What kind of truth is it? If it is an **inference** (premise: I think; suppressed premise: whatever thinks exists; conclusion: I exist), then the suppressed premise needs to be certain too, and the demon might deceive me about it. Descartes denied it was a syllogism: in his replies to the Second Objections he says it is recognized in a single act of mental intuition, not deduced from a general premise. Jaakko Hintikka ("Cogito, Ergo Sum: Inference or Performance?", 1962) argued that its certainty is **performative**: "I do not exist" defeats itself whenever I think or say it, as "I am not speaking" does when spoken aloud.

A sharper worry: what entitles Descartes to the word "I"? Georg Lichtenberg remarked in the eighteenth century that we should say "it thinks," as we say "it rains." The most the cogito establishes may be that thinking is going on, not that there is a single, persisting thinker.

Descartes next asks *what* he is. Not a body, since he can doubt that he has one. He concludes:

> In short therefore I am only a thinking thing that is to say, a mind, or a soul, or understanding, or Reason, words which formerly I understood not
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* II (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

"Thinking" (*cogitatio*) for Descartes covers every conscious state: "a thing, doubting, understanding, affirming, denying, willing, nilling, imagining also, and sensitive." Hobbes, whose objections were printed with the *Meditations*, pounced on the move from "I think" to "I am a mind":

> For in the same manner I may Conclude, I am a Walking Thing, therefore I am the Walking it self.
>
> — Thomas Hobbes, Third Objections to the *Meditations* (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

The thing that thinks, Hobbes suggested, might be a body. Descartes replied that he had not yet concluded anything about what the thinking thing is made of; that comes in the Sixth Meditation.

### The wax

To show that even bodies are known by the intellect rather than the senses, Descartes takes a piece of beeswax fresh from the comb, with its taste of honey, its scent of flowers, its color, shape and size, hard and cold, ringing when struck. He puts it by the fire. The taste and smell vanish, the color changes, it melts and stops ringing, "and yet the Wax remains." What remains is only "a thing extended, flexible, and mutable," and since it can take more shapes than I could ever imagine, my grasp of it is an act of the mind alone. The lesson anticipates the new science: the real properties of bodies are the geometrical ones (the primary qualities of [Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)).

### God and the Cartesian Circle

From one certainty Descartes extracts a general rule:

> I concluded that I might take, as a general rule, the principle, that all the things which we very clearly and distinctly conceive are true
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part IV (tr. John Veitch)

But the demon could still corrupt even clear and distinct perceptions, unless there is a God who is no deceiver. So the Third Meditation offers a **causal** proof (the "trademark argument"): I have an idea of an infinite, perfect being; the cause of an idea must have at least as much reality as the idea represents; I, a finite doubter, could not be the cause; so God exists and placed the idea in me like a craftsman's mark on his work. The Fifth Meditation adds a version of Anselm's ontological argument ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)): existence belongs to the essence of a perfect being as having three angles belongs to a triangle.

Antoine Arnauld spotted the problem in the Fourth Objections, and it has been called the **Cartesian Circle** ever since:

1. I can be sure that whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive is true only if I know that God exists and is no deceiver.
2. I can know that God exists only by clearly and distinctly perceiving the premises of the proof.

So the proof of God relies on the rule it is meant to guarantee. Descartes's reply was that the divine guarantee is needed only for *remembered* conclusions, not for what I perceive clearly at this moment. Harry Frankfurt (*Demons, Dreamers, and Madmen*, 1970) argued that Descartes aimed at coherence, showing that reason does not undermine itself, rather than truth in an absolute sense. Most readers conclude that the circle cannot be fully escaped: you cannot use reason to certify reason without assuming it.

### Mind and body

The Sixth Meditation argues for the **real distinction** of mind and body:

> And first because I know that whatever I clearly and distinctly perceive, may be so made by God as I perceive them; the Power of understanding clearly and distinctly one Thing without the other is sufficient to make Me certain that One Thing is different from the Other
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* VI (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

Laid out:

1. I can clearly and distinctly conceive of my mind existing without my body.
2. Whatever I can clearly and distinctly conceive, God can bring about.
3. So my mind can exist without my body.
4. If A can exist without B, A is not identical to B.
5. So my mind is not identical to my body.

This is **substance dualism**: reality contains two kinds of created substance, mind (*res cogitans*, thinking thing), whose essence is thought, and body (*res extensa*, extended thing), whose essence is extension. The weak point is premise 1, or the step from conceivability to possibility. Antoine Arnauld noted that someone might clearly conceive a right triangle without knowing that the square on the hypotenuse equals the squares on the other sides; it does not follow that the triangle could lack the property. Perhaps you can conceive your mind without your body only because you do not know enough about either. The same move from conceivability to possibility drives today's zombie arguments ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)).

Descartes insists that the union is intimate:

> My Nature tells me that I am not in my Body, as a Mariner is in his Ship, but that I am most nighly conjoyn'd thereto, and as it were Blended therewith; so that I with It make up one thing
>
> — René Descartes, *Meditations* VI (tr. William Molyneux, 1680)

If I were only a pilot, I would perceive damage to my body as a sailor sees a broken mast, without pain. But how can an unextended mind and an extended body interact? In May 1643, Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia (1618–1680), then twenty-four and living in exile in The Hague, wrote to Descartes asking exactly this. On the mechanics of the day, one body moves another by contact and pushing, which requires extension and surface; how then can a soul that has neither determine the body's movements? Descartes replied that the union of mind and body is a "primitive notion," understood through ordinary life and the senses rather than through metaphysics. Elisabeth replied that she found it easier to grant the soul matter and extension than to grant that an immaterial thing could move a body. Their correspondence is one of the best philosophical exchanges of the century; scholars such as Lisa Shapiro and Eileen O'Neill have restored Elisabeth to the canon. Descartes's own physiological proposal was that the mind acts on the body at the pineal gland, a small organ deep in the brain, which only relocates the mystery. This is the **interaction problem**, and it is the main reason few philosophers today are substance dualists.

Descartes also asked how we could tell a person from a perfectly made automaton. His answer anticipates the Turing test:

> Of these the first is that they could never use words or other signs arranged in such a manner as is competent to us in order to declare our thoughts to others
>
> — René Descartes, *Discourse on the Method*, Part V (tr. John Veitch)

The second test is flexibility: "reason is an universal instrument that is alike available on every occasion," whereas machines need a special arrangement for each task. Whether large language models pass the first test, and whether they fail the second, is a question Descartes would have enjoyed.

## Spinoza: God or Nature

Baruch (Bento, later Benedict) de Spinoza (1632–1677) was born into the Portuguese Jewish community of Amsterdam, descendants of families forced to convert in Iberia. In 1656, at twenty-three, he was placed under a *cherem*, a writ of expulsion, of unusual severity; the document cites his "abominable heresies" without saying what they were. He never joined another religious community. He supported himself partly by grinding lenses, published his *Theological-Political Treatise* (1670) anonymously, a pioneering work of biblical criticism and a defense of freedom of thought, declined a professorship at Heidelberg to protect his independence, and died at forty-four, probably of a lung disease worsened by glass dust. The *Ethics* appeared posthumously.

He explains in *On the Improvement of the Understanding* why he became a philosopher:

> After experience had taught me that all the usual surroundings of social life are vain and futile; seeing that none of the objects of my fears contained in themselves anything either good or bad, except in so far as the mind is affected by them, I finally resolved to inquire whether there might be some real good having power to communicate itself
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *On the Improvement of the Understanding* §1 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

The *Ethics*, despite its title, is mostly metaphysics, set out "in geometrical order" (*ordine geometrico demonstrata*) like Euclid: definitions, axioms, propositions, proofs. The form is not decoration. Spinoza holds that everything follows necessarily from the nature of God, as the properties of a triangle follow from its definition, so the right way to present reality is as a deduction.

### One substance

Definition 3 is the key:

> By substance, I mean that which is in itself, and is conceived through itself: in other words, that of which a conception can be formed independently of any other conception.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* I, Def. 3 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

Take that definition seriously and Descartes's created substances are not substances at all, since minds and bodies depend on God. Spinoza argues that two substances cannot share an attribute, that substance is infinite and necessarily exists, and that God, defined as a being with infinitely many attributes, is the only possible substance:

> Besides God no substance can be granted or conceived.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* I, Prop. 14 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

> Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or be conceived.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* I, Prop. 15 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

You, your cat and this book are **modes** of God: ways in which the one substance exists, as waves are ways the sea moves. Of God's infinitely many attributes we know two, thought and extension, and they are two ways of describing one reality:

> The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* II, Prop. 7 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

Your mind is the idea of your body; they are one thing conceived under two attributes. This **dual-aspect monism** dissolves Elisabeth's problem: mind and body do not interact, because they are not two things. Contemporary philosophers of mind who look for an alternative to both dualism and physicalism often cite Spinoza.

Spinoza writes of "God or Nature" (*Deus sive Natura*) as interchangeable terms. Was he a pantheist, identifying God with the world, or an atheist using pious words? Novalis called him "a God-intoxicated man"; his contemporaries called him an atheist. Edwin Curley reads him as identifying God with the basic laws of nature, not with the totality of things. The God of the *Ethics* certainly has no will, no plan and no love for us, and cannot be prayed to.

### No purposes, no free will

The Appendix to Part I is a sustained attack on teleology, the idea that nature acts for purposes. People are born ignorant of causes, conscious of their desires, and seek what is useful to them. From this:

> men think themselves free inasmuch as they are conscious of their volitions and desires, and never even dream, in their ignorance, of the causes which have disposed them so to wish and desire.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* I, Appendix (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

They then project their own purposes onto nature, imagining that God made the sun to light us and the animals to feed us, and when earthquakes and diseases strike, they say God is angry. Spinoza's verdict:

> There is no need to show at length, that nature has no particular goal in view, and that final causes are mere human figments.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* I, Appendix (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

This is a strict **necessitarianism**: not only is everything caused, but nothing could have been otherwise. In a letter (Letter 58) he imagines a stone, set in motion and conscious of its endeavor, believing it is moving of its own free will.

### Emotions as lines, planes and solids

If human beings are part of nature, emotions can be studied like anything else:

> I shall consider human actions and desires in exactly the same manner, as though I were concerned with lines, planes, and solids.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* III, Preface (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

The basic fact about every thing is its **conatus** (striving): "Everything, in so far as it is in itself, endeavours to persist in its own being" (III, Prop. 6). Joy is the passage to greater power of acting, sadness the passage to less, and every other emotion is built from these and desire. Love is joy accompanied by the idea of an external cause; hatred is sadness accompanied by the idea of an external cause.

Part IV diagnoses our condition:

> Human infirmity in moderating and checking the emotions I name bondage: for, when a man is a prey to his emotions, he is not his own master, but lies at the mercy of fortune
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* IV, Preface (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

And Part V offers the cure. Passions are confused ideas, caused by things outside us that we do not understand. Understanding them changes them:

> An emotion, which is a passion, ceases to be a passion, as soon as we form a clear and distinct idea thereof.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* V, Prop. 3 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

If I understand that the person who insulted me acted from causes that stretch back indefinitely, my hatred loses its object. The Stoics said something similar ([the Stoicism course, Chapter 7](../learn-stoicism/07-the-passions.md)), and modern cognitive therapy says something similar again. Freedom for Spinoza is not uncaused choice but acting from your own nature, understood. It culminates in seeing things "under a certain form of eternity" (*sub specie aeternitatis*) and in the "intellectual love of God." The free man looks forward:

> A free man thinks of death least of all things; and his wisdom is a meditation not of death but of life.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* IV, Prop. 67 (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

Compare Montaigne's "to study philosophy is to learn to die" ([Chapter 10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md)). The *Ethics* ends with a modest warning:

> But all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare.
>
> — Benedict de Spinoza, *Ethics* V, Prop. 42, Note (tr. R. H. M. Elwes)

## Leibniz: the best of all possible worlds

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz (1646–1716) may have been the last person to know everything. He invented the calculus independently of Newton (and spent his last years in a bitter priority dispute with him), designed a calculating machine that could multiply, studied binary arithmetic and the *I Ching*, worked on reuniting the Catholic and Protestant churches, wrote the history of the House of Brunswick as court librarian in Hanover, and wrote philosophy mostly in letters and short essays. He visited Spinoza in 1676 and spent much of his later work trying to avoid Spinoza's conclusions.

### Two great principles

Leibniz rests all reasoning on two principles:

> the other principle is that of the determinant reason: it states that nothing ever comes to pass without there being a cause or at least a reason determining it, that is, something to give an a priori reason why it is existent rather than non-existent, and in this wise rather than in any other.
>
> — G. W. Leibniz, *Theodicy* I, §44 (tr. E. M. Huggard)

The first is the **principle of contradiction**, which governs **truths of reason** (necessary truths, whose opposite is impossible, like those of mathematics). The second is the **principle of sufficient reason** (PSR), which governs **truths of fact** (contingent truths, like "Caesar crossed the Rubicon"). You met the PSR as the crux of Aquinas's First Way ([Chapter 9](09-faith-and-reason.md)). Hume will attack it ([Chapter 12](12-the-empiricists.md)), and Kant will build his philosophy on the distinction between the two kinds of truth ([Chapter 13](13-kant.md)).

From the PSR follows the **identity of indiscernibles**: no two things can be exactly alike, since God would have no reason to put one here and the other there. Leibniz liked to tell how a courtier in the gardens of Herrenhausen searched for two identical leaves to refute him, and failed.

### Monads

What are the ultimate constituents of reality? Matter is infinitely divisible, so it cannot be made of ultimate units. Leibniz concludes that reality consists of simple, unextended, soul-like substances: **monads** (Greek *monas*, "unit"). Each monad is a center of perception and appetite, mirroring the entire universe from its own point of view, mostly unconsciously. And no monad acts on any other. In the *Monadology* (1714), which he wrote in French, Leibniz puts it in a phrase:

> Les Monades n'ont point de fenêtres, par lesquelles quelque chose y puisse entrer ou sortir.
>
> — G. W. Leibniz, *Monadologie* §7 (French text, ed. Clodius Piat); "Monads have no windows through which anything could come in or go out"

Then why does my arm move when I decide to raise it? Because God, at creation, set every monad going so that their internal states correspond perfectly, like two clocks built so well that they always agree without any connection. This is the **pre-established harmony**, Leibniz's answer to the interaction problem.

Section 17 of the *Monadology* contains an argument that philosophers of mind still discuss, the **mill argument**. Suppose a machine could think. Imagine it enlarged so that you could walk inside it, as into a mill. You would find only parts pushing one another, and nothing that would explain a perception. So perception cannot be explained mechanically. It is an ancestor of the "explanatory gap" and the hard problem of consciousness ([Chapter 20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md)).

### The best of all possible worlds

God, being perfectly good, wise and powerful, surveyed infinitely many possible worlds and chose the best:

> so it may be said likewise in respect of perfect wisdom, which is no less orderly than mathematics, that if there were not the best (optimum) among all possible worlds, God would not have produced any.
>
> — G. W. Leibniz, *Theodicy* I, §8 (tr. E. M. Huggard)

The *Theodicy* (1710; the word means "justification of God") is Leibniz's answer to the problem of evil, written against Pierre Bayle's skeptical arguments. Evil exists, but a world with evil can be better overall than any world without it, as a painting needs shadows and a melody dissonance. "Best" does not mean "pleasantest"; it means richest in variety combined with the greatest simplicity of laws.

Voltaire was not persuaded. After an earthquake destroyed Lisbon on All Saints' Day, 1755, killing tens of thousands, he wrote *Candide* (1759), in which Dr. Pangloss, a parody of Leibniz, insists through shipwreck, earthquake, syphilis and the Inquisition that all is for the best. The satire is funny and unfair. Leibniz never claimed that every event is good, only that no better *whole* was possible. But it raises the real question: is the claim that this is the best possible world testable by anything that happens? If not, does it tell us anything?

## Pascal: the reasons of the heart

Blaise Pascal (1623–1662), mathematician (Pascal's triangle, probability theory with Fermat), physicist (experiments on the vacuum), and inventor of a calculating machine, underwent a religious conversion on the night of November 23, 1654, which he recorded on a parchment sewn into his coat and found after his death. His *Pensées* ("thoughts") are notes for an unfinished defense of Christianity. Pascal accepted Descartes's science but rejected his God, a first cause reached by proof, which Pascal contrasted with the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and he rejected Descartes's confidence in reason.

His picture of the human condition is the opposite of the rationalists' serene systems:

> The eternal silence of these infinite spaces frightens me.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* 206 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

> Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking reed. The entire universe need not arm itself to crush him. A vapour, a drop of water suffices to kill him. But, if the universe were to crush him, man would still be more noble than that which killed him, because he knows that he dies
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* 347 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

The most famous argument in the *Pensées* is the **wager** (fragment 233). Reason cannot decide whether God exists. But you cannot abstain: "you must wager. It is not optional." So compare outcomes:

> Let us weigh the gain and the loss in wagering that God is. Let us estimate these two chances. If you gain, you gain all; if you lose, you lose nothing. Wager, then, without hesitation that He is.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* 233 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

In decision-theoretic terms: if believing has an infinite expected payoff given any nonzero probability that God exists, and not believing has at best a finite one, belief maximizes expected value. It is the first use of expected utility reasoning in philosophy. The standard objections: the **many gods** problem (which God should you bet on, given that some gods might punish belief in the others?); whether belief can be chosen at all (Pascal answers that you can act as if you believed, "taking the holy water," until belief follows); and whether a God would reward belief adopted as a bet. William James returns to the wager in "The Will to Believe" ([Chapter 17](17-pragmatism.md)).

Pascal's deepest point is not the wager but the limit of argument:

> The heart has its reasons, which reason does not know.
>
> — Blaise Pascal, *Pensées* 277 (tr. W. F. Trotter)

By "heart" he means not sentiment but an intuitive grasp of first principles, such as that space has three dimensions, which reason uses but cannot prove. On this reading Pascal is not an irrationalist, but a critic of the rationalist hope that reason can ground itself.

## Scholarly debates worth knowing

**Descartes.** Is the cogito an inference, an intuition or a performance (Hintikka)? Is the Cartesian Circle vicious (Arnauld) or avoidable (the memory reading; Frankfurt's coherence reading)? Is Descartes a "real" dualist, or does his talk of the union as a "third primitive notion" point toward a trialism (John Cottingham)? Gilbert Ryle's *The Concept of Mind* (1949) mocked Cartesian dualism as "the dogma of the Ghost in the Machine," and the phrase stuck.

**Spinoza.** Was he a rationalist in the strongest sense, committed to the PSR without exception (Michael Della Rocca), or a naturalist close to modern science (Curley, Jonathan Bennett)? Is his God the world, its laws, or something beyond both? Was his politics democratic (Étienne Balibar, Jonathan Israel's "radical Enlightenment" thesis)?

**Leibniz.** Was he a thoroughgoing idealist all along, or did he hold, in his middle years, a theory of corporeal substances (Daniel Garber, *Leibniz: Body, Substance, Monad*, 2009)? Can his doctrine that every true predicate is contained in the concept of its subject avoid making every truth necessary, as Antoine Arnauld charged in their correspondence?

**The canon.** Elisabeth, Anne Conway (whose *Principles of the Most Ancient and Modern Philosophy* influenced Leibniz's term "monad"), Margaret Cavendish and Damaris Masham are now taught alongside the "big three"; see Eileen O'Neill's "Disappearing Ink" (1998) on why they vanished from the histories.

## Living it

**Do one Meditation.** Pick a belief that matters to you (that your partner loves you, that your work is useful, that the news you read is accurate). Apply Descartes's three waves: Could I be mistaken in the way the senses mislead? Could I have the same experience if it were false (the dream)? Is there a systematic source of error I cannot detect from inside (the demon)? Then do what Descartes did: find what survives and see what you can rebuild from it. Notice that the method only works if you are willing to suspend the belief for a while.

**Spinoza's exercise for a passion.** The next time you are angry with someone, write down, as precisely as you can, the causes of their action: their history, pressures, beliefs, the circumstances. Then write down the causes of your anger. Spinoza predicts that the passion will lose force as your idea of it becomes clearer and more complete. Test the prediction. If it fails, ask whether you understood the causes or only listed them.

**Wager carefully.** Identify a decision you are avoiding because the evidence is unclear. Pascal's point is that not deciding is also a decision. Write out the outcomes and their stakes. Then apply the many-gods objection to your own case: are there options you have not considered that would change the calculation?

## Summary

- Rationalism (Descartes, Spinoza, Leibniz) holds that reason is the primary source of knowledge; the labels are later and the contrast with empiricism is one of emphasis.
- Descartes: method; provisional (Stoic) morality; hyperbolic doubt (senses, dreams, the evil spirit); "I am, I exist"; the thinking thing; the wax; clear and distinct ideas; God as guarantor; the Cartesian Circle (Arnauld); the real distinction of mind and body; the interaction problem (Elisabeth); the language and flexibility tests for machines.
- Spinoza: one substance, God or Nature; minds and bodies as modes under two attributes (dual-aspect monism); no final causes, no free will; emotions studied geometrically; *conatus*; bondage as the rule of passions; freedom through understanding; the free man meditates on life.
- Leibniz: the principles of contradiction and sufficient reason; truths of reason and truths of fact; identity of indiscernibles; windowless monads; pre-established harmony; the mill argument; the best of all possible worlds and Voltaire's satire.
- Pascal: the thinking reed; the terror of infinite space; the wager as the first expected-utility argument; the heart's knowledge of first principles.

### Dialogue 1 — On whether your mind could exist without your body

**Princess Elisabeth:** You have read Monsieur Descartes and say you are persuaded that your mind is a thing distinct from your body. Tell me why.

**You:** Because I can imagine my mind existing without my body: waking up as a disembodied consciousness, or with a different body. If I can conceive of them apart, they must be two things.

*Your turn: Elisabeth will now ask how your mind moves your body. Before she does, write three or four sentences on how you think a decision to raise your arm produces the movement.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** I decide to raise my arm, and signals go from my brain down the nerves to the muscles.

**Princess Elisabeth:** The signals in your brain: are they bodily things, with location and size?

**You:** Yes. Electrochemical events in neurons.

**Princess Elisabeth:** Then what does your *mind*, which you say is not extended, contribute? Either it starts those electrochemical events or it does not. If it does, it must push something, and I cannot understand how a thing with no size or surface pushes anything.

**You:** Maybe it does not push. Maybe it just causes, in some way different from physical contact.

**Princess Elisabeth:** That is what Monsieur Descartes told me: that the union is a "primitive notion," known by experience rather than understanding. I confess I find it easier to give the soul matter and extension than to give an immaterial thing the power to move a body. Would you not?

**You:** But if the mind is material, how do I explain that I could conceive of it existing without the body?

**Princess Elisabeth:** Must everything you can conceive be possible? Could someone conceive water existing without hydrogen, if he did not know chemistry?

**You:** Yes. People did, for centuries. And water is hydrogen and oxygen nonetheless.

**Princess Elisabeth:** Then your conceiving that your mind exists apart from your body may show only that you do not know what your mind is.

**You:** That is Arnauld's triangle objection. But there is a difference. With water, we discovered what was underneath the appearance. With the mind, the appearance is the thing: pain just is how it feels. There is nothing hidden behind it to discover.

**Princess Elisabeth:** That is a much better argument than the one you started with. You are no longer saying "I can imagine being disembodied." You are saying that conscious experience is known from the inside in a way that leaves no room for a hidden nature. Am I right?

**You:** Yes. And I admit that does not prove dualism. It might show only that we have two ways of knowing one thing, as Spinoza said.

**Princess Elisabeth:** Then here is where we stand. The argument from conceivability is weak, because ignorance can make impossible things seem possible. The interaction problem is serious for anyone who makes mind and body two substances. What remains is a puzzle about consciousness that neither Monsieur Descartes nor I have solved.

**You:** And which, I gather, philosophers still argue about under the name of the hard problem.

**Princess Elisabeth:** I am pleased to hear that I posed a durable question.

### Dialogue 2 — On whether you are free

**Baruch Spinoza:** Why did you choose the coffee this morning, rather than tea?

**You:** Because I felt like it. I could have chosen tea.

*Your turn: Spinoza will argue that your feeling that you could have chosen otherwise is an illusion. What would count as evidence either way? Write three or four sentences.*

<details><summary>Continue the dialogue</summary>

**You:** When I choose, I am aware of no cause compelling me. I deliberate and decide.

**Baruch Spinoza:** You are aware of your desire. Are you aware of its causes: the chemistry of your body, your habits, the smell from the kitchen, what you drank yesterday?

**You:** Not all of them. But not being aware of causes is not the same as their determining me.

**Baruch Spinoza:** Correct. My argument is not that you are unaware of causes, therefore there are causes. It is that everything in nature follows from prior causes, and you are part of nature. Your ignorance only explains why you *feel* free. A stone, if it were conscious of its motion, would think it moved by its own will.

**You:** But that makes me a puppet. If I am not free, why should I try to be better? Your book is called *Ethics*.

**Baruch Spinoza:** Because there is a difference between a man pushed about by passions he does not understand, and a man who acts from understanding. The first I call in bondage. The second is free: not uncaused, but caused by his own nature and his clear ideas.

**You:** So freedom is a kind of causation, not the absence of it.

**Baruch Spinoza:** Yes. When you understand why you crave coffee each morning, the craving changes its character. It is no longer something that happens to you; it is something you comprehend.

**You:** I am not sure understanding the craving makes it go away. I understand my addiction to my phone quite well, and I still check it.

**Baruch Spinoza:** Do you understand it adequately, or only in words? An emotion can only be checked by a stronger emotion. Knowledge helps when it becomes a stronger affect: when you feel, and do not merely know, what the habit costs you.

**You:** That is more realistic than I expected. But it still seems unfair to blame or praise anyone, if everything is necessary.

**Baruch Spinoza:** I do not blame. I try to understand. Should we not stop hating people for what follows necessarily from their nature, as we do not hate the storm? That is not indifference. It is what makes forgiveness possible.

**You:** Then here is my position. I am not convinced that everything is necessary; quantum physics, for one, suggests some events have no determining cause. But you have shown me that the freedom worth wanting may not be the freedom to have done otherwise, but the freedom of acting from understanding rather than compulsion. And that kind of freedom comes in degrees.

**Baruch Spinoza:** Then you have the conclusion of my Fifth Part, if not my First. As I said, all things excellent are as difficult as they are rare.

</details>
