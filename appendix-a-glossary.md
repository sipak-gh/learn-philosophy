# Appendix A — Glossary

Key terms of the course in alphabetical order, with the original-language term where one matters and the chapter where the term is treated most fully. Use it to check a word you have forgotten, or as a review list: if you can explain each entry in two sentences and say who would disagree, you have the course's vocabulary. Previous: [Chapter 24 — Building a Philosophy of Life](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md). Next: [Appendix B — Timeline](appendix-b-timeline.md).

| Term | Original | Meaning | Chapter |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A priori / a posteriori** | Latin, "from what is prior" / "from what is posterior" | Knowable independently of experience (by reason alone) / knowable only through experience. Kant asked how synthetic a priori knowledge is possible. | [5](05-plato.md), [13](13-kant.md) |
| **Abduction** | — | Peirce's name for inference to a hypothesis that would explain a surprising fact; now usually called inference to the best explanation. | [17](17-pragmatism.md), [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Absurd** | French *l'absurde* | In Camus, the confrontation between the human demand for meaning and the silent indifference of the world; to be lived with in revolt rather than escaped by suicide or a leap of faith. | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Akrasia** | Greek *akrasia*, "lack of mastery" | Weakness of will: knowingly acting against one's better judgment. Socrates denied it was possible: no one does wrong knowingly. | [4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) |
| **Alienation** | German *Entfremdung* | In the young Marx, the estrangement of workers from the product and activity of their labor, from other people and from their "species-being" under capitalism. | [14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md) |
| **Analytic / synthetic** | — | A statement true by virtue of meaning alone ("all bachelors are unmarried") / one whose truth depends on how the world is. Kant drew the distinction; Quine argued it cannot be drawn sharply. | [13](13-kant.md), [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Anattā** | Pali; Sanskrit *anātman* | "Not-self": the Buddhist doctrine that there is no permanent, unchanging self behind the changing stream of mental and physical events. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md), [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Apeiron** | Greek, "the boundless" | Anaximander's name for the indefinite, unlimited source from which all things arise and into which they return. | [3](03-the-presocratics.md) |
| **Aporia** | Greek, "no way through" | The state of puzzlement in which a Socratic dialogue typically ends, when every proposed definition has failed. | [4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) |
| **Archē** | Greek, "beginning, principle" | The first principle or basic stuff of things, which the Presocratics sought (water for Thales, the boundless for Anaximander, air for Anaximenes). | [3](03-the-presocratics.md) |
| **Ataraxia** | Greek, "freedom from disturbance" | Tranquillity: the goal of the Epicureans and the Pyrrhonian Skeptics. | [7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md) |
| **Authenticity** | German *Eigentlichkeit*; French *authenticité* | In Heidegger and Sartre, owning one's existence and choices instead of losing oneself in "the They" or in bad faith. It concerns *how* one chooses, not *what*. | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Bad faith** | French *mauvaise foi* | In Sartre, self-deception about one's own freedom: pretending to be a thing determined by role, nature or circumstance. | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Bayesianism** | — | The view that degrees of belief (credences) should obey the probability calculus and be updated on evidence by conditionalization, following Bayes' theorem. | [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Being-in-the-world** | German *In-der-Welt-sein* | Heidegger's term for the basic structure of human existence (*Dasein*): always already engaged with things and others in a meaningful world, not a detached subject facing objects. | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Biopower** | French *biopouvoir* | In Foucault, modern power that manages the life of populations (health, birth rates, sexuality) rather than only threatening death. | [23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md) |
| **Bundle theory** | — | Hume's view that the self is "nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions" with no underlying substance. | [12](12-the-empiricists.md), [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Categorical imperative** | German *kategorischer Imperativ* | In Kant, the unconditional moral command, binding whatever one's desires: act only on a maxim you could will as a universal law; treat humanity always as an end, never merely as a means. | [13](13-kant.md) |
| **Categories** | Greek *katēgoriai*; German *Kategorien* | For Aristotle, the most general kinds of being (substance, quantity, quality, relation and others); for Kant, the pure concepts of the understanding (such as cause and substance) that structure all possible experience. | [6](06-aristotle.md), [13](13-kant.md) |
| **Cogito** | Latin, "I think" | Descartes' first certainty, *cogito, ergo sum* ("I think, therefore I am"): even in doubting, the doubter must exist. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Communicative action** | German *kommunikatives Handeln* | In Habermas, action aimed at reaching mutual understanding through reasons, as opposed to strategic action aimed at success. | [23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md) |
| **Compatibilism** | — | The view that free will and moral responsibility are compatible with determinism. | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md), [24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md) |
| **Conatus** | Latin, "striving" | In Spinoza, the effort by which each thing strives to persevere in its being; in humans, it is the core of desire. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Correspondence theory of truth** | — | The view that a belief or statement is true when it corresponds to a fact. The pragmatists challenged it. | [17](17-pragmatism.md) |
| **Deconstruction** | French *déconstruction* | Derrida's method of reading that exposes how a text's hierarchical oppositions (speech/writing, presence/absence) undermine themselves. | [23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md) |
| **Deduction / induction** | — | Reasoning in which the truth of the premises guarantees the conclusion / reasoning in which they make it probable. | [2](02-logic-and-argument.md) |
| **Dependent origination** | Pali *paṭicca-samuppāda* | The Buddhist doctrine that everything arises in dependence on conditions and nothing exists on its own. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Dialectic** | Greek *dialektikē*; German *Dialektik* | For Socrates and Plato, philosophical reasoning by question and answer; for Hegel, the movement in which a position generates its opposite and both are overcome (*aufgehoben*) in a richer whole; for Marx, the same movement in material and social history. | [5](05-plato.md), [14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md) |
| **Difference principle** | — | Rawls's principle that social and economic inequalities are just only if they work to the greatest benefit of the least advantaged. | [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Double consciousness** | — | Du Bois's term for the experience of Black Americans of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others." | [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Dualism (substance)** | — | The view that mind and body are distinct kinds of substance, one thinking and unextended, the other extended and unthinking. Descartes is its classic defender. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md), [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Elenchus** | Greek *elenchos*, "refutation, test" | The Socratic method of cross-examination: draw out an interlocutor's belief, then show that it conflicts with other things they believe. | [2](02-logic-and-argument.md), [4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) |
| **Empiricism** | Greek *empeiria*, "experience" | The view that all ideas and knowledge come from experience. Locke, Berkeley and Hume are the classic British empiricists. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md), [12](12-the-empiricists.md) |
| **Emptiness** | Sanskrit *śūnyatā* | In Nāgārjuna, the lack of intrinsic nature (*svabhāva*) in all things, which is just their dependent origination. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Epiphenomenalism** | — | The view that mental states are caused by physical states but have no effects of their own, like a steam whistle on a locomotive (Huxley's image). | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Epistemology** | Greek *epistēmē*, "knowledge" | The theory of knowledge: what knowledge is, how it is possible and how far it extends. | [1](01-what-is-philosophy.md), [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Epochē** | Greek, "suspension" | Suspension of judgment: for the Pyrrhonian Skeptics, the route to tranquillity; for Husserl, "bracketing" the natural attitude in order to describe experience as it is given. | [7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md), [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Eternal recurrence** | German *ewige Wiederkunft* | Nietzsche's thought experiment: could you will to live your life again, exactly the same, an infinite number of times? | [15](15-nietzsche.md) |
| **Eudaimonia** | Greek, "having a good guardian spirit" | Happiness or flourishing: for Aristotle, the highest human good, activity of the soul in accordance with virtue over a complete life. | [6](06-aristotle.md) |
| **Existence precedes essence** | French *l'existence précède l'essence* | Sartre's slogan for existentialism: human beings exist first and then define themselves by their choices; there is no given human nature. | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Externalism (about content)** | — | The view that the content of some thoughts depends on the environment, not only on what is in the head (Putnam's Twin Earth). | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Fallibilism** | — | The view that any of our beliefs, however well supported, could turn out to be false. Peirce made it central to pragmatism. | [3](03-the-presocratics.md), [17](17-pragmatism.md) |
| **Falsafa** | Arabic, from Greek *philosophia* | Philosophy in the Greek tradition as practiced in the Islamic world (al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes), as distinct from *kalām* (dialectical theology). | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md) |
| **Falsifiability** | — | Popper's criterion for a scientific theory: it must forbid some possible observations, so that experience could refute it. | [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Family resemblance** | German *Familienähnlichkeit* | Later Wittgenstein's idea that the things a word applies to (such as "games") may share no single common feature, only overlapping similarities. | [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Fideism** | Latin *fides*, "faith" | The view that religious belief rests on faith rather than reason, or need not answer to reason. | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md), [10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md) |
| **Forms** | Greek *eidos*, *idea* | In Plato, the eternal, unchanging, perfect realities (the Just itself, the Beautiful itself) that sensible things imperfectly "participate in" and that only reason can know. | [5](05-plato.md) |
| **Four causes** | Greek *aitiai* | Aristotle's four kinds of explanation: material (what a thing is made of), formal (its form or structure), efficient (what produced it) and final (what it is for). | [6](06-aristotle.md) |
| **Four noble truths** | Pali *cattāri ariyasaccāni* | The Buddha's diagnosis: life involves suffering (*dukkha*); suffering arises from craving; it can cease; the Eightfold Path leads to its cessation. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Functionalism** | — | The view that mental states are defined by their causal roles (inputs, outputs and relations to other states), not by what they are made of, so the same mind could be realized in different materials. | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Genealogy** | German *Genealogie*; French *généalogie* | A historical inquiry into how values or practices came to be, used to unsettle their claim to be natural or timeless (Nietzsche, Foucault). | [15](15-nietzsche.md), [23](23-continental-philosophy-today.md) |
| **General will** | French *volonté générale* | In Rousseau, the will of the people as a body aimed at the common good, distinct from the "will of all," a sum of private wills. | [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Gettier case** | — | A case of justified true belief that is not knowledge because the belief is true only by luck. Gettier's 1963 paper refuted the justified-true-belief analysis. | [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Hard problem of consciousness** | — | Chalmers's name for the problem of explaining why physical processes give rise to subjective experience at all, as opposed to the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions. | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Harm principle** | — | Mill's principle that the only legitimate ground for interfering with someone's liberty against their will is to prevent harm to others. | [16](16-utilitarianism-and-liberalism.md), [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Historical materialism** | — | Marx's view that the economic base of a society (its forces and relations of production) shapes its legal, political and ideological superstructure. | [14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md) |
| **Humanism (Renaissance)** | Latin *studia humanitatis* | The Renaissance movement to recover classical texts "back to the sources" (*ad fontes*) and to center education on grammar, rhetoric, history, poetry and moral philosophy. | [10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md) |
| **Hylomorphism** | Greek *hylē* (matter) + *morphē* (form) | Aristotle's view that every physical substance is a compound of matter and form; the soul is the form of a living body. | [6](06-aristotle.md) |
| **Idealism** | — | The view that reality is fundamentally mental or mind-dependent: Berkeley's "to be is to be perceived," Kant's transcendental idealism, Hegel's absolute idealism. | [12](12-the-empiricists.md), [13](13-kant.md), [14](14-hegel-marx-and-their-critics.md) |
| **Idols** | Latin *idola* | Bacon's four sources of error that beset the mind: the idols of the Tribe, the Cave, the Marketplace and the Theatre. | [10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md) |
| **Incommensurability** | — | Kuhn's and Feyerabend's claim that rival scientific paradigms may lack a common measure, so that theory choice is not settled by neutral rules alone. | [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Intentionality** | German *Intentionalität* | The "aboutness" of mental states: every act of consciousness is consciousness *of* something (Brentano, Husserl). | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md), [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Is–ought gap** | — | Hume's observation that no conclusion about what ought to be follows from premises solely about what is. | [12](12-the-empiricists.md) |
| **Justified true belief** | — | The traditional analysis of knowledge, traced to Plato's *Theaetetus* and *Meno*, refuted by Gettier. | [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Kalām** | Arabic, "speech" | Islamic dialectical theology, which defended doctrine by argument; contrasted with *falsafa*. | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md) |
| **Karma** | Sanskrit, "action" | The principle that actions have consequences that shape future experience and rebirth. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Language-game** | German *Sprachspiel* | Later Wittgenstein's term for a practice of using words embedded in a form of life; meaning is use within such practices. | [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Li** | Chinese 禮 | Ritual or propriety: in Confucius, the forms of right conduct, from ceremony to courtesy, through which humaneness (*ren*) is expressed and cultivated. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Liberty, negative / positive** | — | Isaiah Berlin's distinction between freedom from interference by others and freedom as self-mastery or self-government. | [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Logical positivism** | — | The doctrine of the Vienna Circle that a statement is meaningful only if it is either analytic or empirically verifiable, so that metaphysics is literally meaningless. | [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Logos** | Greek, "word, reason, account" | For Heraclitus, the rational order that governs all change; for the Stoics, the divine reason pervading the cosmos. | [3](03-the-presocratics.md), [7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md) |
| **Master and slave morality** | German *Herren-* and *Sklaven-Moral* | Nietzsche's contrast between values that affirm the strong (good/bad) and values born of the *ressentiment* of the weak (good/evil). | [15](15-nietzsche.md) |
| **Metaethics** | — | The study of the nature of moral claims: whether they can be true, what makes them true and how we could know them. | [1](01-what-is-philosophy.md), [24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md) |
| **Metaphysics** | Greek *ta meta ta phusika*, "the things after the physics" | The study of the most general features of reality: being, substance, causation, time, mind and freedom. The name comes from the editorial placement of Aristotle's treatise. | [1](01-what-is-philosophy.md), [6](06-aristotle.md) |
| **Mokṣa** | Sanskrit, "release" | Liberation from the cycle of rebirth (*saṃsāra*), the highest goal in most Indian traditions. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Monad** | Greek *monas*, "unit" | In Leibniz, a simple, indivisible, mind-like substance; the universe consists of monads coordinated by pre-established harmony. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Naturalistic fallacy** | — | Moore's name for the mistake of identifying goodness with a natural property, such as pleasure; supported by his open question argument. | [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Nihilism** | Latin *nihil*, "nothing" | The view or condition in which the highest values devalue themselves and life seems to lack meaning or value. Nietzsche diagnosed it; the [nihilism course](../learn-nihilism/README.md) covers it in depth. | [15](15-nietzsche.md) |
| **Nominalism** | Latin *nomen*, "name" | The view that only particulars exist and universals are mere names or concepts (strictly, in Ockham's version, conceptualism). | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md) |
| **Nomos / physis** | Greek, "law, custom" / "nature" | The sophists' distinction between what holds by human convention and what holds by nature. | [4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) |
| **Ockham's razor** | — | The principle of parsimony: do not multiply entities beyond necessity. The famous Latin formula is not in Ockham's writings (it seems to come from the seventeenth-century editor John Punch), but Ockham wrote that "plurality must never be posited without necessity" and used the principle relentlessly. | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md) |
| **Ontological argument** | — | An a priori argument that God exists, from the concept of God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived (Anselm) or as a supremely perfect being (Descartes). | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md), [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Original position** | — | Rawls's thought experiment: principles of justice are those that free and equal people would choose behind a "veil of ignorance" about their own place in society. | [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Paradigm** | Greek *paradeigma*, "pattern" | In Kuhn, the shared framework of theory, exemplars and methods that defines "normal science" in a field until a revolution replaces it. | [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Perspectivism** | — | Nietzsche's view that all knowing is from some perspective, and that more perspectives give a fuller view rather than none being true. | [15](15-nietzsche.md) |
| **Phenomenology** | Greek *phainomenon* + *logos* | The study of the structures of experience as it is lived, from the first-person point of view (Husserl, Heidegger, Merleau-Ponty). | [19](19-phenomenology-and-existentialism.md) |
| **Philosopher-kings** | Greek *philosophoi basileis* | In Plato's *Republic*, the rulers of the ideal city, who must be philosophers because only they know the Good. | [5](05-plato.md) |
| **Physicalism** | — | The view that everything that exists is physical or is fixed by the physical facts. | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Pragmatic maxim** | — | Peirce's rule for clarifying ideas: the meaning of a concept lies in the conceivable practical effects of its object. | [17](17-pragmatism.md) |
| **Pre-established harmony** | — | Leibniz's account of mind–body correlation: God set mind and body running in perfect agreement from the start, with no interaction between them. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md), [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Primary / secondary qualities** | — | Properties objects really have (shape, size, motion) / powers to cause sensations (color, taste, warmth) that do not resemble anything in the object. Galileo, Locke; challenged by Berkeley. | [3](03-the-presocratics.md), [10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md), [12](12-the-empiricists.md) |
| **Principle of charity** | — | The rule of interpreting an argument in its strongest reasonable form before criticizing it. | [1](01-what-is-philosophy.md), [2](02-logic-and-argument.md) |
| **Principle of sufficient reason** | — | Leibniz's principle that nothing is so without a reason why it is so and not otherwise. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Problem of induction** | — | Hume's problem: no non-circular argument shows that the future will resemble the past, so inductive inference seems to lack rational justification. | [2](02-logic-and-argument.md), [12](12-the-empiricists.md), [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Problem of universals** | — | The question whether general properties (redness, humanity) exist in their own right, in things or only in the mind. | [9](09-faith-and-reason.md) |
| **Qualia** | Latin, "of what kind" | The subjective, felt qualities of experience: what it is like to see red or taste coffee. | [20](20-philosophy-of-mind.md) |
| **Rationalism** | Latin *ratio*, "reason" | The view that reason, independently of experience, is a source of substantive knowledge. Descartes, Spinoza and Leibniz are the classic rationalists. | [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md) |
| **Recollection** | Greek *anamnēsis* | Plato's doctrine that learning is remembering what the soul knew before birth, illustrated in the *Meno* by the slave boy's geometry. | [5](05-plato.md) |
| **Reflective equilibrium** | — | Rawls's method of adjusting principles and considered judgments about cases against each other until they fit together. | [22](22-political-philosophy.md), [24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md) |
| **Relations of ideas / matters of fact** | — | Hume's fork: truths knowable by thought alone, whose denial is a contradiction / truths about the world, knowable only by experience and ultimately resting on cause and effect. | [12](12-the-empiricists.md) |
| **Relativism** | — | The view that truth, knowledge or value is relative to a person, culture or framework. Protagoras' "man is the measure" is its classic statement. | [4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) |
| **Ren** | Chinese 仁 | Humaneness, benevolence or "perfect virtue" (Legge): the central Confucian virtue of caring for others in the right way. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Ressentiment** | French, "resentment" | Nietzsche's term for the impotent hatred of the weak toward the strong, which he held to be the creative source of slave morality. | [15](15-nietzsche.md) |
| **Saṃsāra** | Sanskrit, "wandering" | The cycle of birth, death and rebirth driven by karma. | [8](08-classical-india-and-china.md) |
| **Sense and reference** | German *Sinn* and *Bedeutung* | Frege's distinction between the mode of presentation of an object and the object itself; "the morning star" and "the evening star" differ in sense but share a reference. | [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Skepticism** | Greek *skepsis*, "inquiry" | Doubt about whether knowledge is possible, in general or in some domain. Pyrrhonian skeptics suspended judgment; Descartes used doubt as a method; Hume's skepticism was "mitigated." | [7](07-hellenistic-philosophy.md), [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md), [12](12-the-empiricists.md), [21](21-knowledge-and-science.md) |
| **Social contract** | — | The idea that political authority rests on an agreement, actual or hypothetical, among those subject to it (Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, Rawls). | [10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md), [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Sophists** | Greek *sophistai* | Fifth-century BCE itinerant teachers of rhetoric and "virtue" for pay, such as Protagoras and Gorgias. | [4](04-socrates-and-the-sophists.md) |
| **Spiritual exercises** | French *exercices spirituels* | Hadot's term for the practices (meditation, self-examination, rehearsal of death) by which ancient philosophers sought to transform how they lived. | [1](01-what-is-philosophy.md), [24](24-building-a-philosophy-of-life.md) |
| **State of nature** | — | The condition of human beings without political authority. For Hobbes, a war of all against all; for Locke, a state governed by natural law but lacking an impartial judge. | [10](10-renaissance-and-the-new-science.md), [22](22-political-philosophy.md) |
| **Sublime** | German *das Erhabene* | In Kant, the feeling aroused by what is vast or overwhelming, in which the mind becomes aware of a power of reason beyond nature. | [13](13-kant.md) |
| **Substance** | Greek *ousia*; Latin *substantia* | What exists in its own right and bears properties. Aristotle's primary substances are individuals; Descartes recognized two created kinds; Spinoza only one, God or Nature. | [6](06-aristotle.md), [11](11-descartes-and-the-rationalists.md), [12](12-the-empiricists.md) |
| **Synthetic a priori** | — | Kant's category of judgments that extend knowledge but are known independently of experience, such as the truths of geometry and "every event has a cause." | [13](13-kant.md) |
| **Tabula rasa** | Latin, "scraped tablet" | Locke's image (he wrote "white paper") of the mind at birth, empty of innate ideas. | [12](12-the-empiricists.md) |
| **Theory of descriptions** | — | Russell's analysis of sentences like "The present King of France is bald" as claims that exactly one thing has a property, which removes the need for non-existent objects. | [18](18-the-analytic-turn.md) |
| **Thought experiment** | — | An imagined scenario used to test a concept or theory, such as the ring of Gyges, the evil demon, the Chinese room or the veil of ignorance. | [2](02-logic-and-argument.md) |
| **Transcendental idealism** | — | Kant's view that space, time and the categories are forms the mind imposes on experience, so that we know things as they appear, not as they are in themselves. | [13](13-kant.md) |
| **Übermensch** | German, "overman" | Nietzsche's figure, in *Thus Spoke Zarathustra*, of a human being who creates values after the death of God. | [15](15-nietzsche.md) |
